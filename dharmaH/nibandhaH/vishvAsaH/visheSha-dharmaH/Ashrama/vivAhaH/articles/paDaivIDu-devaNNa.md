There's an inscription from Paṭaivīṭu (c. 1426 AD) that's of interest not just because it attests (implicitly) to local brāhmaṇa-s practicing cousin-marriage, but also as a samayapattra that establishes it in-line with its legal defense by Devaṇṇa Bhaṭṭa (13th c. AD):
[TW](https://twitter.com/marthandavelan/status/2033747368735334596)


Note that as the Bṛhaspati Smṛti (1.6.15) here states, a Samayapattra is a legal document that formally establishes some convention for a specific subgroup provided they don't conflict with the ruling authority. The specific purpose of the inscription is to establish as law a prohibition on brāhmaṇa-s in Paṭaivīṭu from participating in non-kanyādāna marriages lest they be subjected to an unspecified punishment and loss of caste.

But why did they feel the need to do this in the first place? This is because the defense of cross-cousin marriage as propounded by Devaṇṇa Bhaṭṭa requires the practice of kanyādāna marriages (as he defines it) for it to be a valid cousin-marriage.

Devaṇṇa posits that the act of kanyādāna in the rites of a marital union (which he states belongs only to the brāhma, daiva, ārṣa, and prājāpatya unions) causes her natal relatives to cease to be her sapiṇḍa-s, which wouldn't break the taboo of marrying one's sapiṇḍa.

What makes this particular inscription interesting to have with us an example of an on-ground consequences and effects of juridical debates that otherwise only appears to us to stay in the folios even as that may not be the case.
