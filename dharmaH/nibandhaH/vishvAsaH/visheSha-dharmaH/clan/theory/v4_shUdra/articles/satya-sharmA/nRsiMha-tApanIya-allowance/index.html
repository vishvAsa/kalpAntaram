Introduction

The majority of traditional Hindu scholars believe that the mantras and upāsanās stated in Vedas produce the desired effects only for the dvijas or twice-borns, i.e., the Brāhmaṇa, Kṣatriya and Vaiśya males who have duly undergone the upanayana saṃskāra (a ritual after which the traditional study of Vedas begin). It is further believed that such practices do not produce the desired effects for the men who are beyond these three varṇas, and women of all varṇas. The author of the commentary on the Nṛsiṃhapūrvatāpanīya Upaniṣat referred to in this paper, is also the author of Prapañcasāra, an authoritative text on mantras and related rituals. In this commentary, the author states the adhikāra (ability to produce and experience the desired effect through rituals, etc.) of women and Śūdras for the upāsanā of Nṛsiṃha through the Nṛsiṃha-mantra. He views this upaniṣat as ‘śruti’, i.e., Veda. Scholars of Śaṅkarācārya’s tradition attribute both of these texts to Śaṅkarācārya. Some contemporary scholars dispute such attribution, while others favour it.

Even the disputers can clearly see the allegiance of the commentary to Śaṅkarācārya’s Advaita philosophy. Both these groups have to deal with the apparent contradiction with Śaṅkarācārya’s concluding statement in his commentary on the Apaśūdrādhikaraṇa of Brahmasūtras.

In part 3, I discuss and attempt to resolve the apparent contradiction between the commentary on this upaniṣat and Śaṅkarācārya’s commentary on Apaśūdrādhikaraṇa, along with some related issues.

Part 1: The upaniṣat, its tradition and the commentator

1a) Authority of Nṛsiṃhapūrvatāpanīya Upaniṣat

The Nṛsiṃhapūrvatāpanīya Upaniṣat (NṛPūTā) has been cited by scholars across various traditions, such as Madhvācārya1 (1197-1276 CE), Raṅga Rāmānuja2 (16th century CE), Mitra Miśra3 (17th century CE), and Lakṣmīdhara4 (15th century CE). Although it is not as popular as other upaniṣats, it nonetheless holds authority among the aforementioned and such other scholars.

1b) Authority of the commentator of NṛPūTā

The author of the commentary of NṛPūTā, which I have referred to in this paper, calls the text named Prapañcasāra (PrPaSā) his own work.5 This text, which is concerned with mantras, the related rituals, etc., has been referred to by many traditional scholars like Mādhavācārya6 (14th century CE), Narasiṃha Vājapeyin7 (16th century CE), Śivānanda Bhaṭṭa8 (17th century CE) and Śrīnivāsa Makhin9. This points to the general acceptance of the authority of PrPaSā, and indirectly of its author. Another work which the commentator mentions, is his commentary on the Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣat (MāUp).10

1c) Author of PrPaSā

For the followers of Śaṅkarācārya’s tradition, Śaṅkarācārya (788-820 CE) is the author of PrPaSā.11 Gīrvāṇendra Sarasvatī (between 16th and 17th centuries CE) attributes it to him.12 Mādhavācārya addresses the author as Ācārya.13 Amalānanda (1247-1347 CE) also does the same.14 T. K. Balasubrahmanya, a disciple of Śrī Śivābhinava Saccidānanda Nṛsiṃha Bhāratī Svāmin, the erstwhile head of the Śāradā Śṛṅgerī Maṭha (an institution connected with Śaṅkarācārya), got the works of Śaṅkarācārya published, which include PrPaSā and Nṛsiṃhapūrvatāpanīyopaniṣadbhāṣya (NṛPūTāBh).15

In an edition of Tattvaloka, a journal published under the aegis of the same Maṭha, it has been stated that the aforementioned head of the Maṭha personally approved the manuscripts of the texts for this publication.16 This commentator of NṛPūTā has also referred to his commentary on the MāUp. Portions of the NṛPūTāBh have a striking resemblance with Śaṅkarācārya’s commentary on the MāUp.17

1d) Author of NṛPūTāBh : Śaṅkarācārya or someone else?

There are two ways to ascertain the attribution of a traditional text to a traditional scholar. The first is the traditional way based on those persons who claim to follow the scholar. The other is the independent analytical way based on the study of the text and related works. As per the traditional way, Śaṅkarācārya is the author of NṛPūTāBh, whereas some of those who follow the independent analytical way dispute the said attribution.18 I suggest a very simple method to take a few steps toward ascertaining who the author may have been.

The author of NṛPūTāBh is also the author of PrPaSā. PrPaSā has been cited by scholars from within and outside of Śaṅkarācārya’s tradition. Mādhavācārya, who belongs to the tradition, cites PrPaSā and calls its author ‘Ācārya’.19 Amalānanda also calls him the same.20 Another such scholar, Gīrvāṇendra Sarasvatī attributes PrPaSā to Śaṅkarācārya. This establishes at least one fact: the author of PrPaSā is revered in Śaṅkarācārya’s tradition. This consequently makes NṛPūTāBh an authoritative text for it.

Even outside of the said tradition, the author of PrPaSā undoubtedly holds authority on the matters of mantras and the related rituals. This very fact grants enough authority to NṛPūTāBh for those who may doubt the attribution of PrPaSā and NṛPūTāBh to Śaṅkarācārya. Settling the debate regarding the said attribution is beyond the scope of this paper. Hence, in the following sections of this paper, I shall refer to the author of NṛPūTāBh as Bhāṣyakāra (author of commentary).

1e) NṛPūTā and its tradition

NṛPūTā, which is connected with Atharvaveda, teaches about the upāsanā (guided meditation/worship) of Nṛsiṃha as sākāra (with form) Brahman. The central instrument of this upāsanā is the Nṛsiṃha-mantra, also called the mantrarāja (chief among mantras), which is in the anuṣṭup-meter.21 This mantra holds special significance in the kaliyuga.22 Based on NṛPūTāBh it appears that there were other commentaries and interpretations of NṛPūTā.23 Bhāṣyakāra further tells about two recensions of the same, one connected with Bhṛguśākhā and another connected with Āṅgirasaśākhā.24 He also mentions some related textual variants.25

Part 2: An overview of Bhāṣyakāra’s commentary on NṛPūTā

2a) NṛPūTā as per Bhāṣyakāra

Bhāṣyakāra does not call the upaniṣat as NṛPūTā. He calls it ’tāpanīyopaniṣat’, which begins with ‘āpo vā idam āsan’ and ends with ’tad etan niṣkāmasya bhavati’.26 As per him, NṛPūTā is divided into five ‘upaniṣats’. Below is an overview of them:

  • First: Upāsanā of Brahmaveda, sāman with its ancillaries, devas like Agni, lokas like pṛthivī, in Nṛsiṃha through the sāman.
  • Second: Similar upāsanā in Nṛsiṃha, but through praṇava, anuṣṭup-mantra, five ancillaries, etc.
  • Third: Śakti and bīja.
  • Fourth: Kavaca, ancillaries of mūlamantra and sāman, etc.
  • Fifth: Mahācakra and effects produced.

Brahman is understood in two ways: sākāra (with form) and nirākāra (without form). Nṛsiṃha is sākāra Brahman, a form assumed by the nirākāra Brahman for the benefit of upāsakas (those who meditate on a certain kind of Devatā).27 This form of Brahman is to be seen as or meditated upon as fundamentally true.28 Nṛsiṃha is accompanied by his Māyā, which is his śakti (power) meant for creation, sustenance and dissolution of the world.29 In this vidyā/upāsanā, the abheda (non-difference) between the upāsaka (meditator) and upāsya (object of meditation) has been expressed, which points to Nṛsiṃhabrahmādvaita (non-duality with Nṛsiṃhabrahman).30 Further, mokṣa (liberation from the cycle of death and rebirth) is through praṇava (oṅkāra), which shall be taught by the upāsya to the upāsaka at the time of the latter’s death.31

The central instrument for Nṛsiṃha’s upāsanā is the Nṛsiṃha-mantra, which helps in gaining the knowledge of nirākāra Brahman through the knowledge of sākāra Brahman.32 Whoever chants this mantra regularly, attains the effects of performing various vedic yajñas.33 This does not mean that such yajñas would become superfluous, because the said upāsanā, through which the mantra is to be chanted, is very difficult as compared to yajñas, because it is to be performed only through mental activity.34 It is also to be noted that the mantra is similar to praṇava in its power.35 The ultimate goal of this upāsanā is the upaśama (silencing) of the manifestation of duality and realisation of the true self.36

The Nṛsiṃha-mantra, which is in the anuṣṭup-meter, appears in a scattered manner.37 It is to be sung in the form of a sāman (a musical form of the mantra following the rules of Sāmaveda). Bhāṣyakāra has given a detailed account of how the mantra is to be converted into a sāman.38 Because NṛPūTā itself states each part of the mantra in a scattered manner, the Bhāṣyakāra also mentions the conversion similarly. One would expect him to collect all the pieces and put them together for the ease of the reader. But Bhāṣyakāra says that because the sāman is rare and very secret, it is revealed only by speech.39

NṛPūTā states three ancillaries of sāman : praṇava, sāvitrī and yajurlakṣmī. It prohibits the use of these three by women and Śūdras.40 Because the use of these three is prohibited as ancillaries of the sāman, Bhāṣyakāra says that women and Śūdras have adhikāra (ability to generate and experience the desired effect of a certain ritual, etc.) of the pradhāna upāsanā, i.e., chanting of sāman without praṇava, sāvitrī and yajurlakṣmī.41 Such a sāman is called śuddha-sāman.42 We are told that Prajāpati had himself instructed both men and women in this upāsanā.43

The Nṛsiṃha-mantra is to be received from an Ācārya, whereas the sāman can be learnt either from an Ācārya or through other sources like Veda and its commentary/explanation.44 A person who desires to learn it by going to the Ācārya, is fit to receive the sāman.45 The one who fears death, pāpa (unseen demerit) and saṃsāra (cycle of death and rebirth) should approach the Ācārya and receive the Nṛsiṃha-mantra.46 The ultimate result of this upāsanā could be seen as kaivalya (liberation).47

2b) Uniqueness of NṛPūTā

As per the majority traditional view, the upāsanās and mantras mentioned in upaniṣats are meant only for the Brāhmaṇa, Kṣatriya and Vaiśya males who have duly undergone the upanayana saṃskāra and are studying or have studied Vedas in a traditional manner (memorisation of Vedas by listening to one’s Guru’s recitation and repeating after him).49 Hence, those who are beyond these three varṇas and women of all varṇas, are said to have no adhikāra for such upāsanās and mantras.

NṛPūTā as interpreted by Bhāṣyakāra, states the adhikāra of women and Śūdras for performing Nṛsiṃhabrahma-upāsanā through the sāman-form of Nṛsiṃha-mantra, but without the usage of praṇava, sāvitrī and yajurlakṣmī.50 Like many other traditional scholars, Bhāṣyakāra regards this upaniṣat as ‘śruti’, i.e., Veda. So, it is in this part of the Vedas that women and Śūdras have been stated as possessing the adhikāra of a Veda-mantra proper. This mantra is to be received only through a Guru. Although Bhāṣyakāra states that the sāman-form of the mantra can be learnt from other sources also, but it would be better to learn it from a Guru trained in Sāmaveda.

The adjective of adhikāra is ‘vedapūrvaka’, i.e., that which begins with Vedas, or that which is preceded by Vedas. Many words of the form of ‘-pūrvaka’ are found in BrSūBh.55 It is possible that by ‘vedapūrvaka adhikāra’, Śaṅkarācārya points to the adhikāra which starts with the traditional study of Vedas. In this sense, lacking such adhikāra only means that such knowledge for Śūdras does not begin with the study of Vedas. Śūdras are told to begin the acquisition of such knowledge through Itihāsas and Purāṇas. This tells us that for Śūdras, Vedas are not the primary source of a fruitful knowledge of Brahman. Being prohibited as the primary source does not necessarily lead to the prohibition of the source itself.

3b) Possibility of another interpretation of the stated prohibition

The portion where the prohibition has been stated, is concerned with the sāman clubbed with its ancillaries (praṇava, etc.).62 Immediately after stating the ill effects which a woman and a Śūdra would experience by employing the ancillaries in the upāsanā, it is stated that knowing the sāman of the thirty-two syllables (Nṛsiṃha-mantra) would lead to a good result.63

Just after this, once again the ill effects of knowing ancillaries have been stated.64

We find another portion where the good effects of the Nṛsiṃha-mantra for both women and men have been stated.65 In this way, not much space is left for an interpretation where it could be concluded that women and Śūdras do not have adhikāra of the sāman.

Two other commentaries on NṛPūTā have been published. One is by Upaniṣadbrahmendra (1750-1850 CE) and the other is by Govindapaṇḍitācāryya (Bannanje Govindacharya), of which the former is an old one, whereas the latter is a recent creation.66

Upaniṣadbrahmendra restricts the prohibition to the ancillaries only.67 Commenting on the statement of NṛPūTā where the good effects of the Nṛsiṃha-mantra have been stated for both men and women, he takes the expression ‘men and women’ as a general one, and understands it as an indication of the benefit which will be surely received by Brāhmaṇas and the like (Kṣatriyas and Vaiśyas).68

Govindapaṇḍitācāryya says that this statement of NṛPūTā is a clarification of the prohibitive statement.69 This implies the adhikāra of women and Śūdras in the said mantra.

3c) Interpretations of the prohibition by various traditional scholars

Mādhavācārya takes it to be a prohibition of praṇava.70 Nīlakaṇṭha Bhaṭṭa (17th century CE) views it similarly.71 Gadhādhara Rājaguru (18th century CE) cites it to state that the wives of dvija men (those who have duly undergone upanayana, etc.) should not employ Vedic mantras during rituals other than yajñas, rituals after death, etc.72 Raghunandana Bhaṭṭācāryya cites this to state the general prohibition for women against using Vedic mantras.73 If the prohibition is about all Vedic mantras, it will contradict the adhikāra of Nṛsiṃha-mantra and its sāman given to women and Śūdras. Mādhavācārya’s and Nīlakaṇṭha Bhaṭṭa’s understanding of the prohibition seems to be in accordance with that of Bhāṣyakāra.

Conclusion

In NṛPūTāBh, the Bhāṣyakāra expounds the upāsanā of sākāra Brahman of the form of Nṛsiṃha. While he maintains the position that the nirākāra Brahman is the absolute truth, Bhāṣyakāra states that in this upāsanā, the described form of Nṛsiṃha is to be understood or meditated upon as true. The nirākāra becomes sākāra for the benefit of upāsakas. Bhāṣyakāra states the adhikāra of women and Śūdras for singing the sāman-form of Nṛsiṃha-mantra without the use of praṇava, sāvitrī and yajurlakṣmī. This presents to us a rare case where such an adhikāra has been stated on the basis of a text which is considered to be Veda.

Bhāṣyakāra calls PrPaSā his own text. Scholars of Śaṅkarācārya’s tradition attribute PrPaSā to Śaṅkarācārya. Because both the texts are of one author, they attribute NṛPūTāBh to Śaṅkarācārya.

For others, the allegiance of NṛPūTāBh with Śaṅkarācārya’s Advaita philosophy is very clearly visible. This leads to an apparent contradiction between such an adhikāra and the Apaśūdrādhikaraṇa of BrSūBh.

It could be removed by reexamining Śaṅkarācārya’s concluding statement in the said section of BrSūBh. The reinterpretation of the same in the light of NṛPūTāBh tells us that Vedas are not entirely prohibited for Śūdras (and women).

NṛPūTā restricts the restriction regarding Veda-mantra for Śūdras and women only to praṇava, sāvitrī and yajurlakṣmī. Mādhavācārya, Nīlakaṇṭha Bhaṭṭa and Upaniṣadbrahmendra understand the prohibitive statement in this very manner.

Other traditional scholars such as Gadādhara Rājaguru and Raghunandana Bhaṭṭācāryya understand it in a wider sense, of which the latter states a blanket application of the prohibition. A blanket application of the prohibitive statement will lead to a contradiction with the main text itself.

Hence, a restricted and specific application, is a better option. Even for those who may dispute the attribution of NṛPūTāBh to Śaṅkarācārya, it is still the work of an authoritative person in the field of mantras and related rituals, given the fact that his other work PrPaSā has been referred to by various traditional scholars of the olden times, both within and outside of Śaṅkarācārya’s tradition.
