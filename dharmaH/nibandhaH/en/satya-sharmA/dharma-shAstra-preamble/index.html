[[P1]]

A Preamble to Dharmaśāstra

Dr. Satyan Sharma
Author of ‘Bare Minimum Hinduism’
June 24, 2026

[[P2]]

Dharmaśāstra is the name given to the body of texts which teaches about dharma. Dharma is an action, performing which one attains puṇya (unseen merit). Such an attainment is not empirical in nature. It is through dharmaśāstra that we could learn about which actions lead to puṇya and which lead to pāpa (unseen demerit) and under what conditions. Manusmṛti, Yājñavalkyasmṛti, Gautamadharmasūtra, Āpastambadharmasūtra, et cetera, and numerous other texts constitute dharmaśāstra.

[[P3]]

I wanted to find some foundational principles in them, which could act as a general context and help us understand the teachings of dharmaśāstra. Below are ten foundational principles, which I have collected from various texts of dharmaśāstra, followed by a small discussion.

[[P4]]

Dharma leads to eternal happiness, whereas adharma leads to suffering.

adharmaprabhavaṃ caiva
duḥkhayogaṃ śarīriṇām
dharmārthaprabhavaṃ caiva
sukhasaṃyogamakṣayam
- Manusmṛti 6.64

[[P5]]

One must protect oneself in all ways.

sarvata evātmānaṃ gopāyet

  • Gautamadharmasūtra 9.35/1.9.34

[[P6]]

Self-reliance leads to happiness.

yadyat paravaśaṃ karma
tat tad yatnena varjayet
yad yad ātmavaśaṃ tu syāt
tat tat seveta yatnataḥ
sarvaṃ paravaśaṃ duḥkhaṃ
sarvam ātmavaśaṃ sukham
etad vidyāt samāsena
lakṣaṇaṃ sukhaduḥkhayoḥ
- Manusmṛti 4.159-160

[[P7]]

Through contentment, one should stay disciplined, because contentment is the root of happiness, and discontent is the root of suffering.

saṃtoṣaṃ param āsthāya
sukhārthī saṃyato bhavet
saṃtoṣamūlaṃ hi sukhaṃ
duḥkhamūlaṃ viparyayaḥ
- Manusmṛti 4.12

[[P8]]

To the best of one’s ability, one should perform the best practices common to all humans. Some of these practices are; not injuring beings (mentally, verbally and physically), expressing beneficial truth, not stealing, being mentally and physically clean, having control over senses.

ahiṃsā satyamasteyaṃ
śaucamindriyanigrahaḥ
etaṃ sāmāsikaṃ dharmaṃ
cāturvarṇye’bravīn manuḥ
- Manusmṛti 10.63

[[P9]]

Control over senses (and mind) can be better achieved through knowledge (of the reality or faults of the things toward which one gets attracted).

na tathaitāni śakyante
saṃniyantum asevayā
viṣayeṣu prajuṣṭāni
yathā jñānena nityaśaḥ
- Manusmṛti 2.96

[[P10]]

One should strive to possess these eight inner qualities; compassion for all beings, forbearance, absence of envy, cleanliness, ease, good conduct, absence of miserliness, lack of covetousness. Such qualities are also the protectors of dharma.

dayā sarvabhūteṣu
kṣāntiranasūyā śaucam anāyāso maṅgalam akārpaṇyam aspṛheti
yasyaite catvāriṃśat saṃskārā na cāṣṭāv ātmaguṇā
na sa brahmaṇaḥ sāyujyaṃ sālokyaṃ gacchati
yasya tu khalu saṃskārāṇāmekadeśo’py aṣṭāv ātmaguṇā
atha sa brahmaṇaḥ sāyujyaṃ sālokyaṃ ca gacchati
- Gautamadharmasūtra 8.20-23 (or 1.8.23-26)

Mādhava’s commentary on Parāśarasmṛti 1.37.

[[P11]]

The highest dharma is the knowledge of one’s Self (Ātman) through yoga.

ijyācāra-damāhiṃsā-dāna-svādhyāya-karmaṇām
ayaṃ tu paramo dharmo yad yogenātma-darśanam
- Yājñavalkyasmṛti 1.8

[[P12]]

Performance of dharma (mainly ritualistic) also depends upon where the person lives. Hence a person is suggested to reside in a place where similar people (inclined toward dharma) live, and the materials required for performance of rituals are readily available. For others, they should perform only as much as is possible with relative ease.

prabhūtaidhodaka-yava-sasamit-kuśa- mālyopaniṣkramaṇa-māḍhya janākulam analasa-samṛddham
ārya-jana-bhūyiṣṭham adasyu-praveśyaṃ grāmam āvasituṃ yateta dhārmikaḥ
- Bodhāyanadharmasūtra 2.3.51

Govindasvāmī’s commentary on this:

“yatra hi dharmāśramāvirodhena jīvanaṃ sukaraṃ bhavati
tatra dhārmiko nityaṃ nivased ityarthaḥ.”

Medhātithi on Manusmṛti 2.24:

स तु नित्य-काम्यानाम्[^११६] उक्तया रीत्या
एतद्-देश एवानुष्ठान-संभवाद्
अधिकृतानां प्राप्त एव ।
न ह्य् एतद्-देश-व्यतिरेकेण कृत्स्न-धर्मानुष्ठान-संभवः ।
तथा हि, हिमवति तावत्
काश्मीरादौ शीतेनार्दिता न बहिः संध्योपासने ऽधिक्रियन्ते,
न च यथाविधि स्वाध्याय-संभवः
“प्राग् वोदग् वा ग्रामाद् उपनिष्क्रम्य” इति ।
न हि हेमन्त-शिशिरयोर् अहर् अहर् नदी-स्नानादि-संभवः ।

[[P13]]

Dharmaśāstra is not a collection of laws.

[[P14]]

All of us desire to attain lasting happiness. The only difference is that of the path we choose. Authoritative texts of Sanātana Dharma, including those under the title of dharmaśāstra, have been reiterating the fact that happiness is within us and that we do not have to increase our involvement with the outer world to attain it. In this way, dharma, as proclaimed by these texts, offers us protection from the damage which could be caused by excessive and wrong involvement with the world (for the sake of happiness which could be easily achieved through lesser involvement).

Dharmaśāstra does not speak against sensory pleasures, but against the excess of them. It says that one should be aware of the undesirable effects which accompany the desirable effects resulting from any kind of sensory pleasures. Further, it is our own duty to protect our own selves, both physically and mentally. By following the advice of dharmaśāstra to the best of our ability, we can protect ourselves.

[[P15]]

The foundations of dharma are the best practices which are common to all humans. The most important of these is the control over senses and mind. Even if one performs specific ritualistic activities like yajñas, but the person’s mind is not stable, such activities will not bear their complete fruit. Hence one must strive to perform these practices in the best possible manner.

Perfection cannot be achieved in a hurry. This is why dharmaśāstra tells us to move ahead slowly and gradually. The highest goal is to attain knowledge about one’s own ātman or self, which would further lead to mokṣa (liberation from the cycle of birth and death).

[[P16]]

In today’s time, dharmaśāstra is usually seen as a collection of laws or rules to be imposed on people. But that is not the case. Dharmaśāstra does not impose its teachings on anyone, primarily because it is not a living being, but also because it does not intend to do so. Out of the three general divisions of it, namely ācāra, vyavahāra and prāyaścitta, vyavahāra deals with the resolution of disputes.

Ācāra deals with saṃskāras (purificatory rituals), various dharmas such as varṇa dharma, etc. Prāyaścitta deals with expiations aimed at removal of the pāpa (unseen demerit) incurred due to acting against the teachings of the Ācāra section.

[[P17]]

Yet it cannot be seen as law, in the way we know it today. Despite the availability of textual instructions related to vyavahāra, an inscription from the 6th century shows that people could themselves choose their own rules to resolve disputes.

See Charter of Vishnushena, Epigraphia Indica, Vol. 30, No. 30

[[P18]]

This is not at all surprising because dharmaśāstra itself tells that laws or such rules should generally be made by the concerned people themselves and not by the king at his own will.

For example:

A king should protect the traditional and other agreed upon practices of jātis, janapadas (deśas), śreṇīs, kulas, etc. - Manusmṛti 8.41

Farmers, traders, shepherds, money lenders, carpenters, washermen, etc. should decide their matters themselves. The king should learn about their practices from them and make rules. - Gautamadharmasūtra 2.2.22/11.24

[[P19]]

Mādhava, the prime minister of king Bukka of Vijayanagara, says that because the ideals presented in dharmaśāstra are nowhere to be found, instead of discarding it completely, one must follow it as much as possible.

See Mādhava’s commentary on Parāśarasmṛti 11.50½-11.51½

[[P20]]

Such kinds of flexibility would not have been possible if dharmaśāstra were anything like a constitution or a collection of laws, to be imposed on people.

The foundational principles of dharmaśāstra presented above provide relevant context as per which one could view the teachings of it, no matter which text one picks for the study. This list of principles cannot be said to be final. You can take this as a first attempt.
