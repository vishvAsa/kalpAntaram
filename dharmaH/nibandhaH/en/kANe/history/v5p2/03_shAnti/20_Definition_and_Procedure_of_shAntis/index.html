ŚĀNTI (Propitiatory rites for averting a deity’s wrath, a calamity or unlucky event).

CHAPTER XX

Vedic meaning and procedure of [[Sāntis|Śāntis]]

The word [[śānti|Śānti]] is derived from the root ‘śam’, which has several meanings (such as ’to stop’, ’to be appeased’, ’to kill’ rarely) and belongs to the 4th conjugation and also to the 9th. The word Śānti itself does not occur in the [[Rgveda|Ṛgveda]], but it occurs in the Atharvaveda and the Vājasaneya [[Samhitā|Saṁhitā]]. The root ‘śam’, its several forms and derivatives and the indeclinable particle ‘śam’ occur hundreds of times even in the [[Rgveda|Ṛgveda]]. The word ‘śam’ is often conjoined in the [[Rgveda|Ṛgveda]] to ‘[[yoh|yoḥ]]’ either as ‘[[samyoh|śaṁyoḥ]]’ ([[Rg|Ṛg]]. I. 93.7, I. 106.5, III 17.3, III[[|,]]. 18.4, IV. 12.5, V. 47.7, V. 53.14, V. 69.3, VI. 50.7, VII. 35.1, VII[[|,]]. 69.5, X. 9.4, X 15.4, X. 37.11, X. 182.1-3) or as ‘[[sam|śaṁ]] ca yośca’ (as in [[Rg|Ṛg]]. I. 114.2, 189.2, II. 33.13, VIII[[|,]]. 39.4, VIII. 71.15). In these places the words are generally rendered as [[’|"’]]happiness and welfare’ or [[’|"’]]health and wealth’ (by Keith in translation of Tai. S. II. 6.10.3) and these meanings generally suit the context, e. g. in [[Rg|Ṛg]]. VI. 50.7 ‘dhāta tokāya tanayāya [[sam yoh’|śaṁ yoḥ’]] (confer on our sons and progeny happiness and welfare), in [[Rg|Ṛg]]. X. 182 the words [[’*|’]]athā karad-yajamānāya saṁ [[yoh’|yoḥ’]]’ (may [[Bșhaspati|Bṛhaspati]] confer [[happi ness|happiness]] and welfare on the sacrificer) occur as the last quarter of all the three verses. ‘[[Sam|Śam]]’ by itself occurs about 160 times in the [[Rgveda|Ṛgveda]] and it is [[somewliat romarkable|somewhat remarkable]] that in [[Rg|Ṛg]]. VII. 35. 1-131 the word [[sam|śam]] occurs in each verse from 4 to 7 times (68 times in all). [[Rg|Ṛg]]. I. 114. 12 ‘we bring these lauds to Rudra who is powerful, who has braided hair, who rules over valiant men, so that there may result welfare to our two-footed and four-footed beings and every thing in this village may be prosperous and free from distress’ will bring out the import of

[[P720]]‘śam’. In some cases [[“san’|‘śam’]] and [[‘yoh’|‘yoḥ’]] clearly appear to be employed like nouns in the objective case. For example, [[Rg|Ṛg]]. II. 33. 133 “I hanker after the ‘śam’ and [[‘yoh’|‘yoḥ’]] of Rudra (i. [[6|e]]. under the power of Rudra), [[Ry|Ṛg]]. I. [[111|114]]. 2 “O Rudra! may we secure by your guidance that ‘śam’ and ‘yoḥ’ that father Manu procured through sacrifice”. Yāska (Nirukta IV[[|,]]. 21) while dealing with [[Rg|Ṛg]]. X. 15. 4 ‘athā naḥ [[sam|śaṁ]] yor-arapo dadhāta’ holds ‘[[samyoh’|śaṁyoḥ’]] to be ablative or [[genitiro|genitive]] of [[samyu|śaṁyu]] and explains as ‘[[samanam|śamanam]] ca rogāṇā[[m|ṁ]] [[sīvanam|yāvanam]] ca bhayānā[[m|m’]] (allaying of diseases and warding off of dangers). This is an etymological explanation and is in some cases accepted by [[Sayans|Sāyaṇa]] and in others he paraphrases ‘śam’ by ‘sukha’ (happiness) and ‘yoḥ’ as ‘duḥkha-viyoga’ ([[freedoni|freedom]] from pain or distress). The word ‘yoḥ’ presents the appearance of the ablative or genitive singular of a noun from the root ‘yu’ which means ’to bind’ or ‘separate’ or from ‘yā’ to go. [[’“Yoh’|‘Yoḥ’]] by itself [[ocours|occurs]] only three times in the [[Rgveda|Ṛgveda]] (i. [[e,|e.]] in I. 74. 7, X. 105. 3, X. 176. 3). The meaning of [[‘yoh’|‘yoḥ’]] in these three cases is doubtful. In the Tai. Br.4 we have the mantra ‘we choose that happiness and [[wel fare|welfare]] and success (or progress) to the [[sacrifico|sacrifice]] and the sacrificer; may divine fortune be ours; let there be good fortune for (our) men; may medicine (or [[remedy )|remedy)]] go up; may there be happiness to our men and quadrupeds’.

In Atharvaveda 19. 9 the word śānti occurs about 17 times. In verses 3 to 5 speech ([[ |]]vāk), the mind ([[ |]]manaḥ[[ |]])5 and the five senses are referred to and it is said that these [[geven|seven]] usually produce what is ghora (terrible or inauspicious) and these same must exert themselves for producing śānti (i. e. appeasement of angered deities or averting calamities or misfortunes). Verses 6-11 pray to several gods, planets ([[grabāḥ|grahāḥ]]), the earth, falling stars, cows, the nakṣatras, magical rites, Rāhu, Dhūmaketu (comets), the Rudras, Vasus and [[Adityas|Ādityas]], sages and Bṛhaspati to confer happiness. Verse 12 prays to Indra, Brahmā, and all

[[P721]]the gods for refuge to the composer of the hymn and 13 declares that ‘whatever things were appeased (by śāntis) in this world[[–|—]]these the seven sages know. May they all be happiness for me; may happiness be mine and may freedom from fear be mine’. Verse 14 which is similar to Vāj. S. 36. 17 declares that ’the earth, mid-regions, heaven, waters, trees and plants, all gods[[–|—]]these have become appeased and auspicious by the śānti rites performed by the composer and that by those śāntis, by all śāntis I (we) appease (remove the evil effect of) what is here terrible, what is cruel (or inauspicious), what is evil; may all that be appeased, be beneficial and happy for us’.6 Atharva 19. 10 (verses 1-10) contains the word ‘śam’ 51 times and prays for welfare to [[soveral|several]] gods and 19. 11 is also a śānti containing the word ‘śam’ 18 times. The Vājasaneya-[[samhitā|saṁhitā]] chapter 36 (verses 8-12) similarly contain the word ‘śam’ several times. One of these viz. (Vāj. S. 36. 12) occurs in many Vedic texts7 [[’|”’]]May the divine waters confer on us happiness, help and protection; may they flow towards us for [[our our|our]] happiness and welfare’.

In the Tai. S. the words ‘śamayati’ and ‘śānti’ are frequently used [[oftea|often]] in the same passage or context. For example, it is said “Rudra8 is the fierce (or harsh) one among gods…; He (the priest) does as it were a harsh thing when he recites (that passage containing the word) Rudra; ‘in the path of Mitra’ he says for the sake of appeasing”. There is a similar passage in the Ait. Br. While prescribing the recital of a [[sk|ṛk]] [[V|v]]erse ([[Rg|Ṛg]]. II[[|,]]. 33. 1) in the Āgnimāruta-[[sastra|śastra]] the Ait. Br. changes the original words of the latter half of the verse in the [[Rgveda|Ṛgveda]] and also suggests another [[verso|verse]] altogether for recital

[[P722]](I. 43. 6 ‘[[śani|śaṁ]] naḥ karatyarvate [[sugani mosāya mesye nrbhyo nāribhyo|sugaṁ meṣāya meṣye nṛbhyo nārībhyo]] gave’) because in the first place that verse begins with the auspicious word [[‘sam’|‘śaṁ’]] and in the second place because that verse, though the [[derala (|devatā (]]deity) thereof is Rudra, does not expressly mention that word; the result, says the Ait. Br., is that that verse being one of appeasement (śānti) the priest secures long life and procures long life for the sacrificer.

Another example of [[‘samayati|‘śamayati’]] in the Tai. S. is as follows : When an [[anihotrin|agnihotrin]] is going on a journey with his [[houschold ho|household he]] should offer homa to [[Vastospati|Vāstoṣpati]], but if he goes without offering a homa to Vāstoṣpati evil [[consequencos|consequences]] follow; ‘Rudra is indeed Vāstoṣpati; if he were to go out without offering homa to Vāstoṣpati, Agni would become Rudra, would leap after him and kill him; (but) when he offers to [[Vastospati|Vāstoṣpati]], he appeases him (Rudra-Agni) with his own share and the sacrificer meets no injury (or [[distress ):1140|distress)’.9]

Another example of ‘śāmayati’ and [[‘santi’|‘śānti’]] in the Tai. S. (VI. 3. 3. 2–3) may be set out. ‘O tree! protect it; O axe! do not harm it; the axe indeed is a thunderbolt; (therefore he utters those words) for allaying (the fierce power of the axe); these worlds are afraid of the tree (of which the yūpa is made) when it moves; (therefore he recites ‘with thy top do not graze the sky, with thy middle do not harm the atmospheric region’. (By these words) he appeases (removes the terrible power for evil of) this [[tree’, 1141|tree’.10]

The foregoing passages are quite sufficient for showing the significance attached to the words [[‘sam’, ‘samayati’|‘śaṁ’, ‘śāmayati’]] and ‘śānti’ in the Vedic [[samhitās|saṁhitās]]. The word [[‘santama’|‘śaṁtama’]] occurs in the [[Rgveda|Ṛgveda]] about two dozen times. It is applied to the great gods like Agni, Indra, Soma (I. [[16.7|16. 7]], I. [[77.2|77. 2]], IX. [[104,3|104. 3]]), to the praises of gods (I. 76. 1, VI. 32. 1), to the worshipper or singer (VIII. 13. 22), to protection by gods ([[1.76.3|I. 76. 3]], X. 15. 4) and generally means ‘beneficent or conferring happiness’. Simi-

[[P723]]larly, the word [[‘santāti’|‘śaṁtāti’]] ([[Rg|Ṛg]]. I. 112. 20, VIII[[|,]]. [[18.7|18. 7]]) means [[’“beneficent’|‘beneficent’]].

The causal (śamayati) of the verb ‘śam’ and the word ‘śānti’ do not occur in the [[Rgveda|Ṛgveda]], but they are as said above frequently employed in the Taittirīya and other [[Sambitās|Saṁhitās]] and in the Brāhmaṇas. Some further examples may be set out here. The Tai. S. relates the following legend “Agni was in the yonder world (heaven), the sun was in this world; these worlds were (then) unappeased (disturbed). The gods said ‘come, let us change (transpose) these two’. They placed Agni in this world with the words ‘O Agni, come here for the dainty meal’ and (placed) the Sun in the yonder world with the [[worlds|words]] ’the great and powerful one, O Agni’; then indeed these two worlds became free from disturbance. Since he repeated those words in this way, it served for the purpose of quieting these worlds (that were once disturbed). These worlds became undisturbed (auspicious) for him who knows this”.11 Here we have both the verbal form ‘aśāmyatāma’ (from ‘śam’ 4th conj.) and the the word ‘śāntyai’.

In some passages of the [[Roveda|Ṛgveda]] the word [[‘sami’|‘śami’]] occurs (as in I. 87. 5, II. 31. 6, III. 55. 3, VIII. 45. 27, X. 40. 1.). It is generally interpreted by Sāyaṇa in these passages as the locative singular of [[‘sami’|‘śamī’]], which is explained to mean ‘karman’ (action, sacrifice or the like). Two of these may be cited here12 “my desires fly in many directions; I shall brighten ancient (lauds) towards [[‘sami’|‘śamī’]] (for employing them in various sacrifices?); knowing the truth in [[Turvaṣa|Turvaśa]] (king) and Yadu (king) he (Indra) enveloped Ahnavāyya (their enemy) in warlike action.” It is possible to take [[‘sami’|‘śami’]] as the locative singular of ‘śam’ treated as a noun. The word [[‘sami’|‘śamī’]] occurs in many passages of the [[Roveda|Ṛgveda]] (I. 20. 2, I. 83. 4, I. 110. 4, II. 1. 9, III. 60. 3, IV. 3. 4, IV. 17. 18, IV. 22. 8, IV. 33. 4, V. [[77, 4|77. 4]], V. 42. 10, VI. 3. 2, VI. 52. 1, VIII. 75. 14, IX. 74. 7, X. 28. 12). In all these passages

[[P724]]Sāyaṇa interprets it as ‘karma’ and not as the [[‘sami|‘śamī’]] tree or branch’. But in one place at least (if not in more places) the word [[sami|śamī]] can easily be held to mean ’the [[sami|śamī]] wood or fuel-[[stick ‘. 11+|stick’.]]13 ‘To that mortal [[wlio|who]] worships with sacrifices and appeases with [[‘samis’ (saml|‘śamīs’ (śamī]] fuel-sticks) and gives offerings to Agni that abounds in wealth disappointment as to glory never comes nor does sin nor arrogance overtake him.’

The whole of chapter 36 of the [[Vājasaneya-sarhitā|Vājasaneya-saṁhitā]] is employed as [[sānti|śānti]] at the beginning and end of the Pravargya rite, according to Kāt. Śr. S. 26. [[11 ‘śīntikaranain-ūdyantayoh’;|11 ‘śāntikaraṇam-ādyantayoḥ’;]] verses 9-12 of Vāj. S. 36 employ the word [[‘sim’|‘śam’]] 17 times and verse 9 is the same as [[Rgveda|Ṛgveda]] I. 90. 9.

An interesting text in connection with the root [[‘sam’|‘śam’]] is the [[adhvribu praiṣu|adhvrigu praiṣa]], to be recited by [[tho Hot!|the Hotṛ]] priest before the paśu is killed in a sacrifice. Vide H. of Dh. vol. II. p. 1121 note 2504 for the formula which is rather long. The [[important 115|important]]14 words for the present purpose are at the beginning and at the end ‘O divine slayers and human slayers! begin (the process of killing the [[pasu|paśu]]). …O Adhrigu! you should slay (the [[pasu|paśu]]) in such a way that it would be properly carried out’. Here the root ([[sam )|(śam)]] certainly means to slay; this meaning is, however, apparently quite different from the one so far con-[[sidered (viz,|sidered (viz.]] to appease, to remove evil effects). But it may be that there is a secondary meaning, viz. appease the gods by offering parts of the animal killed in the sacrifice.

The [[Taittirlya|Taittirīya]]-brāhmaṇa closely connects the [[sami|śamī]] tree or branch with the conjuring away or appeasing the terrible or angry aspects of deities in the following myth :15 “Prajāpati

[[P725]]created Agni; he (Prajāpati) became afraid ’this Agni might burn me’. He (Prajāpati) pacified (the terrible flame or glow of) Agni with [[śami (branch );|śamī (branch);]] that is the beneficent or happy aspect of [[śami|śamī]] in that the equipment required for Agni is full of [[sami|śamī]] in order to appease Agni and for freedom from being burnt thereafter.’ The idea is that Agni, the moment it was created had a fierce and evil aspect, that was removed by the use of the [[sami|śamī]] and śānti means an action or rite that appeases the evil aspects of a deity and makes the deity beneficent. Similarly, the [[Aitareya-brāhmana|Aitareya-brāhmaṇa]] says:16 “[[Ho|He]] recites the verse ’they worship you with offerings whatever abodes you may have;…he says: O Soma! move towards houses in such a way as not to kill the sons (of the sacrificer).’ Houses are indeed called ‘duryā’ and the house of the sacrificer is afraid of the king Soma when he approaches the sacrificer’s house; when he (the priest) repeats this (verse) he thereby appeases him (king Soma) by a śānti (propitiatory rite); [[ue|he]] (Soma) becoming beneficent (by the [[respetition|repetition]] of the mantra) does not kill the progeny or the cattle (of the sacrificer).” The idea is that king Soma might be angry with the sacrificer if there be any defect in the sacrificial rite and that when the hotṛ priest repeats the verse ‘aviraha [[o-’|so-’]] that verse is the cause of appeasement.

In the [[Satapatha|Śatapatha]]-brāhmaṇa there is a similar reference to the appeasing power of the [[sami|śamī]] branch. “He (the priest) places fuel sticks on this (Agni). … He places (on [[Agui|Agni]]) a fuel stick of [[saml|śamī]] as the first. This (Agni) was kindled when this oblation (of [[sami|śamī]] branch) was offered and flamed upwards. The gods became afraid of him (Agni) lest Agni might harm them. They (gods) saw this [[sami|śamī]] branch and appeased him (Agni) with that; inasmuch as they appeased this (Agni) by means of [[sami (|śamī (]]branch) this is called [[sami.|śamī.]] In the same way this sacrificer appeases with [[sami|śamī]] this (Agni) for procuring appeasement and not for [[food”‘1118.|food”’.]]17 It would be noticed

[[P726]]that here the name [[‘sami’|‘śamī’]] is derived from the root ‘śam’ and it is the means of effecting śānti (appeasement).

In the Brāhmaṇas the means of appeasement are various but simple. Often times the recitation of a Vedic verse or hymn effects śānti. For example, the [[Taittiriya|Taittirīya]]-brāhmaṇa18 prescribes the singing of [[sānans|sāmans]] at the time of consecrating the sacred [[srauta|śrauta]] fires; the three [[stīms|sāmans]] are Rathantara, Vāmadevya and [[Bșhat,|Bṛhat,]] each being connected with the three worlds respectively. “When Agni is being [[takon|taken]] out, he sings the Vāmadevya sāman; Vāmadevya is the atmospheric region and thereby (i. e. by singing [[lamadevya)|Vāmadevya)]] he makes Agni [[establishod|established]] in the atmospheric region; [[Vimadevya is śinti|Vāmadevya is śānti]] (means of bringing about the appeasement of Agni); (on singing [[Vārnadevya|Vāmadevya]]) he takes out Agni that has become [[appeasod (|appeased (]]beneficent) and as bestower of cattle”. The Tai. S. says19 “he says ‘weave [[yo|ye]] with regular measure the work of the singers’. Whatever superfluity (or flaw) is committed in the [[sacrilico,|sacrifice,]] this (recitation) serves to eradicate its evil effects”. The Ait. Br. provides “(the priest) who is about to sprinkle holy water over him20 (the king) should (himself first recite the mantra and) make the king repeat it (after him) ‘O [[Wators!|Waters!]] look on me with a beneficent eye, (and) touch my skin with (your) beneficent body. I invoke all the Agnis that dwell in waters for your sake; this should be done for the purpose that waters (the evil aspects of which have been) not appeased may not destroy the vigour of him who is being sprinkled over”. The [[Hotr|Hotṛ]] performs a [[jupa|japa]] before and after reciting the [[sūmulleni|sāmidhenī]] verses. About this the [[Śānkhāyana-brābmana|Śāṅkhāyana-brāhmaṇa]] says21 that the sāmidhenīs are a thun-

[[P727]]derbolt and that if [[japu|japa]] is performed thereby he appeases [[(samajati)|(śamayati)]] Agni (i. e. Agni becomes auspicious and beneficent).

Water also is declared as a means of effecting the removal or appeasement of evil effects. The Ait. Br. remarks22 “they say what is the atonement [[(prayascitti)|(prāyaścitti)]] if a person’s sacrificial material (milk or rice) when put on a fire for cooking spurts out or overflows? (The reply is) ‘he should make it go down with water for the sake of śānti; water indeed is (a means of) śānti and then he should touch the material (that has fallen out) with his right hand and recite a mantra (that is specified) or he should recite another [[rk|ṛk]] verse ‘by whose power the worlds are made fixed’, which is addressed to Viṣṇu and Varuṇa; Viṣṇu indeed is the saviour against what is badly sacrificed [[(i. o.|(i. e.]] the defects in it), while [[Varuna|Varuṇa]] protects (i. e. prevents obstacles to securing the fruit of) what is well sacrificed; (this latter mantra is recited) for śānti in regard to both (defects as well as good points). This is the prāyaścitti in this case.” It should be noticed that here [[sānti|śānti]] (rite) and [[prāgascitti|prāyaścitti]] are identified. The [[Saṅkhāyana.1151|Śāṅkhāyana]]23 Br. also (III. 6) says “waters are (means of) śānti, (they are) an antidote and therefore after pronouncing the word ‘vaṣaṭ’ the priest touches water.” In the Tai. Ār. (IV. 42) there are 37 mantras of śānti used in the Pravargya rite. Some of these occur in the [[Rgveda-samhita.|Ṛgveda-saṁhitā]] e. g. the 8th [[( Vata. a. vatu bhesajam)|(Vāta ā vātu bheṣajam)]] is [[Rg.|Ṛg.]] X. 186. 1, mantras 15-17 are equal to [[Rg.|Ṛg.]] IV. 31. 1-3; mantras 22-24 are [[Rg.|Ṛg.]] X. 9. 1-3. The same Āraṇyaka (IV. 26-35) and Baud. [[Sr.|Śr.]] S. IX. 18 contain several mantras for śāntis, some of which are interesting and are set out here to show how the theory of śāntis was being expanded or enlarged. “If the24 sacrificial vessel called

[[P728]]mahāvīra (in Pravargya rite) of the hot milk for the Gharma offering were to be stolen by a thief, one should offer in the [[Gārbapatya|Gārhapatya]] fire an oblation of ghee with the [[stura|sruva]] ladle to the accompaniment of the mantra ‘May Day and Night disclose thee as thief”. ‘If a wild25 forest dog were to bark (while the Pravargya rite is on), the priest should repeat by way of a magic [[forinula|formula]] the text ‘vi gā Indra vicaran [[spāśayasra’|spāśayasva’]] (O Indra, observe the cows while you [[more about’),|move about’),]] should cast a kindled firebrand on both sides with the words ‘O Agni, converse with Agni, and then he worships Agni with the words ‘O Agni, salutation to thee once, twice, thrice &c.’ If a vulture screeches he recites the formula ‘you are [[undistinguishablo|undistinguishable]] (from other birds) since you have blood in your beak’, if an owl or she-owl hoots he recites the mantra ‘In this way the owl approaches &c.’. Therefore it may be said that [[sānti|śānti]] is used in the early Vedic texts in several senses viz. (1) the state of being freed from evil aspects or effects, (2) means of [[appoasing|appeasing]] or removing evil aspects, such as water, a Vedic verse or hymn, (3) rite for appeasement.

Apart from the simple śāntis for appeasing deities in sacrificial matters, even the [[Rgreda|Ṛgveda]] contains indications that there were other occurrences which were thought to be unlucky and against the supposed evil effects of which some remedies were employed. For example, [[Rg.|Ṛg.]] X. 164 [[11-5)|(1-5)]] is a hymn [[declarod|declared]] by the [[Anukramapi|Anukramaṇī]] and [[Rgvidhāna|Ṛgvidhāna]] IV. 20. 1 as counteracting the indications of bad [[dreams.: 137|dreams.]]26 Verse 3 of it is ‘May Agni place far away from us all evil and undesirable actions which we may have done while awake or asleep whether by our desires or imprecations or want of desire’. In [[Rg.|Ṛg.]] V. 82. 4-527 the

[[P729]]poet prays ‘O God [[Savitpl|Savitṛ!]] today produce for us welfare [[ondowed|endowed]] with progeny and frighten away the effects of bad dreams; O God [[Savitṣ!|Savitṛ!]] drive away all sins (or evils) and confer on [[ug|us]] what is beneficent’ (or auspicious). In [[Rg.|Ṛg.]] II. 28. 10 the poet prays ‘O king Varuṇa! whoever, whether a helper or a friend, declares to me who ascertained a danger (from what I saw) in a dream or whoever, a thief or a wolf, intends to harm me, from that guard us’. In [[Ry.|Ṛg.]] VIII. 47. 15 the sage exclaims ‘we deliver all our bad [[dreains|dreams]] to Trita Āptya; your kindness cannot be obstructed by any one, the protection given by you is good’. Vide also [[Rg.|Ṛg.]] VIII. 47. 14, 16-18, X. 36. 4, X. 37. 4 for bad dreams. Similarly, the [[Rgved|Ṛgveda]] contains verses which show that cries of birds were deemed to indicate coming good or evil and the hooting of the owl was thought to be unlucky. ‘It28 (the bird) frequently cries and proclaims what is to come and it propels [[bis|his]] speech as an oarsman propels a boat; O bird! May you be auspicious to us; may no overpowering (or unfavourable) phenomenon reach you from any side’. The three verses of [[Rg.|Ṛg.]] II. 43 refer to the cries of birds. ‘The birds, chirping, utter cries towards the south (of the house) like composers of songs that speak for food at various seasons; O bird! when you chirp, speak what would be to our welfare, when you are silent think of good thoughts about us; when flying up (from our house) you utter (cries) like a lute; may we be endowed with valiant sons and speak much in assemblies’. [[Rg.|Ṛg.]] X. 165 contains several verses about a kapota bird and one about an owl. [[Rg.|Ṛg.]] X. 165. 1 and 4 are :29 ‘a kapota bird, messenger of ill-luck, has come to this our house, with whatever (evil) intent; we worship you, we shall perform atonement; O God! May it fare well with our men and quadrupeds. May what the owl expresses (by its [[P730]]hooting) turn out to be fruitless or (untrue). In connection with śāntis three words require to be carefully understood viz. adbhuta, utpāta and nimitta. Adbhuta is an ancient word. It occurs several times in the Ṛgveda and is generally applied to some gods in the sense of ‘wonderful’. In some passages, however, it means ‘future’ and possibly also ‘portentous’. For example, according to Nirukta I. 553 the sage Agastya first promised to offer an offering to Indra, but later he desired to offer the same to the Maruts, that then Indra came to Agastya and complained to him as in Ṛg. I. 170. 1 (what was promised today) even that does not exist, nor will it (the havis) be there (for me) tomorrow; who knows (for certain)

[[P741]]what will happen in future’. Yāska paraphrases ‘adbhuta’ by the word ‘abhūta’ (that has not taken place) and remarks that the word ‘adbhuta’ in popular speech also means ‘something that has not happened before’. [[Rg.|Ṛg.]] I. 25. 1154 says ’the intelligent man expects from this (Varuṇa) all wonders (or portentous happenings), whether already performed or to be performed’. [[Rg.|Ṛg.]] X. 105. 7 is perhaps a little clearer than the preceding. ‘(May Indra reduce our sins) Indra who is golden-bearded, who has tawny horses, whose jaw is never broken and who made against the dasyu for easily killing him a thunderbolt like the sky that is wonderful (or full of portents)’. The word usually employed in the [[Gșhyasūtras|Gṛhyasūtras]] is [[adhluta|adbhuta]] and the śāntis are called ‘adbhutaśāntis’. Adbhuta is a word of very general import. It includes not only such serious phenomena as earth-quakes, eclipses, comets, falling stars but such comparatively minor yet unexpected or unusual happenings as a cow yielding blood-red milk or a cow sucking the udder of another cow. The ancient writer [[Viddha-Garga|Vṛddha-Garga]] defines ‘adbhuta’ as any occurrence that had not occurred before or a total change coming over what has occurred before.55 The 67th [[Ātharvana pariṭiṣta|Ātharvaṇa-pariśiṣṭa]] is called Adbhutaśānti (pp. 432–435). It distributes ‘adbhutas’ into seven groups relating to Indra, Varuṇa, Yama, Agni, [[Kubora,|Kubera,]] Viṣṇu and Vāyu and names some adbhutas under each such as a rainbow at night (relating to Indra), a vulture or owl alighting on a man’s house or a kapota entering it (relating to Yama), smoke without fire (relating to Agni), eclipse on the nakṣatra of a man’s birth (relating to Viṣṇu) and prescribes as śānti the japa of [[Atharvasiras,|Atharvaśiras,]] making brāhmaṇas say ‘svasti,’ honouring and feeding brāhmaṇas. That [[parisista|pariśiṣṭa]] is based on the [[Adbhutabrāhmana|Adbhutabrāhmaṇa]] of the [[Simayeda.|Sāmaveda.]]

The word ‘utpāta’ is rare in the [[Srauta|Śrauta]] or [[Grūya|Gṛhya]] sūtras. The Gautamadharma-sūtra, after enjoining upon the king to

[[P742]]select a learned, well-conducted [[brāhmana|brāhmaṇa]] as his [[purohila,|purohita,]] provides that the king should give heed to what astrologers and interpreters of omens tell (him) and that the purohita should perform śānti rites, rites for prosperity (such as [[vastu-homa|vāstu-homa]]) and magic rites (on behalf of the king)56. But in the Purāṇas and the medieval Sanskrit works it is far more frequent than the word adbhuta; sometimes [[cuibhūta|adbhuta]] and [[!/pāla|utpāta]] are used as synonyms. Garga says57 ‘deities become unfavourable owing to the wrong doings of [[nien|men]] and create extraordinary [[happen ings|happenings]] in the sky, atmospheric region and on the earth. These are the utpātas for all worlds created by gods; these utpātas sally forth for the destruction (of [[peuplu|people]]) and they by their (terrific) appearances rouse people (to do what is proper.)’. Here the words ‘adbhuta’ and ‘[[utpata|utpāta]]’ are used as synonyms. The omens and portents mentioned in the two epics are generally described as occurring at certain important [[timon|times]] and events, e. g. on the eve of battles or when [[Dasaratha|Daśaratha]] proposed that Rāma be crowned as [[yurarūjr,|yuvarāja,]] or when [[Pāpdavas startad|Pāṇḍavas started]] on their exile into the forest or when [[Bbisma|Bhīṣma]] was anointed as commander-in-chief of the Kaurava hosts or when Arjuna vowed that he would kill Jayadratha before sunset. There is no [[grada tion|gradation]] or order in mentioning the omens and portents in these two works, but they are set out pell-mell. Untimely rain, thunder without clouds, croaking of frogs are on the same level and

[[P745]]uttered in the same breath with eclipses. But Garga,62 [[Parasara,|Parāśara,]] [[Sabha-parva,|Sabhā-parva,]] [[Br̥hatsamhita|Bṛhatsaṁhitā]] 45. [[2,Matsyapurapa|2, Matsyapurāṇa]] 229. 5, [[Atharva-parisista|Atharva-pariśiṣṭa]] LXIX (1. 2) and other works divide [[utpatas|utpātas]] into three classes, viz. divya (arising from heavenly bodies), [[ūntariksa|āntarikṣa]] (springing in the sky or atmospheric region) and bhauma (that appear on the earth). This classification is ancient enough. The [[Atharvaveda. 1190_expressly|Atharvaveda]]63 expressly refers to the three classes of utpātas ‘May the earthly and atmospheric utpātas and the planets moving in heaven confer welfare on us’. The [[Br̥hatsamhita. 1191|Bṛhatsaṁhitā]]64 says that it is the king’s business to arrange for śāntis in his kingdom for counteracting (the consequences of portents). Garga65 [[doclared|declared]] that those who, when advised by brāhmaṇas for performing śāntis, perform auspicious rites with faith do not suffer defeat, but that those who having [[po|no]] faith or because of atheism or resentment do not perform rites for counteracting (omens) perish in a short time. Garga, the [[Br̥hatsamhita,|Bṛhatsaṁhitā,]] Matsya 229. 6-9, Agni 263. 12-13 furnish examples of the three kinds of utpātas,66 viz. the divya ones are concerned with the abnormal conditions of planets and nakṣatras, eclipses, comets; those of atmospheric regions are hurricanes, unusual clouds, fall of meteors, twilights, preternatural reddish appearances of the quarters, halo, Fata Morgana (appearance of illusory town in the air), rainbow and strange rainfall (such as

[[P746]]of blood-red water, or fall of rain with fishes, tortoises &c.); those of the earth are earth-quake and unusual states of water reservoirs. The [[Br̥hatsambitā|Bṛhatsaṁhitā]] remarks that the evil [[conse quences|consequences]] of earthly [[(bhaumu)|(bhauma)]] [[utpūtas|utpātas]] when counteracted with śāntis are removed, the evil [[consequencos|consequences]] of utpātas from the atmospheric regions are reduced to a mild form ([[ |]]by śāntis), while, according to [[somo|some]] [[(icāryas|(ācāryas]] like Kāśyapa, says Utpala) divya utpātas are not counteracted by śāntis at all; the opinion of Varāhamihira himself appears to be that the consequences of even [[dilya|divya]] utpātas are conjured away by the gifts of plenty of gold, food, cows and land and by spilling the milk of cows on the ground or in a Rudra temple and [[be|by]] performing [[kritihoma.|Koṭihoma.]] Varāhamihira and Matsya further provide that daiva utpāta has evil effects (lit. bears fruit) in eight ways, viz. on the king himself, his son, his treasury, his [[converancos|conveyances]] (horses, elephants &c,), his capital, his queen, [[purobita|purohita]] and his people.67

Numerous śāntis bearing different names are prescribed in Matsya, by [[Varāhamibira|Varāhamihira]] and others. The 18 śāntis prescribed in Matsya [[(228)114.|(228)]]68 and bearing the names of several gods will be briefly mentioned here. Abhaya-śānti is prescribed when a king desires to be a conqueror or when he is attacked by enemies or when he fears that witchcraft has been practised against him or when he desires to uproot his enemies or when a great danger threatens. The [[Saluun yn|Saumyā]] śānti is prescribed when a man is attacked by Tuberculosis or is weak owing to wounds or when a man desires to perform a sacrifice. When there is an earthquake or when there is a famine of food or there is excessive rain or drought or there is danger of [[locysts|locusts]] or when thieves are operating the [[Vuiṣnuri|Vaiṣṇavī]] śānti is prescribed; [[Raudri|Raudrī]] śānti is employed against an epidemic among cattle or human beings or when ghosts appear or when a coronation is to take place or when there is fear of an invasion or there is treachery in one’s kingdom, or when enemies are to be killed; [[Brūhmi|Brāhmī]] śānti is performed when it is feared that Veda study would

[[P747]]perish or when atheism prevails or where honour is paid to persons unworthy of it; if strong winds blow for more than three days and disease spreads due to vāta, then [[Vāyavi|Vāyavī]] śānti should be performed; [[Vūruṇi|Vāruṇī]] when there is fear of drought or there is abnormal rain (of blood &c.); [[Blūrgavi|Bhārgavī]] when there is danger of false accusation; [[Prūjāpalyū|Prājāpatyā]] when abnormal births take place; [[Trūṣtri|Tvāṣṭrī]] when there are abnormal conditions of implements; [[Kaumūri|Kaumārī]] when śānti is to be performed for children; [[Agneyi|Āgneyī]] when fire shows portentous appearances; [[Gāndharri|Gāndharvī]] when a person is disobeyed or his wife and servants perish, or he desires to perform śānti for horses; [[Āigirasi|Āṅgirasī]] when elephants are [[affocted;|affected;]] [[Nairsli|Nairṛtī]] when danger arises from goblins; [[Yūniyū|Yāmyā]] when there is fear of an accident leading to death or a bad dream; [[Kanberi|Kauberī]] when wealth is lost; [[I’arthivi|Pārthivī]] when trees are affected by abnormal conditions [[ ;|;]] [[Aincri|Aindrī]] when portents happen on [[Jyoșthā|Jyeṣṭhā]] nakṣatra or on [[Anuradhā.|Anurādhā.]]

The Agnipurāṇa (263. 7-8) refers to these 18 śāntis and says that the best śāntis are Amṛtā, Abhayā and Saumyā. Varāhamihira mentions numerous śāntis on the happenings of several abnormal incidents. For reasons of space nothing can be said here about them. But one śānti deserves to be set out.69 [[*If|‘If]] a man perceives [[Yaksas,|Yakṣas,]] the astrologer should declare that an epidemic is very near; for counteracting them Garga [[per formed|performed]] a propitiatory rite, viz. Mahāśāntis, offerings, plentiful food, worship of Indra and [[Indrāṇi.’|Indrāṇī.’]] The [[Bṛhatsambitā|Bṛhatsaṁhitā]] sets out ([[ |]]45. 82-95) certain happenings as not portentous when they happen in certain seasons and quotes several verses of Ṛṣiputra which also occur in Matsya 229. 14-25 with some variations; [[. g.|e. g.]] in Caitra and Vaiśākha the following are auspicious (and not portents requiring śānti) lightning, meteors, earthquake, blazing twilight, noisy storms, halo, dust in sky, smoke in forests, [[rod|red]] sunrise and sunset.
