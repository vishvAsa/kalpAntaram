[[P1352]]SECTION VIII Relation of [[Sāikhya|Sāṅkhya]], Yoga and Tarka to Dharmaśāstra 

CHAPTER XXXI [[Dharmaśāstra and Saṅkhya.|Dharmaśāstra and Sāṅkhya.]]

[[Sāokhya|Sāṅkhya]] is one of the well-known six [[yarārenns|darśanas]] ([[philoso phical|philosophical]] points of view). 

[[Saṅkarācārya|Śaṅkarācārya]] in his [[bhagya|bhāṣya]][^2208] on V. S. II. 2. 17 says that Manu and others who were deeply versed in the Veda took up to some extent in their works the doctrine of [[pralhūna|pradhāna]] being the cause of the world with the idea of relying on that part of its doctrine viz. the effect is (already) existent in the cause. Similarly, on [[V, S. I. 4.38|V. S. I. 4. 28]] he says that great effort was made by the Sūtrakāra and himself for refuting the doctrines of the Sāṅkhya system (and not for the refutation of the theory of atoms being the cause of the world), since the Sāṅkhya system is near to the Vedānta as it accepts the view of the [[non-diffor. ence|non-difference]] of cause and effect and as some Dharmasūtra writers like [[Dovala|Devala]] have had resort to it in their works. On V. S. II. 1. 3 [[Saṅkarācārya|Śaṅkarācārya]] remarks that, though there are many [[smrtis|smṛtis]] dealing with metaphysical topics, great effort was made for the refutation of [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] and Yoga only, since the two systems are well-known in the world as means of securing the highest goal of man, as they have been accepted by [[śistas|śiṣṭas]] (respectable and learned people) and are supported by Vedic indications in their favour (as in [[svetāśvatara|Śvetāśvatara]] Up. 'tat-kāraṇam [[sānkhyayogabhi pannam'|sāṅkhyayogābhipannam']] VI. 13). It would be shown later on that Manu and Devala rely on and propound ([[somo|some]]) Sāṅkhya doctrines.

[[P1353]]It would not be irrelevant to say a few words about the origin and development of the [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] system. This (rise of [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]]) is one of the most difficult problems in Indian [[philo sophy.|philosophy.]] A good many works and papers have been written on the [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] system.[^2209] As to what the original Sāṅkhya teaching revised in the Sāṅkhyakārikā of [[fśvarakrsna|Īśvarakṛṣṇa]] was, no generally accepted answer can be given. From the 5th century [[A, D.|A. D.]] at least the [[Saṅkhyakārikā|Sāṅkhyakārikā]] of [[Iśvarakrsna|Īśvarakṛṣṇa]] has been [[re garded|regarded]] as the foremost exponent of the [[Sārkhya.|Sāṅkhya.]] The [[Saṅkhya kārikā|Sāṅkhyakārikā]] and a commentary on it were translated into Chinese about 546 A. D. by [[Paramartha,|Paramārtha,]] originally a brāhmaṇa of Bhāradvāja gotra and a [[śramaña|śramaṇa]] from [[Ujjayini|Ujjayinī]] (vide BEFEO, 1904, p. 60). [[Saṅkarācārya|Śaṅkarācārya]] on V. S. [[I, 4, 11|I. 4. 11]] quotes the whole of the 3rd [[perse|verse]] of the [[Sāökbyakārikā|Sāṅkhyakārikā]] and the first quarter of it on V. S. I. 4. 8. But the Sāṅkhya system, it appears, went through several phases. Chinese sources say that there were eighteen [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] schools (p. 2 of Johnston's [["Early Saṅkhya,"|“Early Sāṅkhya,”]] quoting Takakusu in BEFEO, 1904, p. 58). 

There is the [[Saṅkhyasūtra|Sāṅkhyasūtra]] or [[Saṅkhyapravacanasātra|Sāṅkhyapravacanasūtra]] attributed to Kapila (published with two commentaries viz. that of Aniruddha and parts of the [[Tilā|Ṭīkā]] of Vedāntin [[Mahadeva|Mahādeva]] edited by Garbe in B. I. Series 1881). This is a late work composed about 1400 A. D. as shown by Garbe in his Introduction and by [[P1354]]Fitz-Edward Hall in his learned Introduction to the edition of the [[Saṅkhyaprayacana-bhāṣya|Sāṅkhyapravacana-bhāṣya]] (B. I[[. Series,| Series,]] 1856). There is an edition of 23 sūtras called Tattvasamāsa with a commentary called [[Kramadipika|Kramadīpikā]] in the Chowkhamba Sanskrit series [[whioh|which]] has also published several other brief late works that are passed over here. Several commentaries on the [[Saṅkhyakārikā|Sāṅkhyakārikā]] have been published. The earliest appears to be the one translated into Chinese by [[Paramirtha|Paramārtha]] about 546 A. D., which has been reconstructed into Sanskrit from the Chinese by the learned Pandit Aiyaswami Sastri and published (in 1944) in the [[Sri.|Śrī]] Venkatesvara Oriental Series with a valuable Introduction; another commentary styled [[Mātharavștti|Māṭharavṛtti]] was published (in 1922) in the [[Chowkbamba|Chowkhamba]] Sanskrit Series. Dr. Belvalkar (in ABORI vol. V. pp. [[133-161)|133–161)]] contributed a long and scholarly paper on the [[Matharavstti|Māṭharavṛtti]] in which he holds that the [[Mātharavștti|Māṭharavṛtti]] is the original of the commentary translated into Chinese by Paramārtha together with some accretions made from time to time, that [[Gaudapāda's|Gauḍapāda's]] commentary is a simplified [[abridgmont|abridgment]] of the [[Matharavștti|Māṭharavṛtti]] (p. 148), that the [[Mātharavștti|Māṭharavṛtti]] cannot be placed later than 450 [[A D.|A. D.]] (p. 155) and that [[Iśvarakļṣṇa|Īśvarakṛṣṇa]] cannot be assigned to a date later than 250 A. D. (p. 168). Prof. A. B. Keith in [['Saṅkhya system'|'Sāṅkhya system']] (p. 51) places [[Iśvarakrsna|Īśvarakṛṣṇa]] at a [[data not later thao 323|date not later than 325]] A. D. Another early commentary is the [[Yuktidspikā|Yuktidīpikā]] of an unnamed author edited from a single [[ma.|ms.]] by Shri [[Pulipabehari|Pulinbehari]] Chakravarti in Calcutta Sanskrit Series (1938). This is a very valuable commentary and has been very competently edited [[oven|even]] from a single ms, though there are a few gaps here and there. That commentary is replete with quotations and polemical discussions, mentions several [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] teachers who differed among themselves and whose opinions are cited on many topics. For example, vide under Vindhyavāsin below. It mentions some ācāryas that are hardly [[over|ever]] mentioned in any [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] work. It frequently cites an author called [[Pañcādhikarana|Pañcādhikaraṇa]] (vide pp. 6, 108, 114, 132, 144, 147, 148 where two Āryās apparently of [[Pañcādhikarana|Pañcādhikaraṇa]] are quoted). Another hitherto unknown [[Sāākhyācārya|Sāṅkhyācārya]] mentioned by this com. is Paurika on p. 169 and p. 175, who holds the surprising view[^2210] that there is a separate [[prailhāna|pradhāna]] for each Puruṣa. [[Patanjali|Patañjali]] is frequently mentioned on [[pp.|p.]] 32 (who denied the existence of [[P1355]][[Ahankara),|Ahaṅkāra),]] 108, 132 (there are 12 [[karanas|karaṇas]] and not 13 as [[8.|S.]] [[Kārlka|Kārikā]] in verse 32 asserts), 145, 149, 175; [[Vārsagañaḥ|Vārṣagaṇāḥ]] (in the plural) are mentioned on pp. 39, 67, 95, 102, 133, 145, 170; [[Vārṣagapa|Vārṣagaṇa]] (pp. 6, 108) and [[Vārṣagaṇavira ( on|Vārṣagaṇavīra (on]] pp. 72, 108, 175) styled bhagavān on p. 72 and [[Viṣagapavira (on p. 103 probably meaning the son|Vṛṣagaṇavīra (on p. 103 probably meaning 'the son]] of Vṛṣagaṇa'); all these refer to the views of [[Vārṣaganas.|Vārṣagaṇas.]] [[Pañoaśikha|Pañcaśikha]] (p. 31 in the plural, p. 61, p. 175) is mentioned and one passage which is quoted in the [[Vyisa bhāṣya ( on Y. 8.|Vyāsa-bhāṣya (on Y. S.]] 1. 4) and ascribed to [[Pancaśikha|Pañcaśikha]] by Vācaspati is quoted by the [[Yuktidipikā|Yuktidīpikā]] on p. 41 [[ds|as]] Śāstra. From pp. 113 and 129 it appears that the author of the [[com,|com.]] was a Vedāntin.[^2211] It is probable that he flourished between 500 and 700 A. D., since he quotes (on [[p,|p.]] 39) [[Dinnāga's|Diṅnāga's]] definition of 'pratyakṣa' and is not cited by [[Vaoaspati|Vācaspati]] and other commentators on the [[Bankhya.|Sāṅkhya.]]

Before proceeding further the most characteristic and fundamental conceptions of the standard Sāṅkhya must be set out. The most fundamental conception is that there are two entirely distinct [[esgences|essences]] existing from eternity, viz. [[praksti|prakṛti]] (i. e. nature or matter) also called pradhāna and avyakta, and puruṣa (soul or spirit, the knowing subject). The 2nd fundamental conception is that puruṣas are many. Another most [[characteri. stio|characteristic]] feature of the [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] system is the doctrine of three[^2215] guṇas (factors) called sattva (light, intelligent), rajas (energetic, active, domineering), tamas (coarse, inert, enveloping, dark). [[Pradhaps|Pradhāna]] or prakṛti or Avyakta is said to be constituted by the three guṇas (triguṇātmaka) when they are in equilibrium ([[Sattvarajastamasām sāmyāvasthā|Sattvarajastamasāṁ sāmyāvasthā]]). The [[Sārkbya|Sāṅkhya]] analysed all physical and mental phenomena. The lowest factor is the heavy impenetrable matter and coarse foolish impulses. They are called tamas ([[desoribed|described]] as guru, heavy and [[varanaka,|varaṇaka,]] enveloping). Then there is the factor of constant change in the physical world as well as in the mental. This is called [[rajag|rajas]] (cala, changing and [[upastambhaka,|upaṣṭambhaka,]] exciting, as the [[8.|S.]] [[Karika|Kārikā]] puts it). The third factor or element in the world is the varied operation of consciousness that leads to knowledge and [[experi ence,|experience,]] which is called Sattva (laghu, light as opposed to the coarse, merely material objects and prakāśaka, luminous, as contrasted with tamas). These three factors or elements combine in various proportions and make up the evolving universe. The three are called [[gupas|guṇas]] from several points of view viz. they are qualities, they also bind as with a rope the Puruṣa to [[samsāra.|saṁsāra.]] The basis of the universe is to be found in the guṇas. In view of the fact that the word 'Sāṅkhya' occurs in the Śv. Up. and that the Kaṭha and Muṇḍaka have some doctrines similar to the Sāṅkhya system and the Śvetāśvatara employs many words that occur frequently in the works on Sāṅkhya, the question arises—what is the relation of the Sāṅkhya to the Upaniṣads. There are three possible views, (1) that [[Upe nizad|Upaniṣad]] thought and [[Sāṅkbya|Sāṅkhya]] made parallel progress, (2) that [[Sinkhya|Sāṅkhya]] adopted and expanded some of the germinal ideas [[P1363]]of the [[Upaniṣada,|Upaniṣads,]] (3) that some Upaniṣads borrowed from the Sāṅkhya. It is not possible to discuss these questions here for reasons of space and relevance. The present author holds that it is the Sāṅkhya that based itself on the thoughts of the [[Upanigads,|Upaniṣads,]] particularly when the older Upaniṣads like the Bṛ. Up. and Chāndogya hardly exhibit any traces of Sāṅkhya doctrines or technique, when Sāṅkhya [[reference|references]] are found only in Kaṭha, Muṇḍaka, Śvetāśvatara, Praśna (which are held to be later than Chān. Up. and Bṛ. Up. by modern scholarship) and when there is hardly any work or writer of purely Sāṅkhya doctrines that can be assigned to a period even a few centuries earlier than the Christian era and when no one can place the principal Upaniṣads (about twelve, including the Śvetāśvatara) later than 300 [[B.O.,|B. C.,]] since even the Śv. Up. is referred to as 'Śruti' in [[V, s.|V. S.]] I. 4. 8, II. 3. 22. Garbe (in [['Die Sāökhya Philosophie'|'Die Sāṅkhya Philosophie']] pp. 3 ff) denies that Sāṅkhya underwent any [[essen tial|essential]] modifications in its long history. Jacobi does not agree, holding that Sāṅkhya originated out of a common cultural and philosophical heritage and Oldenberg [[goes|sees]] the origin of [[Sāökhya|Sāṅkhya]] in the Kaṭha and Śv. Up. and holds that epic Sāṅkhya is an independent development ([['Die|('Die]] Lehre der Upanishaden [[uöd|und]] die [[Anfānge|Anfänge]] des Buddhismus', 1915, pp. 206 ff). Sāṅkhya and Yoga were known to [[Kautilya|Kauṭilya]] ([[Saṅkhyam Yogo Lokāyatam cetyanyikṣiki' Arthaśāstrā|('Sāṅkhyaṁ Yogo Lokāyataṁ cetyānvīkṣikī' Arthaśāstra]] I. 2. p. 6.). Therefore, we may say that Sāṅkhya as a philosophical school started at least before the 4th century [[B. O.|B. C.]]

In the Nārāyaṇīya section of the [[Santiparva|Śāntiparva]] (quoted in note 1544 p. 954 above) 'Sāṅkhya, Yoga, [[Pāvoarātra,|Pāñcarātra,]] Vedas and [[Pasupata|Pāśupata]] are said to be knowledges (jñānāni) and 'nānāmatāni' (different points of view) and Kapila, [[&|a]] paramarṣi, is said to be propounder of Sāṅkhya. Chapter 294 (in verses 26–49) deals with the twenty-five tattvas of the Sāṅkhya from [[Prakçti|Prakṛti]] or Avyakta, mahat, ahaṅkāra, the five elements from [[abankāra|ahaṅkāra]] (these eight being called prakṛtis) and then 16 vikāras ([[Verse 29).|verse 29).]] These are called kṣetra, the soul is the 25th (v. 35) and is called kṣetrajña and Puruṣa (v. 37 'avyaktake pure śete [[purusas-ceti|puruṣaś-ceti]] kathyate'), and Īśvara or brahma is not brought in anywhere in that chapter.

Chapters 211–212 (containing in all 100 [[versen)|verses)]] of [[Santi parva|Śāntiparva]] deal with what Janaka, called Janadeva,[^2231] king of [[Mithila,|Mithilā,]] learnt from Pañcaśikha, when the latter, after wandering over the whole earth, visited Mithilā and who is described as the first (or foremost) pupil of Āsuri and who is said to have performed [[&|a]] sattra for a thousand years on [[Pañoasrotas|Pañcasrotas]][^2232] [[P1366]](verse 10). He was the son of a [[brahmana|brāhmaṇa]] woman called [[Kapila|Kapilā]] and was therefore known as Kāpileya (verses 13–15). Janaka had a hundred [[acāryas|ācāryas]] in his palace who propounded the [[diffaront|different]] views of different sects. [[Vorse|Verse]] 9 says that Pañcaśikha made people wonder when he appeared like Kapila, the great sage (paramarṣi) and Prajāpati, that he [[oonfounded|confounded]] the hundred ācāryas by his arguments (v. 17). Later Janaka abandoned those ācāryas and followed Pañcaśikha (verse 18). He, after creating disgust in Janaka about his caste or about rites and about everything, expounded to [[Janaks|Janaka]] the highest mokṣa which is declared as Sāṅkhya. In [[chấp.|chap.]] 212 [[Pancaśikba|Pañcaśikha]] expounds five elements, five jñānendriyas, five karmendriyas, mind (verses 7–22) and the signs ([[lingas)|(liṅgas)]] of sāttvika, rājasa and tāmasa bhāvas (verses 25–28) and describes [[bow|how]] a person who seeks the Self is freed from the fetters of pleasure and pain and being beyond the fear of old age and death reaches immortality. In the first place, these two chapters are not sometimes very clear, offer no consistent [[cloar-cut|clear-cut]] system and employ words that are difficult to construe and are not completely identical with the scheme of the 25 tattvas of the official Sāṅkhya of the Kārikā, since chap. 211 verse 13 speaks of [['ekāksara brabm8|'ekākṣara brahma]] assuming many forms'. For example, it is difficult to say what is meant by 'puruṣāvastham-avyaktam'. The meaning that can be drawn out of the words seems to be 'he (Pañcaśikha) conveyed knowledge (to Janaka) about Avyakta (i. e. Pradhāna) that depends upon Puruṣa (i. e. that becomes active through union of Puruṣa with it), which is the highest truth. It further says that Pañcaśikha became perfect (in knowledge) by the performance of [[istis|iṣṭis]] and sattras, again by abounding tapas, had a vision of the deity (God) and he realized the distinction between kṣetra and [[ksetrajña|kṣetrajña]] and also realized brahma symbolized[^2233] by om. There-[[P1367]]fore, the doctrine of Pañcaśikha in these chapters of Śāntiparva is really advaita (monism) on which are grafted some doctrines similar to the later official Sāṅkhya for explaining creation and so forth.[^2234] In Śāntiparva 306. 56–66 (= [[Chitra ed,|Chitra ed.]] 318. 58–62) [[Visvāvasu|Viśvāvasu]] tells Yājñavalkya that he has heard about the 25th tattva (i. e. Puruṣa) from Jaigīṣavya, Asita-Devala, Vārṣagaṇya (of Parāśara gotra), Bhṛgu, Pañcaśikha, Kapila, Śuka, Gotama, [[Arṣṭiṣopa,|Ārṣṭiṣeṇa,]] Garga, Nārada, [[Āguri,|Āsuri,]] Pulastya, Sanatkumāra, Śukra and Kaśyapa (father of Viśvāvasu) and [[336.65|306. 65]] (Chitra. ed. 318. 67) states that Yājñavalkya had mastered completely both [[sānkhya|Sāṅkhya]] and Yoga. Śāntiparva 304. 4 (= 316. 4 of Citra. ed.) states that Sāṅkhya and Yoga are one.[^2235]

Pañcaśikha figures frequently in the [[Mahabharata.|Mahābhārata.]] In Śāntiparva, chap. 307 (containing only 14 [[verges)|verses)]] Yudhiṣṭhira asks [[Bbiṣma|Bhīṣma]] how one can rise superior to old age and death, [[P1368]]whether by very great tapas[^2236] or by rites or by Vedic learning or by the use of [[rayāyanas (Blixirs|rasāyanas (elixirs]] of life or alchemist's [[prescrip tions ).|prescriptions).]] Bhīṣma speaks of an ancient dialogue between Janaka and Bhikṣu Pañcaśikha, where the same question was put by Janaka and Pañcaśikha replied that none can escape these two and that union with wife and relatives is like meeting [[persor.s|persons]] when on a journey (i. e. the company of these is not durable or permanent), nobody has seen heaven or hell, that one's duty is not to violate the ordinances of Vedas, and to make gifts and to offer sacrifices. In this chapter there is hardly anything which approaches the special doctrines of the Sāṅkhya, though Pañcaśikha's views are [[liere|here]] set forth. Chap. 308 (191 [[versos|verses]] out of which less than 30 have anything to do with Pañcaśikha's teachings directly) begins with the question of Yudhiṣṭhira 'who secured mokṣa without giving up the stage of householder.' Then Bhīṣma narrates to him the dialogue between Janaka called [[Dharmadbvaja 2337|Dharmadhvaja]][^2237] and Sulabhā, a nun. Janaka was learned in the Veda, in mokṣaśāstra and in his own [[sāstra (|śāstra (]]rājadharma), he had controlled his senses and ruled the earth. She heard from ascetics about [[Jadaka's|Janaka's]] righteous conduct and she desired to find out the truth. She gave up her form as [[& pun|a nun]] by her yogic powers and became a woman, a paragon of beauty, and met [[Japaka.|Janaka.]] The latter informed [[ber|her]] that he was the pupil of old [[bhikṣu 2238|bhikṣu]][^2238] Pañcaśikha of the Pārāśarya gotra who stayed with him during the four months of the rainy season and who expounded to him the threefold mokṣa (viz. Sāṅkhya, Yoga and the science of government) and yet who did not ask him to give up rulership. Janaka said [[P1369]]“I, giving up all attachment, though occupying the highest position (as ruler), follow the three paths of mokṣa; the highest rule of this mokṣa is 'freedom from attachment', the absence of attachment is due to correct knowledge whereby one is [[freod|freed]] from bondage (of saṁsāra)”. Janaka further avows that[^2239] on account of the knowledge imparted to him by the bhikṣu who [[ig. called|is called]] after his top-knot, he is free from all attachment, though looking after the kingdom and that he is different [[froma other sannyāsing.|from other sannyāsins.]] Then Janaka gives in [[verges|verses]] (308. 38–41) another meaning[^2240] of the threefold Mokṣa propounded by Pañcaśikha to him viz. (1) highest knowledge and renunciation of actions, (2) strict adherence to the [[patha|paths]] of karma (duties prescribed by Śāstra) and (3) combination of knowledge and actions, and it is said that those who follow this third path resemble householders in [[soveral|several]] respects. Janaka asserts his view 'wearing ochre-coloured[^2241] garments, shaving the head, carrying a [[water-Vossel|water-vessel]] placed on three [[staves those|staves—these]] are merely outward signs and do not lead to mokṣa and that mokṣa does not consist in non-possession of wealth and bondage does not follow possession of some wealth, but it is jñāna by which a [[porson|person]] secures deliverance, whether there is possession or non-possession of wealth'. It appears from verse 40 that Pañcaśikha emphasized the third kind of [[Mokṣaniṣtha (Jõānakarmasamuccaya)|Mokṣaniṣṭhā (Jñānakarmasamuccaya)]] and Janaka followed that teaching. The rest of the chapter (308) is concerned with Janaka's charges against Sulabhā and Sulabhā's [[soathing|scathing]] remarks against [[Janaka.3242|Janaka.]][^2242] She says at the end 'you [[P1370]]have indeed heard from [[Pañoaśikha|Pañcaśikha]] the whole (doctrine) about mokṣa together with the means (for attaining it), with the Upaniṣad passages expounding it or with the auxiliaries (of meditation) and with definite conclusions.'
