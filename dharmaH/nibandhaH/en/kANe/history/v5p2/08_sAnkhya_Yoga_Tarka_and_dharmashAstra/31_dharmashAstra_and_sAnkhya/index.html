[[P1352]]SECTION VIII Relation of [[Sāikhya|Sāṅkhya]], Yoga and Tarka to Dharmaśāstra

CHAPTER XXXI [[Dharmaśāstra and Saṅkhya.|Dharmaśāstra and Sāṅkhya.]]

[[Sāokhya|Sāṅkhya]] is one of the well-known six [[yarārenns|darśanas]] ([[philoso phical|philosophical]] points of view).

[[Saṅkarācārya|Śaṅkarācārya]] in his [[bhagya|bhāṣya]]1 on V. S. II. 2. 17 says that Manu and others who were deeply versed in the Veda took up to some extent in their works the doctrine of [[pralhūna|pradhāna]] being the cause of the world with the idea of relying on that part of its doctrine viz. the effect is (already) existent in the cause. Similarly, on [[V, S. I. 4.38|V. S. I. 4. 28]] he says that great effort was made by the Sūtrakāra and himself for refuting the doctrines of the Sāṅkhya system (and not for the refutation of the theory of atoms being the cause of the world), since the Sāṅkhya system is near to the Vedānta as it accepts the view of the [[non-diffor. ence|non-difference]] of cause and effect and as some Dharmasūtra writers like [[Dovala|Devala]] have had resort to it in their works. On V. S. II. 1. 3 [[Saṅkarācārya|Śaṅkarācārya]] remarks that, though there are many [[smrtis|smṛtis]] dealing with metaphysical topics, great effort was made for the refutation of [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] and Yoga only, since the two systems are well-known in the world as means of securing the highest goal of man, as they have been accepted by [[śistas|śiṣṭas]] (respectable and learned people) and are supported by Vedic indications in their favour (as in [[svetāśvatara|Śvetāśvatara]] Up. ’tat-kāraṇam [[sānkhyayogabhi pannam’|sāṅkhyayogābhipannam’]] VI. 13). It would be shown later on that Manu and Devala rely on and propound ([[somo|some]]) Sāṅkhya doctrines.

[[P1353]]It would not be irrelevant to say a few words about the origin and development of the [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] system. This (rise of [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]]) is one of the most difficult problems in Indian [[philo sophy.|philosophy.]] A good many works and papers have been written on the [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] system.2 As to what the original Sāṅkhya teaching revised in the Sāṅkhyakārikā of [[fśvarakrsna|Īśvarakṛṣṇa]] was, no generally accepted answer can be given. From the 5th century [[A, D.|A. D.]] at least the [[Saṅkhyakārikā|Sāṅkhyakārikā]] of [[Iśvarakrsna|Īśvarakṛṣṇa]] has been [[re garded|regarded]] as the foremost exponent of the [[Sārkhya.|Sāṅkhya.]] The [[Saṅkhya kārikā|Sāṅkhyakārikā]] and a commentary on it were translated into Chinese about 546 A. D. by [[Paramartha,|Paramārtha,]] originally a brāhmaṇa of Bhāradvāja gotra and a [[śramaña|śramaṇa]] from [[Ujjayini|Ujjayinī]] (vide BEFEO, 1904, p. 60). [[Saṅkarācārya|Śaṅkarācārya]] on V. S. [[I, 4, 11|I. 4. 11]] quotes the whole of the 3rd [[perse|verse]] of the [[Sāökbyakārikā|Sāṅkhyakārikā]] and the first quarter of it on V. S. I. 4. 8. But the Sāṅkhya system, it appears, went through several phases. Chinese sources say that there were eighteen [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] schools (p. 2 of Johnston’s [[“Early Saṅkhya,"|“Early Sāṅkhya,”]] quoting Takakusu in BEFEO, 1904, p. 58).

Before proceeding further the most characteristic and fundamental conceptions of the standard Sāṅkhya must be set out. The most fundamental conception is that there are two entirely distinct [[esgences|essences]] existing from eternity, viz. [[praksti|prakṛti]] (i. e. nature or matter) also called pradhāna and avyakta, and puruṣa (soul or spirit, the knowing subject). The 2nd fundamental conception is that puruṣas are many. Another most [[characteri. stio|characteristic]] feature of the [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] system is the doctrine of three8 guṇas (factors) called sattva (light, intelligent), rajas (energetic, active, domineering), tamas (coarse, inert, enveloping, dark). [[Pradhaps|Pradhāna]] or prakṛti or Avyakta is said to be constituted by the three guṇas (triguṇātmaka) when they are in equilibrium ([[Sattvarajastamasām sāmyāvasthā|Sattvarajastamasāṁ sāmyāvasthā]]). The [[Sārkbya|Sāṅkhya]] analysed all physical and mental phenomena. The lowest factor is the heavy impenetrable matter and coarse foolish impulses. They are called tamas ([[desoribed|described]] as guru, heavy and [[varanaka,|varaṇaka,]] enveloping). Then there is the factor of constant change in the physical world as well as in the mental. This is called [[rajag|rajas]] (cala, changing and [[upastambhaka,|upaṣṭambhaka,]] exciting, as the [[8.|S.]] [[Karika|Kārikā]] puts it). The third factor or element in the world is the varied operation of consciousness that leads to knowledge and [[experi ence,|experience,]] which is called Sattva (laghu, light as opposed to the coarse, merely material objects and prakāśaka, luminous, as contrasted with tamas). These three factors or elements combine in various proportions and make up the evolving universe. The three are called [[gupas|guṇas]] from several points of view viz. they are qualities, they also bind as with a rope the Puruṣa to [[samsāra.|saṁsāra.]] The basis of the universe is to be found in the guṇas. Pradhāna

[[P1358]]is not different from the guṇas, but it is only the name of the primordial matter before evolution starts. [[Praksti|Prakṛti]] being anādi and eternal, the [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] system did not postulate God [[48|as]] creator, and held God to be a [[superfuity.|superfluity.]] The [[Sinkhya|Sāṅkhya]] propounds a theory of cosmic evolution which is practically as reasonable as any modern theory of evolution. It is probably the earliest attempt to furnish an answer from reason alone to the questions about the origin of the world, about the nature of man and his relation to the physical world and man’s future destiny. In the nineteenth century mind and matter were held to be entirely [[dif. eront|different]] and atoms were supposed to be indivisible. Modern physics [[bas|has]] reduced matter to energy but the nature of this last also is a mystery. Under the [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] [[systom,|system,]] [[puruṣu|puruṣa]] or [[prakpli|prakṛti]] or both serve no religious purpose. There is no definite or convincing answer as to why puruṣa becomes entangled in the web of [[praksti,|prakṛti.]] The [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] simply says that as a result of non-discrimination puruṣa [[somebow|somehow]] becomes entangled. The Vedāntasūtra [[rofera|refers]] to pradhāna as [[amārta|smārta]] in I. 2. 19 and as [[anumānika’|‘ānumānika’]] in I. 4. 1. From Prakṛti evolves [[mahūn|mahān]] (i. [[e,|e.]] buddhi, consciousness) from which arises ahaṅkāra, from which evolve on the one hand the five tanmātras (subtle elements, viz. [[sabda, sparsa, gandba,|śabda, sparśa, gandha,]] rasa, rūpa) and on the other hand manas (mind) and the [[ton|ten]] indriyas (sense organs). From the five subtle elements are evolved the five great elements, earth, water, tejas, vāyu and [[akasa ( other ).|ākāśa (ether).]] These are twentyfour9 tattvas (categories) and puruṣa is 25th. Pradhāna is distinct from puruṣa and [[sorves|serves]] the purpose of puruṣa (who is passive and a sākṣī) who is distinct from the [[bvolutos|evolutes]] of prakṛti and is bhoktṛ (and not kartṛ). The [[Sāökhya|Sāṅkhya]]10 does not admit God. [[PrakȚti|Prakṛti]] and puruṣa come together in order that the latter may see its working; just as a blind man and a [[orip ple|cripple]] (a lame man) may come together (and the blind man may carry the lame man on his shoulder, the latter showing the way and both may reach their destination by this joint effort).11 When [[P1359]]puruṣa realizes the difference between himself12 and the guṇas constituting [[Prakrti|Prakṛti]] he is liberated. Both [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] and Yoga treat the external world as real. They both postulate plurality of souls ([[purugas),|(puruṣas),]] that are eternal and unchanging. This latter is one of the most crucial differences between [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] and advaita Vedānta. It is not necessary for our purpose in this chapter to go into greater details. One more doctrine of the [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] is called satkāryavāda i. e. the theory that the effect already exists in the cause and does not arise from nothing ([[8.|S.]] Kārikā, verse 9); compare [[Chan.|Chān.]] Up. VI. 2. 2 ([[kathamagataḥ|kathamasataḥ]] sajjāyeta) and [[Gita I.|Gītā II.]] 16 (nāsato vidyate bhāvo).

It is difficult to assign a definite date to the [[Sāṅkhya kārikā.|Sāṅkhyakārikā.]] As Paramārtha [[treuslated|translated]] the [[Karikā|Kārikā]] as well as [[com mentary|commentary]] on it into Chinese in about 546 A. D. the [[Karikā|Kārikā]] cannot in any case be placed later than about 250–300 A. D. It may be earlier by a few centuries. Umbeka in his [[common tary|commentary]] on the Ślokavārtika of Kumārila mentions an author called [[Madhava|Mādhava]] as ‘Sāṅkhyanāyaka’ and Yuan Chwang also refers to [[&|a]] [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] teacher called Mādhava. Dr. Raghavan in ‘Sarūpa-Bhāratī’ (pp. 162–164) shows that Mādhava was really [[&|a]] destructive critic of [[Sāökhya,|Sāṅkhya,]] that the proper reading is [[’*Saṅkhyanāśaka-Madhava’|‘Sāṅkhyanāśaka-Mādhava’]] and that he was probably earlier than even [[Dinnāga|Diṅnāga]] and [[Dharmarkirti|Dharmakīrti]] (i. e. earlier than 500 A. D.).

[[Saṅkarācārya|Śaṅkarācārya]] in the passage quoted in n. 2208 remarks that certain Upaniṣad passages may be relied upon as apparently supporting the [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] system. Some of the Upaniṣad passages that either foreshadow [[Bankhya dootrines|Sāṅkhya doctrines]] or employ words that have technical meanings in the [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] system would be [[P1360]]cited.13 But one passage from the Atharvaveda X. 8. 43 deserves notice, viz. ’the knowers of Brahma know that yakṣa endowed with ātman which abides in the lotus with nine gates (the physical body) covered by three guṇas.’ With this may be compared Śv. Up. III. 18 and [[Gita|Gītā]] V. 13 (navadvāre pure [[debi).|dehī).]] The [[Mundaka|Muṇḍaka]] Up. states ‘From Him are born prāṇa, mind, all indriyas, the five elements, and sky, wind, jyotis ([[tejas ),|tejas),]] waters and the earth.’ The Kaṭha Up. refers to indriyas, the objects of senses, mind, buddhi, mahān, avyakta, puruṣa as a rising series, similar to that of the [[Sāökbya|Sāṅkhya]] with one exception viz. the Up. does not mention ahaṅkāra and holds that buddhi and mahān are different, while the official Sāṅkhya identifies them. It is, therefore, clear that the evolution in these two Upaniṣads is very similar to the theory of evolution stated by the classical Sāṅkhya, the only difference being that the Upaniṣads postulate a supreme Creator (that produces the cosmos) while Sāṅkhya drops the creator and accepts the evolutionary series only. On V. S. I. 2. 12 [[Saṅkarācārya|Śaṅkarācārya]] [[citos|cites]] the verse ‘dvā suparṇā sayujā’ (which is Muṇḍaka Up. III. 1. 1, Śv. Up. III. 1 and also Ṛg. I. 164. 20) and explains it, as referring to the individual self (jīva) and the Highest Spirit (paramātman). The [[ācārys|ācārya]] then mentions the argument of a predecessor who relies upon [[Paingirakasys brāhmaṇa|Paiṅgirahasyabrāhmaṇa]] where the latter half of the verse is explained as mentioning sattva (buddhi) and [[kṣetrajñu (. e.|kṣetrajña (i. e.]] the individual self). This indicates that some took that verse as putting forward Sāṅkhya ideas. The Kaṭha Up. III. 4 states that the description of the individual soul as the enjoyer ([[bhoktp )|bhoktṛ)]] results [[P1361]]from the union of the ātman with organs of [[senge|sense]] and the mind. The [[Svetaśvataropaniṣad|Śvetāśvataropaniṣad]] expressly14 refers to Sāṅkhya and Yoga (in VI. 13) and says that ‘on realizing that cause which can be apprehended by means of the study of Sāṅkhya and Yoga he is released from all bonds’. That Upaniṣad is full of words that are frequently employed in the [[Sāökhya|Sāṅkhya]] system such as ‘avyakta’ (I. 8), guṇa (V. 7 ‘sa viśvarūpas-triguṇaḥ’ and VI. 2, 4 and 16), jña ([[v. 2,|V. 2,]] VI. 17), prakṛti ([[māyām tu praktim vidyāt’|‘māyāṁ tu prakṛtiṁ vidyāt’]] IV. 10), puruṣa (I. 2, III. 12, 13, [[1V.7).|IV. 7),]] pradhāna (I. 10, VI. 10 and 16), [[linga|liṅga]] (I. 13, VI. 9). The Śv. Up. (VI. 11) describes the one God as [[sākṣi cetā kovalo nirguṇas-ca”.|‘sākṣī cetā kevalo nirguṇaś-ca’.]] The Sāṅkhya denies God and transfers these epithets to the Puruṣa, who in his true essence is, according to the Sāṅkhya, merely a passive witness, pure intelligence, unaffected by guṇas. The Praśna Up. (IV. 8) speaks of the five elements and their [[matras|mātrās]] ([[prthivi ca prtbivimātrā ca &c),|(pṛthivī ca pṛthivīmātrā ca &c),]] the ten indriyas and their objects, manas, buddhi, ahaṅkāra &c. The Sāṅkhyas rely on the [[verde|verse]] [[‘ajāmekām’(Śv.|‘ajām-ekām’ (Śv.]] Up. The five elements ([[mahābhutani)|(mahābhūtāni)]] are mentioned in the Ait. Up. (III. 3), Praśna VI. 4 and the five special qualities of these five in Kaṭha Up. (III. 15).

In view of the fact that the word ‘Sāṅkhya’ occurs in the Śv. Up. and that the Kaṭha and Muṇḍaka have some doctrines similar to the Sāṅkhya system and the Śvetāśvatara employs many words that occur frequently in the works on Sāṅkhya, the question arises—what is the relation of the Sāṅkhya to the Upaniṣads. There are three possible views, (1) that [[Upe nizad|Upaniṣad]] thought and [[Sāṅkbya|Sāṅkhya]] made parallel progress, (2) that [[Sinkhya|Sāṅkhya]] adopted and expanded some of the germinal ideas [[P1363]]of the [[Upaniṣada,|Upaniṣads,]] (3) that some Upaniṣads borrowed from the Sāṅkhya. It is not possible to discuss these questions here for reasons of space and relevance. The present author holds that it is the Sāṅkhya that based itself on the thoughts of the [[Upanigads,|Upaniṣads,]] particularly when the older Upaniṣads like the Bṛ. Up. and Chāndogya hardly exhibit any traces of Sāṅkhya doctrines or technique, when Sāṅkhya [[reference|references]] are found only in Kaṭha, Muṇḍaka, Śvetāśvatara, Praśna (which are held to be later than Chān. Up. and Bṛ. Up. by modern scholarship) and when there is hardly any work or writer of purely Sāṅkhya doctrines that can be assigned to a period even a few centuries earlier than the Christian era and when no one can place the principal Upaniṣads (about twelve, including the Śvetāśvatara) later than 300 [[B.O.,|B. C.,]] since even the Śv. Up. is referred to as ‘Śruti’ in [[V, s.|V. S.]] I. 4. 8, II. 3. 22. Garbe (in [[‘Die Sāökhya Philosophie’|‘Die Sāṅkhya Philosophie’]] pp. 3 ff) denies that Sāṅkhya underwent any [[essen tial|essential]] modifications in its long history. Jacobi does not agree, holding that Sāṅkhya originated out of a common cultural and philosophical heritage and Oldenberg [[goes|sees]] the origin of [[Sāökhya|Sāṅkhya]] in the Kaṭha and Śv. Up. and holds that epic Sāṅkhya is an independent development ([[‘Die|(‘Die]] Lehre der Upanishaden [[uöd|und]] die [[Anfānge|Anfänge]] des Buddhismus’, 1915, pp. 206 ff). Sāṅkhya and Yoga were known to [[Kautilya|Kauṭilya]] ([[Saṅkhyam Yogo Lokāyatam cetyanyikṣiki’ Arthaśāstrā|(‘Sāṅkhyaṁ Yogo Lokāyataṁ cetyānvīkṣikī’ Arthaśāstra]] I. 2. p. 6.). Therefore, we may say that Sāṅkhya as a philosophical school started at least before the 4th century [[B. O.|B. C.]]

We should now turn to Sanskrit works other than the Upaniṣads for seeing the origins of the Sāṅkhya system. First comes the Mahābhārata.

[[Purāpas|Purāṇas]] contain lengthy disquisitions on the [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] doctrines. For example, the [[Viṣṇupurana|Viṣṇupurāṇa]] I. 2. 19-23, 25-62, [[VI.4.13.15, 17,32-40|VI. 4. 13-15, 17, 32-40]] (in all about 66 verses) are full of [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] doctrines and are [[all.quoted in the Kștyakalpataru (Mokṣakāṇda|all quoted in the Kṛtyakalpataru (Mokṣakāṇḍa]] pp. 102-108). But that [[Purana|Purāṇa]] emphasizes that [[Paramatman (called Vispu)|Paramātman (called Viṣṇu)]] is the support of all tattvas. In [[Viṣṇupurana|Viṣṇupurāṇa]] I. 2. 22-23 it is asserted59[[ 2766|]] that brahmavādins who are proficient in Vedic doctrines recite (a verse) that expounds [[pradhana,|pradhāna,]] (and state) ’there was then (before creation) neither day nor night, no sky nor earth, neither darkness nor light, there was [[only one male brahma that was evolved as pradbāna|only one male brahma that was evolved as pradhāna]] and that could not be apprehended by the ear (and other indriyas) or by buddhi. Verses 39-40 (of [[Viṣṇupurana|Viṣṇupurāṇa]] VI. 4) assert that both60[[ 2267|]] [[praksti|prakṛti]] and puruṣa become dissolved in the Highest Self who is called Viṣṇu in Vedas and [[Vedantas ( Upanisads).|Vedāntas (Upaniṣads).]]

Many other Purāṇas [[deyote|devote]] a great deal of space to the elucidation of [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] doctrines and terminology. [[Considerar tions|Considerations]] of space preclude any statement beyond references to

[[P1383]] [[ohapters|chapters]] and verses of some Purāṇas and the [[oitation of a fow|citation of a few]] striking or informative verses. Matsya (3. 14-29) begins with [[praksti,|prakṛti,]] guṇas and all the twenty-five tattvas, remarks that the three gods, [[Brahma,|Brahmā,]] Viṣṇu, [[Mahesvara,|Maheśvara,]] though one, arose from the guṇas being put into activity and winds up by saying that [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] is full of enumerations and was proclaimed by Kapila and [[others.n68|others.]]61 The [[Brahmapurana|Brahmapurāṇa]] (1. 33-35, 33. 3-4, [[242,|242.]] 67-70, 76-85) recounts the tattvas from prakṛti to [[ksetrajfia|kṣetrajña]] and remarks at the end that the soul becomes kevala (free from all contacts and liberated) when he realizes the truth about himself. The Padmapurāṇa in several places dilates upon the tattvas of the [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] such as in [[Patalakhanda 85, 11-18, Srstikhanda,|Pātālakhaṇḍa 85. 11-18, Sṛṣṭikhaṇḍa,]] chap. 2. 88-103, chap. 15. 177-187, which closely follow the [[8. Kārikā|S. Kārikā]] verses. The Kūrmapurāṇa (I. 4. [[13–35)|13-35)]] states that Maheśvara enters prakṛti and puruṣa, activates them and sets out Sāṅkhya ideas and in II. 7. 21-26 it refers to the twenty-four tattvas and the state of equilibrium [[(sāmyavasthā).|(sāmyāvasthā).]] The [[Markandeya.purana|Mārkaṇḍeya-purāṇa]] in chap. 42. 32-62 treats of creation with [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] terminology. Three verses of the [[Brahmanda|Brahmāṇḍa]] IV. 3. 37-46 referring to the threefold bondage (bandha) are quoted by the [[Kștyakalpataru|Kṛtyakalpataru]] (mokṣa) p. 124. Similarly, [[Brahmanda (II. 32.71-76) employ|Brahmāṇḍa (II. 32. 71-76) employs]] the [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] terminology of mahat, [[ahaikara &o.|ahaṅkāra &c.]] Prof. Das Gupta in ‘Indian Philosophy’ vol. IV. pp. 24-48 deals at some [[longth|length]] with Kapila’s philosophy in the Bhāgavatapurāṇa and Sri Siddhesvara Bhattacharya contributes a paper ‘a critique of the [[Bhagavata’|Bhāgavata’]] in JBRS [[for. 1950|for 1950]] pp. 9–50 in which he severely criticizes Prof. Das Gupta.

[[P1384]] Vide [[Bhagavatapurāṇa IV. 26.|Bhāgavatapurāṇa III. 26.]] The [[Varābapurāṇa|Varāhapurāṇa]] (B. I. ed. of 1893) narrates the story of king Aśvaśiras to whom Kapila and [[Jaigiṣavya (|Jaigīṣavya (]]the prince of Yogins) came after he had performed the [[avabhrtha|avabhṛtha]] bath and was surrounded by brāhmaṇas and who asked them how the highest [[Nārāyana|Nārāyaṇa]] was to be worshipped, when the two told him that they were themselves [[Nārāyana.|Nārāyaṇa.]] On his doubting these words of theirs Kapila assumed the form of [[Vispu|Viṣṇu]] and [[Jaigisavya|Jaigīṣavya]] of [[Garuda|Garuḍa]] and after other similar wonders, the king requested that his doubt whether Mokṣa is attained by him who follows karmamārga or [[jñānamarga|jñānamārga]] be resolved. The same Purāṇa in chap. 25 (verses 1-5) declares that [[aryakta,|avyakta,]] the first tattva, made up of three (guṇas) arose from the highest Puruṣa, from the union of the two [[ahankāra|ahaṅkāra]] was produced also called mahat, that Puruṣa is named [[Viśṇu|Viṣṇu]] or Śiva while avyakta is called Umā, [[Devi|Devī]] or [[Sri|Śrī]] and Guha (Kārtikeya) is [[abankāra.|ahaṅkāra.]] The [[Bhagavatapurāṇa|Bhāgavatapurāṇa]] (I. 3. 10) mentions Kapila as the fifth avatāra of [[Visou|Viṣṇu]] (vide above p. 1373) and (in X. 8. 37-38) states that when Yaśodā asked the child [[Krspa|Kṛṣṇa]] to open his mouth she saw inside the [[inouth|mouth]] the whole world, the five elements, the indriyas, mind, mātrās and the three guṇas. Even great poets like Kālidāsa and Bāṇa are fond of utilising Sāṅkhya doctrines and terminology. For example, vide Kumārasambhava II. 4 (Guṇatrayavibhāgāya &c.), [[Raghu vamsa|Raghu-vaṁśa]] X. 38 [[(angidām tamagovobhau gunau prathamamadhya mau),|(aṅgināṁ tamasovobhau guṇau prathamamadhyamau),]] [[Raghuvamsa|Raghuvaṁśa]] VIII. 21 [[(Raghurapyajayad-guna-trayam prakstistham &c.);|(Raghurapyajayad-guṇa-trayam prakṛtisthaṁ &c.);]] [[Kādambari,|Kādambarī,]] very first verse [[(rajojuse janmani…triguṇātmane Damaḥ).|(rajojuṣe janmani…triguṇātmane namaḥ).]]

Even the Tantras were influenced by the [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] system as the [[Sāradātilaka|Śāradātilaka]] shows (vide above p. 1061).
