[[P887]]CHAPTER XXIII

Brief Notes on individual Purāṇas and Upapurāṇas

Agnipurāṇa—‘Present Agni’ (by H.) in [[L.|I.]] H. Q. vol. [[Xi|XII]] pp. 683-691; ‘Studies in genuine [[Agneya|Āgneya]] alias Vahnipurāṇa’ by H. in ‘Our Heritage’ vol. I part 2 pp. 209–245 and vol. II part 1 pp. 76-109; ‘Discovery of genuine [[Āgneyapurāṇa ‘|Āgneyapurāṇa’]] by [[E.|H.]] in J. O. [[L|I.]] (Baroda) vol. V (1956) pp. 411-416 (shows that present Agni published by [[Anan.|Ānan.]] Press is not the original [[Purana,|Purāṇa,]] that this was compiled late, while the original [[Agneya|Āgneya]] or Vahni is not yet printed); the Dānasāgara p. 7. verse 63 speaks of an Āgneya which it has discarded apart from one which it has utilized (p. [[% Vorso|2 verse]] 11). As usual with most Purāṇas, the printed [[Agneya|Āgneya]] glorifies itself by saying (in chap. 272. 13 and 17) that in that Mahāpurāṇa Hari resides in the form of [[diffe rent|different]] lores and the Āgneya is a great purāṇa full of Veda and all vidyās.

[[Adipurāṇa|Ādipurāṇa]] (U.)—B. V. [[VOL|vol.]] VI (1945) pp. 60-73 (H. [[posta lates|postulates]] an earlier and a later version). Vāyu 104.7 mentions an [[Adika|Ādika]] among the 18 [[prinoipal|principal]] purāṇas (including the [[Brahma|Brāhma]]). Alberuni’s list (which mixes up Purāṇas and Upapurāṇas) mentions an [[Adipurāpa;|Ādipurāṇa;]] an [[Adipurāṇa|Ādipurāṇa]] is published by the [[Venk. Preas|Veṅk. Press]] in 29 chapters. H. in ‘Studies &c.’ vol. I pp. 279-303 deals at [[longth|length]] with this and tries to establish that there was an earlier [[Adi|Ādi]] Purāṇa of which no [[msg.|mss.]] are yet available (p. 211) and holds that it is to be dated between [[1203|1200]] to 1525 [[AD.|A. D.]] (‘Studies’ p. 288). [[Adi|Ādi]] and Ādya mean the same thing. But Kullūka on Manu II. 54 quotes some verses from [[Adi|Ādi]] which are [[asoribed|ascribed]] to [[Brihma|Brāhma]] by [[G, R.|G. R.]] p. 314. The digests make a [[con fusion|confusion]] between [[Adi|Ādi]] and [[Adityapurāṇa.|Ādityapurāṇa.]] Vide H. in ‘Studies’ vol. I. pp. 302–303. The printed one is a late compilation and none of the verses quoted by [[Lakṣmidhara|Lakṣmīdhara]] and [[Apararka|Aparārka]] occur therein (vide ‘Studies’ vol. I pp. 286–289).

[[Adityapurūna|Ādityapurāṇa]]—Mentioned by Matsya 53.62 as U. and [[Al beruni|Alberuni]] (Sachau I. p. 130, 229, 248); quoted by [[Krbyakalpataru|Kṛtyakalpataru]] on Rājadharma (about 2 verses), Dāna (about 125), [[Sraddha|Śrāddha]] (about 20), Vrata (about 22); the Sm. [[O.|C.]] quotes many verses on [[Ahnika|Āhnika]] and [[Śrāddba|Śrāddha]] from both [[Adi|Ādi]] and [[Aditya|Āditya]] and quotes both [[P888]]separately on ‘śauca’ on the [[game|same]] page (I p. 94). Aparārka also quotes many verses from both [[Adi|Ādi]] and [[Aditya.|Āditya.]] The [[Dana sāgara|Dānasāgara]] quotes about forty verses from both the [[Adi|Ādi]] and [[Aditya|Āditya]] purāṇas.

Ekāmra (a work of Orissa):—H in P. O. vol. 16 pp. 70-76 and ‘Studies &c.’ vol. I. p. 341 (assigns it to 10th or 11th [[contury|century]] A. D.)

[[Kalikū:|Kālikā:—]](an U. in 93 chapters pr. by [[Vank, Prosa,|Veṅk. Press,]] Bombay); vide H. in A.B.O.R.I. vol. XXII. pp. 1-23; Sharma in I. H. XII. pp. 331–360 (shows that there are three separate recensions of the [[Purana|Purāṇa]]). C. [[IL|II.]] p. 442 quotes one verse from it on śrāddha. The Dānasāgara quotes many verses from it. The [[Venk.|Veṅk.]] ed. [[montions|mentions]] Viṣṇudharmottara in chap. 91. 70 and 92. 21; the extant [[Kalikā|Kālikā]] would have to be placed before 1000 A. D. Vide p. 809 n 1317 above for the Kālikā saying that the howl of a female jackal is auspicious.

H. in PRHR (p. 126) states that the Uttara-kāṇda (of the Padma) is later than 900 A. D. and earlier than 1500 A. D. One remarkable fact is that hundreds of verses are common to Matsya and Padma and some writers like Hemādri quote long extracts from the Padma, which others quote from the Matsya. In view of the vast smṛti material in the Matsya and the fact that thousands of verses therefrom [[aro|are]] quoted in the medieval nibandhas the present writer is inclined to hold that it is the Padma that is the borrower. There are no materials to assign a definite date for the borrowing, but it is likely that it was before 1000 A. D. Padma (IV. 102. 40-41 and IV. 110[[|,]] 483) mentions the Kūrma (and a verse is quoted from it) and IV. 5. 32-43 contain such alaṅkāras as Śleṣa and Parisaṅkhyā. The Kalpataru quotes from Padma about 55 verses on vrata, 50 on [[Diyatakāla,|Niyatakāla,]] 50 on [[dana,|dāna,]] [[1%|12]] on [[tirtha,|tīrtha,]] only a verse here and there on other [[kāndes.|kāṇḍas.]] Aparārka quotes only about 12 verses from it, the [[Smsticandrikā|Smṛticandrikā]] only about 25. In the Ānandāśrama edition of Padma there are 48452 verses and 628 chapters. It speaks of the Aśvattha as Bodhisattva ([[Sșstikhanda|Sṛṣṭikhaṇḍa]] 55. 16) and mentions a place called Vanasthala in Gurjaradeśa (II. 51. 36-37).

[[Brahmapurana|Brahmapurāṇa]]—The one printed at the Ānandāśrama [[appearg|appears]] to be a late compilation. Vide H. on ‘Apocryphal Brahmapurāṇa’ in I. [[O.|C.]] II. pp. 235-245 and PRHR pp. 145-157[[|,]] H. states that numerous quotations from the Brahma occurring in the works of Jīmūtavāhana, Aparārka, Ballālasena, [[Deyanna bhatta|Devaṇṇabhaṭṭa]] and Haradatta are not found in the printed Brahma, that the current Brahma borrows chapters from the Mahābhārata, Viṣṇu, Vāyu and Mārkaṇḍeya and that the present [[Brahma.|Brahma]] [[P894]]was composed between 10th century and 12th century A. D. H. Otto [[Sobrader|Schrader]] states that chapters 236-244 of the present Brahma dealing with Sāṅkhya and Yoga are borrowed from the Mahābhārata ([[L.C.|I. C.]] vol. II[[|,]] pp. 592-93). The Dānasāgara refers to two Brahmapurāṇas one of which it did not utilize (p. 7 verse 63). The Kalpataru quotes at least about 1500 verses from the [[Brahmapurāpa|Brahmapurāṇa]] (600 in [[niyalakūla|niyatakāla]] section, 66 on [[tirtha,|tīrtha,]] 60 on mokṣa, 78 on rājadharma, 21 on [[gṭhastha,|gṛhastha,]] 20 on vyavahāra, [[versos 15|15]] on vrata, 15 on [[brabmacarin|brahmacārin]] of which the editor has traced only 9 in the printed text). The Kalpataru on śrāddha quotes [[soveral|several]] hundred verses from the Brahma, the number of quotations being larger than the quotations from any other Purāṇa, the next being Vāyu and Matsya. Brahmāṇḍa ([[pub,|pub.]] by [[Venk. Pregs).|Veṅk. Press).]] It is divided into four pādas (I. 1. [[33-39)|38-39)]] viz. Prakriyā (5 chapters), Anuṣaṅga (33 chap.), Upodghāta (74 chap.), Upasaṃhāra (4 chap.) followed by Lalitopākhyāna in 40 chapters. The Kūrma expressly8 states that the Brahmāṇḍa was narrated to the sages engaged in a sattra in the Naimiṣa forest and the Skanda (Prabhāsa-khaṇḍa 2. 8-9) states there was formerly only one Purāṇa called Brahmāṇḍa containing one hundred crores of verses and that later it was distributed into 18 parts. It was probably composed near the rise of the Godāvarī, since it says that that part of the country towards the northern ranges of the [[Sabya|Sahya]] mountain where the Godāvarī rises is the most charming in the whole world and that there a city called Govardhana was [[foun ded|founded]] by [[Rāma. 1421|Rāma.]]9 In the first two parts it deals with the subjects of creation, the geography of the earth and of Bhāratavarṣa, and the manvantaras, pupils of Vyāsa, the distribution of the Veda [[Sakhās|Śākhās]] &c. The third section is the longest and after mentioning Vaivasvata Manvantara and, after dilating upon the creation of gods, asuras, gandharvas, sages and their progeny, it deals at length with all aspects of śrāddha (in chap. 9-20 and 879 verses), describes [[Parafurāmā’s|Paraśurāma’s]] austerities, his securing of weapons, his[[|,]] slaughter of Kārtavīrya and kṣatriyas, filling five [[lakos|lakes]] with their blood (in chap. 21-47 and over 1550 verses); then follow in chapters 48-57 the story of Sagara and the bringing down of the Ganges by Bhagīratha, the protection of Gokarṇa from the sea, and the story of Śūrpāraka (chap. 57 and 58), the dynasties of the solar and lunar race (chap. [[598);|59 ff);]] then it speaks of Dhanvantari receiving from Bhāradvāja Āyurveda in eight [[angas;|aṅgas;]] in part IV. it refers to Manus, jñāna, karma, mokṣa &c.

[[4%,|42,]] the twelve jyotirliṅgas are mentioned, are spoken of as avatāras of Rudra and described; in [[Kotirudrasam.,|Koṭirudrasaṃh.,]] chap. 35 one thousand names of Śiva are set out; in [[Kailāgasam.,|Kailāsasaṃh.,]] chap. 5 maṇḍala in pūjā is described; in chap. 7 verses 5–26 various Mudrās and Nyāsas are provided for; in the [[Rudrasa seotion,|Rudrasaṃ. section,]] [[Parvati,|Pārvatī,]] there is a close resemblance between this purāṇa and the Kumārasambhava as noted below.

[[śkanda|Skanda]]—This is the most extensive of Purāṇas and [[pogos|poses]] perplexing problems. It is found in two forms, one being divided into seven khaṇḍas, viz. Māheśvara, Vaiṣṇava, Brāhma, Kāśī, Āvantya, Nāgara and [[Prabhāga,|Prabhāsa,]] the other being divided into six saṃhitās, viz. Sanatkumāra, Sūta, [[Sārkarl,|Śāṅkarī,]] Vaiṣṇavī, Brāhmī and Saura. The Skanda in seven khaṇḍas has been published by the [[Venk,|Veṅk.]] Press and the [[Sūtāsambitā|Sūtasaṃhitā]] with the commentary of Mādhavācārya has been published by the [[Anan. Prese,|Ānan. Press,]] Poona. The extent of the Skanda is variously given as 81000 ślokas, at 100000 ślokas (vide PRHR p. 158), at 86000 (in PRHR p. 159). The god Skanda does not figure prominently in this Purāṇa named after him. The Skanda is named in the Padma V. 59. 2. Skanda I. 2. 6. 79 is almost in the same words as [[Kirātārjuniya 1415|Kirātārjunīya]]36 (II. 30 sahasā vidadhīta na kriyām). Skanda, Kāśīkhaṇḍa 24 (8 ff) is full of [[Sloṣa|Śleṣa]] and Parisaṅkhyā in the style of Bāṇa as in ‘yatra kṣa[[P912]][[papakā eva drøyante|paṇakā eva dṛśyante]] maladhāriṇaḥ’ (verse 21) or ‘vibhramo yatra nārīṣu na vidvatsu [[oa karbicit’|ca karhicit’]] ([[verge|verse]] 9). [[Natyaveda|Nāṭyaveda]] and [[Artha gāstras|Arthaśāstras]] are mentioned in Kāśīkhaṇḍa (Pūrvārdha 7. 4-5), [[Dhan vantari|Dhanvantari]] and Caraka on [[medioine|medicine]] are mentioned in Kāśīkhaṇḍa ([[Pārvārdha|Pūrvārdha]] 1. 71); the word [[Jhotinga|Jhoṭiṅga]] occurs in Kāśīkhaṇḍa 72. 74 ([[Jhotingā rākṣasāḥ krūrāḥ).|Jhoṭiṅgā rākṣasāḥ krūrāḥ).]] Skanda is quoted on topics of Dharmaśāstra in early commentaries and digests. The [[Mit|Mit.]] on Yāj. [[11.|II.]] 290 mentions it in connection with the status of veśyās ([[courtozans).|courtezans).]] Kalpataru on vrata quotes only 15 [[vorges|verses]] from it, Kalpataru on [[tirtha|tīrtha]] (pp. 36-39, 32, 46, 130-135) quotes 92 verses from it, on dāna only 44, on niyatakāla 63 verses, 18 verses on Rājadharma (on Kaumudīmahotsava), only 4 in [[śrāddhakanda|śrāddhakāṇḍa]] and 3 in [[gļhasthakāṇda.|gṛhasthakāṇḍa.]] Aparārka37 quotes only 19 verses from it; one quotation indicates Tāntrik influence (vide note). The Dānasāgara cites 48 verses on [[dana|dāna]] from it and the Sm. [[O.|C.]] only 23 in all. Considering the colossal figure of ślokas in the Skanda it must be said that it is rather sparingly quoted in the Dharmaśāstra works. A verse in it seems to echo the very words of Kālidāsa and quotes the view of [[Dovala.14460|Devala.]]38 In such a huge work interpolations could easily be made. So it is difficult to assign a definite date to it. A ms. of the Skanda in the Nepal Durbar Library is written in [[charac tors|characters]] which belong to the 7th century A. D. according to Haraprasad [[Shastri|Śāstrī]] (vide [[Cat,|Cat.]] of Nepal Palm-leaf [[mass.|mss.]] p. LII.)

It would be not far from the truth to say that the Skanda cannot be placed earlier than the 7th century A. D. and not later than 9th century [[A, D,|A. D.]] on the evidence so far available.
