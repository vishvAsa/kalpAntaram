SECTION IX

CHAPTER XXXIV

COSMOLOGY

All writers on Dharmaśāstra are agreed on the existence of God. They rarely enter upon the task of adducing arguments for the existence of God. Christian theologians for hundreds of years put forward various arguments pointing to the existence of God. They are briefly summarised in William [[James ‘Varieties|James’ ‘Varieties]] of religious experience’ p. 437 (ed. of 1920). The cosmological argument reasons from the ordered [[univers|universe]] to a First Cause which must be conceived as God who must at least possess whatever perfection the universe itself contains. The argument from purpose or design (teleological argument) [[based|bases]] itself on the evidence of purpose or design in Nature and concludes that the First Cause (God) must be a creative intellect or mind. Then there are other arguments also such as the ‘moral [[argument’(viz.|argument’ (viz.]] the moral law presupposes a law-giver), the argument ex consensu gentium [[(vir,|(viz.]] there is widespread belief in God all over the world and it should carry weight).1

[[P1484]] The Upaniṣads speak of the Absolute brahman as the creator, preserver and destroyer of the [[bhulas|bhūtas]] (beings or elements or both). For example, the Tai. Up. III. [[1(Bhrgu|1 (Bhṛgu]]2 is [[instruoted ty|instructed by]] his father [[Varupa)|Varuṇa)]] [[desire|‘desire]] to know that from which all [[bhutas|bhūtas]] spring, by which, after being created, they live (are sustained) and to which they return and are absorbed therein ; that is brahma’. This is the basic text on which V. S. I. [[1.2|1. 2]] ([[jappādyasya|janmādyasya]] yataḥ) is based. This means ‘from which the creation (sustenance and dissolution) of this (world ) proceed’ (that is brahma). Another passage of the Tai. Up. (II. 1) states that ‘from this Ātman [[Akāśa|Ākāśa]] [[sprang.|sprang,]] from ākāśa [[Vayu,|Vāyu,]] from Vāyu Agni, from [[Agoi|Agni]] waters, from waters the earth, from earth plants, from plants food, from food man’. A passage of the Chān. Up. states3 [[all|‘all]] this, indeed, is brahma; a man, cultivating restraint of the mind, should meditate on that (world) [[As|as]] springing from, ending in and breathing on account of that (brahma)’. This is the basis of V. S. I. 2. 1 [[( sarvatra prasiddho padeśāt).|(sarvatra prasiddhopadeśāt).]] Here the three attributes of brahma are creator, preserver and destroyer of the universe.

The Vedāntasūtra of [[Bādarāyana|Bādarāyaṇa]] further says that [[sastras|śāstras]] [[( sacred|(sacred]] canon or texts) are the source of the correct knowledge [[P1485]] of [[brahma,|brahma.]]4 To the objection that the Veda [[la|is]] concerned with rites, that some [[partions|portions]] of it are meant for the praise of rites, that the Vedic mantras only remind the performer about the various ingredients of sacrifice, that, therefore, the [[Vedanta texte|Vedānta texts]] either [[serye|serve]] no purpose at all or at the most give information about the soul of the sacrificer or the deity to be worshipped in [[sacrificos,|sacrifices,]] the reply is given by the Vedāntasūtra [[1.|I.]] 1. 4 (tat-tu [[samantayāt),|samanvayāt),]] which means that all Vedānta texts are agreed that their [[parport|purport]] is to establish that brahma, [[whioh|which]] has been postulated [[(in V.S.I.1.2)as|(in V. S. I. 1. 2) as]] the creator, preserver and absorber of the world has that [[charaoter|character]] and is omniscient and [[omnipotente|omnipotent.]]

That the argument from design also was present to the minds of the propounders of Vedānta follows from the fact that the [[Vedantasutra (IL 2. 1, racanānupapattesca nanumānam)|Vedāntasūtra (II. 2. 1, racanānupapatteśca nānumānam)]] denies that the [[Pradhana|Pradhāna]] of the [[Saṅkhya|Sāṅkhya]] (that is postulated as acetana) can be inferred as the cause of the universe.5

It may be noted here that, according to [[Saṅkarācarya,|Śaṅkarācārya,]] the detailed6 statements on the [[dootrine|doctrine]] about creation contained in the [[Upanisads|Upaniṣads]] are not to be taken literally, that no special purpose dependent thereon can be found nor is such a purpose laid down by [[Sruti|Śruti]] (Veda), but all those statements are intended to lead on to the knowledge of brahma and to expound the [[non difference|non-difference]] of the world from brahma.7 From early times most [[P1486]] philosophers [[osoillate|oscillate]] between the [[doetrine|doctrine]] of the First [[Pelnoiple ag|Principle as]] immanent in the universe and the doctrine that God is the creator and is transcendent. The [[Rgveda|Ṛgveda]] and Upaniṣads appear to postulate the first doctrine according to which the Absolute enters into it when it creates the Universe (Tai. Up. II. 6 ’tat [[srstvā|sṛṣṭvā]] [[tad-evānuprāvisat’),|tad-evānuprāviśat’),]] [[Cbān.|Chān.]] Up. VI. [[2.1,|2. 1,]] VI. 3. [[%, BF, Up.|2, Bṛ. Up.]] I. 4. 10. They also speak of God as ruling the Universe (antaryāmin) as in [[Br.|Bṛ.]] Up. III. 7. [[Kausitaki|Kauṣītaki]] Up. III. 8. At that time there was no atomic theory. Early Greek thought also wavered between the two [[doctrinos.|doctrines.]] Later on came the rival theory of cosmology in which atoms played a great part, that was adumbrated by Democritus (died about 370 B. C. [[200.|acc.]] to William James in ‘Some Problems’, and explained by Lucretius. In India also the [[Vaiśesikas|Vaiśeṣikas]] put forward the theory that the world is paramāṇus (atoms). [[Kapāda|Kaṇāda]] or Kaṇabhuk [[(lit,|(lit.]] one who assumes or subsists on kaṇas, very subtle [[partioles)|particles)]] is the reputed founder of the Vaiśeṣika system. He does not expressly say anything about God. But later medieval writers on [[Nyaya Vaiśesika|Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika]] combined the two doctrines of God and atoms. As the [[Tarkadipikā246|Tarkadīpikā]]8 puts the theory it is as follows: when God desires to create, activity is produced among the atoms, two atoms join, dvyaṇuka (dyad) is produced, tryaṇuka is [[produoed|produced]] from three [[dvyapukas|dvyaṇukas]] and in the end the great earth is [[prodaced;|produced;]] of things created when God desires to bring about dissolution an [[aotivity|activity]] is produced in the atoms. The atoms are eternal and infinite in number.

Though there was almost universal agreement about the existence of God among the Dharmaśāstra writers, there was (as in the9 West) divergence about the names, nature and [[P1487]] attributes of God. Most [[wors|were]] agreed that God is one without a [[8600nd,|second,]] is spiritual (and not physical, though several people worshipped Him in the form of [[Siya or Visgu a Davi), it mutable,|Śiva or Viṣṇu or Devī), immutable,]] omnipresent, omniscient, omnipotent, creator, holy, good and just &c. Difficult [[anestions|questions]] arise about belief in God. One or two may be mentioned: Is God omniscient in the [[coma pleted|completed]] and strictest sense viz. whether he could do anything [[what over|whatever]] as he likes or whether there are certain things which he cannot do. Another question is whether all existing things other than himself have been created by him or whether there are some things whose existence is as ultimate as God’s. All religions are [[faoed|faced]] with difficulties and therefore religion is a matter of [[faith|faith.]]

Though the [[Rgveda|Ṛgveda]] is full of the deeds of and prayers to individual Gods (such as Agni, Indra, Mitra, [[Varuna,|Varuṇa,]] Soma), the [[Roveda containg|Ṛgveda contains]] several hymns and verses to the effect that the original Principle is only one, that it creates the world out of itself, enters into it and inspires it. In [[Rg. I. 164.46|Ṛg. I. 164. 46]] the sage says ’the wise speak of the One existing (principle) under various names, they call it Agni, Yama, Mātariśvan (wind god)’. This is not a solitary verse. There are similar verses in the [[Rgyeda|Ṛgveda.]] For example, [[Rgveda VIII, 58. % (&|Ṛgveda VIII. 58. 2 (a]] verse from the [[Valakhilya|Vālakhilya]] hymns) says ‘The one fire is kindled in many places, the one Sun appears in the whole world, the one Dawn shines over all this world and the One (Principle, Spirit) became all this’. In [[Rs. X. 90.2,|Ṛg. X. 90. 2,]] it is declared ‘all this universe is (in reality) the Puruṣa alone, [[(both )|(both)]] what has been and what will come into existence in future’. In [[Rg.|Ṛg.]] II. 1. 3–7 Agni is identified with Indra, [[Viṣṇu, Brahma,|Viṣṇu, Brahmā,]] Varuṇa, Mitra, Aryaman, [[Tvasts,|Tvaṣṭṛ,]] Rudra, Draviṇodas, [[Savitr|Savitṛ]] and Bhaga. All these [[versos|verses]] establish that ultimately all plurality is only a play of words, a mere name [[(Vācārambhanam vikaro dāmadheyam’|(‘Vācārambhaṇam vikāro nāmadheyam’]] as the [[Obān.|Chān.]] Up. VI. 1. 4 states) and that unity is the only Reality and that the fundamental teaching of the Upaniṣads appears in germ in the [[Rgyeda.|Ṛgveda.]]

[[P1488]] In the [[Rgveda|Ṛgveda]] there are several hymns about the creation of the world in the 10th [[Mapdala|Maṇḍala]] (X. 72, X. 81 and 82, X. [[90.|90,]] X. 121, X. 129). For reasons of space all these cannot be set out at length, but some important texts will be mentioned. The main purpose of the [[bymn|hymn]] (X. 72) is to [[desoribe|describe]] the birth of eight [[Adityag.|Ādityas.]] [[Rg.|Ṛg.]] X. 72. 2 states10 that [[Brahmanaspati|Brahmaṇaspati]] brought about the [[( births|(births]] of) gods like & smith (that works with bellows) and that in the primeval ages of the Gods’ sat was produced from asat. In [[Rg.|Ṛg.]] X. 72. 4-5 and 8 it is said that [[Daksa|Dakṣa]] was born from Aditi and Aditi from Dakṣa, that the gods were born after her (Aditi) and that from Aditi eight sons were born. The two [[bymos|hymns]] X. 81 and 82 refer to Viśvakarman who fashioned the worlds. X. [[81.2|81. 2]] and 4 ask questions ‘what was the base11 (from where he created the world), what was the [[P1489]] material (out of which he fashioned the earth), what was the forest and the tree from which heaven and earth were [[obisolled|chiselled]] out’ and the third verse describes the one God as follows ‘The one God who sees all round, whose face is turned in all directions, who has hands and feet everywhere, who when creating heaven and earth sends them forth with his (two) hands as with bellows and with wings’ (as a bird is propelled). [[Rgveda X, 90 (001 taining 16 versus)|Ṛgveda X. 90 (containing 16 verses)]] is the famous hymn called Purusasūkta and postulates a supreme creator as a Puruṣa (called [[Adipurusa|Ādipuruṣa]] by Sāyaṇa) with a thousand heads, eyes and feet, [[asports|asserts]] that all this that has come into being and that is yet to come is [[Purasa,|Puruṣa,]] that from Puruṣa arose Virāj, from whom arose (what we may call the second) Puruṣa [[(Hiranyagarbha)|(Hiraṇyagarbha)]] whom the gods offered as havis (oblation or paśu) in a symbolical sacrifice in which the three seasons, Vasanta, [[Grisma|Grīṣma]] and [[Sarad|Śarad]] were respectively the ghee, the fuel and the [[haris.|havis.]] This hymn was probably composed at a time when, it appears, there was a firm belief (as in Śat. Br. [[V.2.4.7,|V. 2. 4. 7,]] VI. 1. 1. 3 and [[Tai. 8. VIL 4.2.1)|Tai. S. VII. 4. 2. 1)]] that nothing great can be accomplished without yajña or tapas. The hymn then proceeds to say that from that [[primoval|primeval]] yajña all animals [[(borses,|(horses,]] cows &c.), the four varṇas, the Sun, the Moon, Agni, Indra, the Veda, heaven and earth were produced. In the Atharvaveda XIX. 6 also there are 16 verses of which the first fifteen correspond with the first 15 verses of the [[Paruṣasūkta,|Puruṣasūkta,]] but the order of verses is different and some of the words also are slightly changed. The Vāj. S. 31 has all the 16 verses of the [[Prusan sakta|Puruṣasūkta]] but it contains five more verses and a [[prode|prose]] passage at the end. [[Rg.|Ṛg.]] X. 121. 1 declares12 that in the beginning [[P1490]] [[Hiranya garbha|Hiraṇyagarbha]] (the Golden Egg) was born; and verse 10 identifies him with [[Prajapati|Prajāpati]] and verses 8 and 10 declare that waters were produced by him from which issues forth [[Hiranyagarbha|Hiraṇyagarbha]] (the Golden egg), which was Prajāpati Himself. The second verse is quoted in n. [[1410.|1440.]] [[Rg.|Ṛg.]] X. 125 is a hymn placed in the mouth of Vāk (speech), wherein speech is portrayed as a power even beyond the gods and as creative. Three (1, 6, 8) out of the eight [[verges|verses]] may be rendered here ‘I move along with the Rudras and Vasus, with the [[Adityas|Ādityas]] and [[Viśyedevas;|Viśvedevas;]] I support both Mitra and [[Varupa,|Varuṇa,]] Indra and Agni and the two [[Asvins,|Aśvins.]] I stretch the bow of Rudra in order to kill the enemy, the hater of brahma (sacred prayer); I cause war for [[mon;|men;]] I entered heaven and [[earth,|earth.]] I blow like the wind, producing all the worlds; I am beyond [[hearen|heaven]] and beyond this earth; by my greatness (power) I have become such a one’. Here one must [[told|hold]] that the sage is not referring to ordinary speech or language but to the conception that word has creative power and that it is one with God or was the thought uttered by brahman.

Was there deep unfathomable abyss of waters?; (2) There was no death, [[honne|hence]] nothing immortal; there was no [[congoiousness|consciousness]] (distinction) of night and day; that one breathed by its own nature (power) without there being any air, really nothing other than that existed ; [[( 4 ) destre oame|(4) desire came]] into being, that was the first flow (seed, offspring) of the mind; (6) who knows directly, who can proclaim it here whence this [[crea tion|creation]] came into being; (7) He from whom arose this [[area tion,|creation,]] whether he made it or did not make it, the [[Higheat|Highest]] [[soor|seer]] in the highest heaven, he indeed knows or even he does not know?

It would be noticed that the sage, who was a [[poot|poet]] and philosopher, proclaimed that there was that one Being, raised high above all gods, conditions and limitations; he, the sage, gives expression to what he conceived to be the state before the creation of the universe. Night and day, death and [[immorta lity|immortality]] are opposites. These exist only when there is [[manifest ness|manifestness]] or creation and therefore he says ’there was no death, nor anything immortal’. The hymn does not say that there was non-being out of which being [[grow.|grew.]] What he means is that That One alone breathed, the opposites, being and non-being, did not exist. The first is the province of parā-vidyā which teaches that only the Ātman exists, that the cosmos exists within the Ātman and nothing else has intrinsic reality apart from it. The 2nd kind of reality is empirical and practical and the dogmas of the creation and dissolution of the world, of the individual self, its bondage, [[transmigaration|transmigration]] and final liberation are valid only for the aparā-vidyā. Most religions postulate three fundamental entities viz God, individual self and the external world. These three are true but only up to a certain limit (only so long as a man holds his own ego as [[&|a]] separate reality) but these three are not the ultimate Truth. Even in this lower kind of reality, a man who is in deep sleep becomes (for a time) united with (or absorbed in) the True, as stated by the [[Chān,|Chān.]] Up. VI. 8. 1 (yatraitat puruṣaḥ svapiti nāma satā somya [[tada gampanno|tadā sampanno]] bhavati). The third kind of reality pertains to dream [[state,|state.]] One may have experience of pleasure and pain and misery from what one sees in a dream, which are real as long as the dream lasts, but all this that is seen in a dream vanishes the moment the man is awake. As stated above (p. 1485 and note 2434) the descriptions of the creation of the world have only this in view that there is non-difference between cause and effect and that they all lead to a correct understanding about brahman. [[Saṅkarācārya|Śaṅkarācārya]] on V. [[s.|S.]] II. 3. 30 extends the same reasoning to individual selves (to be quoted later on under [[“Karma|‘Karma]] and transmigration’).

On the subject of creation, the question arises whether the individual Self is also a creation like that of the earth, trees and shrubs. The Upaniṣads have a good deal to say on this. Here also two kinds of texts have to be considered. In the first place, some texts seem to state that the individual selves spring from the Supreme Spirit. A few passages that are sometimes relied upon for this last matter may be cited here.31 The Bṛ. Up. states ‘just as tiny sparks spring forth from fire, in the same way from this [[Atman|Ātman]] spring up all [[prānas,|prāṇas,]] all worlds, all gods and all creatures’. The [[Mundaka|Muṇḍaka]] Up. expands this [[game|same]] idea as follows: As from a well-kindled [[fro|fire]] sparks of the same nature spring forth in thousands, so from the Imperishable [[varioús|various]] living beings issue and return into it. The [[Smrti|Smṛti]] of Yāj. [[oites|cites]] the same illustration of fire and sparks. Another and perhaps apter illustration is in the [[Katha|Kaṭha]] [[Up..just|Up. ‘just]] as pure water poured in (other) pure water becomes like it (i. e. not [[P1508]] [[distingui shable|distinguishable]]), so the self of the wise sage becomes (indistinguishable from the Supreme Essence). On the other hand, there are [[aumerous|numerous]] [[Upanisad|Upaniṣad]]32 passages which categorically state that the individual self is unborn, [[uniying,|undying,]] is not a product, that the Supreme Spirit enters as individual self, that there is [[non difference|non-difference]] between the one Supreme Spirit and the individual self. Some of these passages are set out in the note below. All those passages are cited by [[Śhaṅkaracarya|Śaṅkarācārya]] on [[v. S.|V. S.]] II. 3. 17, which states two propositions viz. the individual self is [[upproduced|unproduced]] and that it is eternal according to [[Sruti|Śruti]] passages ([[natmasruter-nityatvacca tābhyah|nātmāśruter-nityatvācca tābhyaḥ]]). How the one Supreme Spirit expands into and pervades the manifold universe of plurality is a great mystery and can only be explained by illustrations. The few passages in which the texts seem to mention the creation or dissolution of individual selves are to be understood as referring to the Upādhis (such as body and mind) by which the self is affected. Yājñavalkya gives this answer in finally winding up his exposition to [[Maitreyi2462|Maitreyī]]33 ’this self is imperishable and indestructible; but (when one speaks of death what is meant is) that the self has no longer any contact with material elements’. The [[śāntiparva|Śāntiparva]]34 and the [[Gitā|Gītā]] (II. [[20,21,24,25|20, 21, 24, 25]] ) say the same thing.

The highest metaphysical standpoint can be realized by only a few. For millions of men, the empirical standpoint alone remains and it is for them that texts speak of a personal God, ritual and sacrifices; they are only on the first rung of the ladder [[P1509]] to enlightenment and are only dimly aware of God; there is a much smaller class of people other than the preceding, who pray, seek God and come to realize that God is both immanent and transcendent; there is a third class of a very few people, the great sages and masters, the spiritual elite such as [[Saṅkarā cārya.|Śaṅkarācārya,]] who reach the peak of pure monism, who lose [[tine|the]] sense of the ego and who are ripe for entering into union with the One and they cannot and should not say that the individual soul and the physical world are all unreal (or Māyā). Both Bādarāyaṇa (V. S. II. 2. 29 [[Vaidharmyāo-ca na syapnādivat’)|‘Vaidharmyāc-ca na svapnādivat’)]] and [[Saṅkarācārya|Śaṅkarācārya]] are agreed that the ordinary physical world is entirely different from dreams and that the impressions in the waking state are not independent of existing objects. Apart from the question whether the word [[‘Māya’|‘Māyā’]] used in [[V, 8, IT, 2. 3(Māyāmātram|V. S. III. 2. 3 (Māyāmātram]] tu &c.) is used by [[Bādarāyana|Bādarāyaṇa]] in the sense in which [[Saṅkarācārya|Śaṅkarācārya]] understands it, it cannot be denied that Upaniṣad passages like Kaṭha Up. II. 4. 2, Praśna [[1,16,|I, 16,]] Chān. Up. [[VIII, 3.|VIII. 3.]] 1-2, the prayer in Bṛ. Up. I. 3. 28 ( asato mā sadgamaya &c) could easily suggest the [[dootrine|doctrine]] of Māyā and [[load|lead]] to it as an intelligible development. Hence the proper language for almost all [[man|men]] is not to speak about the world [[20|as]] Māyā (illusion ). If the individual soul and the world are unreal, then it may be argued by one who does not subscribe to the doctrine of Māyā as against those who hold it that you are teaching that an unreal soul has to escape from an unreal Saṁsāra and secure what you call [[moksa|mokṣa]] by means which are themselves unreal (such as Upaniṣad study) and that therefore [[moksa|mokṣa]] itself is unreal. How the one Reality becomes many and expresses itself in the ever-changing physical world is really an inexplicable mystery, but that does not entitle all of us to say that the world is unreal or a dream. The few highly [[philosophioal|philosophical]] men may say that what is [[roal|real]] is the one Absolute, that all else is only an appearance of that Absolute. Common men may, however, complain that explanations offered by [[thege|these]] philosophers do not satisfy them or are beyond them.

When one has to emphasize what the Reality behind the world is in itself, one speaks of the Absolute brahman, but when one has to speak about the relation of the one Reality to the individual selves and the physical world one speaks of a personal God. When the [[Vedantasūtra|Vedāntasūtra]] ([[II, 1.|II. 1.]] 14) states35 that the [[P1510]] world is non-different (ananya) from brahman, what is meant [[to|is]] not that the two are identical, but only this that the [[golves|selves]] and the world are not entirely different from brahman. When it is said that [[Moksa|Mokṣa]] results if one realizes brahman, there is no question of the destruction of the world but all that it comes to is that the false idea [[of|or]] outlook in that case is displaced or sublated by a true one. How the finite world arises from the Infinite is a mystery, for which [[Saṅkarācārya|Śaṅkarācārya]] employs the word [[* Māsā’.|‘Māyā’.]] But he is positive that till a person realizes the one Ātman all religious and worldly courses of life, [[real-upreal,|real-unreal,]] go on unobstructed. The concept of [[Maya|Māyā]] as postulated by [[Saṅkarācārya|Śaṅkarācārya]] (on V. S. [[IL|II.]] 1. 14 and other places) is one of the most misunderstood elements of Vedānta. Further, it should not be forgotten that a very large number of philosophically minded Hindus do not advocate the doctrine that the world is an illusion; all that is said by [[advaiting|advaitins]] is that the world is not as real as the Absolute is. The passages quoted below from [[Saṅkarādārya’s|Śaṅkarācārya’s]] Bhāṣya clarify his position, which is this. There is the [[physioal|physical]] world with its manifold distinctions, but it must rest on [[some thing|something]] else; that something is called the absolute brahman. The relation between the two is inexplicable and therefore it is spoken of as [[Mayā.|Māyā.]] In that way [[Sapkarācārya|Śaṅkarācārya]] is [[agnostia,|agnostic,]] while other religious philosophers are not willing to admit the futility of theories or their helplessness to put forward a generally acceptable and reasonable theory of the relation of the universe and the Eternal Spirit behind it.

It should [[pot|not]] be forgotten that, according to our śāstras the goals of human life are four, Dharma (an ethical life of doing what is right), Artha (a life of acquisition of wealth [[i. 8.|i. e.]] economic life based on justice), Kāma (a life of the enjoyment of innocent pleasures and right desires) and Mokṣa (liberation), this last being the highest goal to be attained only by a few people ( it is called Paramapuruṣārtha). Indradvīpa, Kaśeru, Tāmraparṇa, Gabhastimat, Nāgadvīpa, Sahya, Gandharva, Vāruṇa and a ninth which is 1000 yojanas long from north to south, on the east of which are Kirātas and on the west Yavanas and the four varṇas in the middle of it.57 It may be noted that though Bhāratavarṣa is only [[one the|one of the]] divisions of Jambudvīpa, some of the nine divisions are themselves called Indradvīpa and Nāgadvīpa. Another [[impor tant|important]] matter is that Matsya 114. 10, Vāyu 45. 81, Vāmana 13. 11, and Brahmāṇḍa appear to call the 9th dvīpa Kumāra or as extending from [[Kumāriki|Kumārikī]] to the sources of the Gaṅgā. [[P1527]] [[Thero fore,|Therefore,]] it [[appear|appears]] that the 9th division of [[Bharatvarṣa|Bhāratavarṣa]] was held to be a country like modern India and the other eight divisions seem to be countries and [[Islands|islands]] lying to the south-east of [[prosent|present]] India. It is probable that early works identified [[Bharatvaras|Bhāratavarṣa]] with what is now modern India, but when Indian culture spread to South-East Asia, Bhāratavarṣa was used to denote India as well as Greater India.
