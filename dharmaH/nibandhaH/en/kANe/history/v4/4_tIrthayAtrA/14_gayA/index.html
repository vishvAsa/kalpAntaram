[[P643]]CHAPTER XIV

[[GAYA|GAYĀ]]

Gayā occupies a unique place in the hearts of all pious Hindus even in these days. Hundreds of pages have been written about the history, the antiquities, the architectural remains of Gayā, the sacred places in and round about Gayā, the śrāddha rites performed in Gayā and about Gayāwals (the priests of Gayā). It is not possible to deal with this vast mass in this work. But it is impossible not to say a few words about the many conflicting views on certain important matters put forward by scholars for about three quarters of a century. Those who are deeply interested in the history and antiquities of [[Gaya|Gayā]] should carefully read the following books and papers; Dr. Rajendralal Mitra’s [[‘Buddha-Gaya’|‘Buddha-Gayā’]] with 51 plates (1878); General Cunningham’s ‘Mahābodhi’ with 31 plates at the end (1892); L. [[S.S.|S. S.]] O’Malley’s District Gazetteer of Gayā, vol. XII and J. [[A.S.B.|A. S. B.]] for 1903 LXXII, No. 3, pp. 1-11 on [[‘Gayāśrāddha and Gayāvals;’|‘Gayāśrāddha and Gayāwals;’]] Indian Antiquary, vol. X pp. 339-340 for a Chinese Inscription at Buddha Gayā dated in a Chinese date corresponding to 1033 [[A, D,|A. D.]] and pp. 341-347 for Inscriptions at Gayā, one of which is engraved in a temple of the Sun near a tank [[‘Daksinamānasa’|‘Dakṣiṇamānasa’]] near the [[Visnupada,|Viṣṇupada,]] dated 1813 years after Buddha’s Parinirvāṇa; Indian Antiquary, vol. 16 pp. 63 ff Inscription of Yakṣapāla, son of Viśvāditya, which records the building of a temple for certain images in the 15th regnal year of the Pāla king Nayapāladeva who died in 1045 [[A, D.,|A. D.,]] edited by Kielhorn; Dr. Benimadhav Barua’s [[‘Gayā and Buddha Gayā’|‘Gayā and Buddha-Gayā’]] in two volumes (1934) with plates; Journal of Bihar and Orissa R. Society, vol. 24 (for 1938) pp. 89-111 where [[Dr.J.C.|Dr. J. C.]] Ghose deals with ’the antiquity of Gayā.’ Some of the remarks of Dr. Ghose are most unwarranted and cannot be accepted, such as holding ‘maganda’ in ‘Pramagandasya’ in [[Rg.|Ṛg.]] III. [[53.14|53. 14]] as identical with Magadha and the explanation of [[‘udantyāh’in|‘udantyāḥ’ in]] the Ait. Br. as ‘people of sunrise.’ Among medieval digests Gayā is dealt with in Kalpataru on [[Tirthas|Tīrthas]] pp. 163-174, [[Tyrblade cintāmani|Tīrtha-cintāmaṇi]] of Vācaspati pp. 268-328, Tristhalīsetu of [[Narayana ) bhatta|Nārāyaṇa-bhaṭṭa]] pp. 316-379, [[Tirthaprakāśa|Tīrthaprakāśa]] pp. 384-452, [[Tirthendi, sekhara|Tīrthendu-śekhara]] pp. 54–59, [[Triṣthalisetu-sāra-sangraha|Triṣthalīsetu-sāra-saṅgraha]] of [[Bhattojin|Bhaṭṭoji]] pp. 36-38.

[[P644]]The most important work on Gayā relied upon in such works as the [[Tristhali-setu|Tristhalī-setu]] and in modern times is the [[Gayā māhātmya|Gayā-māhātmya]] that occurs at the end of the [[Vayupurāṇa|Vāyupurāṇa]] (chapters 105-112 of the Ānandāśrama ed.). [[Scholars, differ|Scholars differ]] about the antiquity of these chapters. Rajendralal Mitra assigned the Gayā-māhātmya to the 3rd or 4th century [[A, D.|A. D.]] O’Malley was inclined to hold that the legend of [[Gayasura|Gayāsura]] was invented in the 14th or 15th century, principally on the ground that the Gayāwals profess the [[Vaisnava|Vaiṣṇava]] faith established by [[Madhva cārya 1468|Madhvācārya]]1 and acknowledge as their spiritual head the mahant of Hari Narasimhapura; vide J. A. S. B. vol. LXXII (1903) p. 4. To my mind there is hardly anything in this. The Gayāwals are notorious for their indolence, dissoluteness and ignorance and they are a dying race. O’Malley himself notes in the paper mentioned above (at p. 8) that originally the families of Gayāwals were 1484, that in Buchanan Hamilton’s time they numbered about 1000, that in 1893 a Gayāwal counted 128 families, that in the census of 1901 there existed only 168 males (as pure Gayāwals) and 153 females. Gayā is a place sacred to [[Visnu|Viṣṇu]] and if the Gayāwals had to turn in medieval times for support to some great ācārya they would naturally choose to align themselves with the [[Vaisnava|Vaiṣṇava]] ācārya [[Madhya|Madhva]] rather than with [[Sankara.|Śaṅkara.]] Dr. Barua, after an elaborate [[exami nation|examination]] of the several items of evidence, holds that the [[Gaya māhātmya|Gayā-māhātmya]] is not earlier than the 13th or 14th century A. D. [[(vo]. I.|(vol. I.]] p. 64 of ‘Gayā and Buddha Gayā’). From considerations of the space at my disposal I cannot examine in detail his [[argu ments.|arguments.]] His conclusion is vitiated mainly for two reasons. He relies too much on the dubious and unconvincing argument from silence. He examines the account of Gayā that appears in the Vanaparva and compares it with the fuller account in the Gayāmāhātmya and arrives at the rather startling conclusion “The Gayā proper as known to the Epic (the Mahābhārata) is essentially a place sacred to Yama Dharmarāja, Brahmā and [[Sivasūlin,|Śivaśūlin,]] and [[Visnu and Vaisna vism|Viṣṇu and Vaiṣṇavism]] has no place in it either as a name or as an idea. Except [[P645]]Brahmayūpa, [[Sivalinga|Śivaliṅga]] and [[Vrsabha|Vṛṣabha]] there is no [[refe rence|reference]] to any structural erection nor any iconic suggestion’. In order to show how this conclusion is not at all justified the references to Gayā in the Epic and other Sanskrit works will have to be set out and compared with each other and with the Gayāmāhātmya. The second reason that vitiates Dr. Barua’s conclusion is the wrong interpretation that he puts on verse 12 of the Inscription edited by Kielhorn in I. A. vol. 16.

I shall now turn to the name Gayā and references to it or similar names from the [[Rgveda|Ṛgveda]] downwards. A Gaya, son of Plati, is the reputed author of two hymns of the [[Rgveda|Ṛgveda]] (X. 63 and X. 64). In [[Rg.|Ṛg.]] X. 63. 17 and X. 64. 17 we have the words ‘astāvi jano divyo Gayena’ (the divine host was praised by Gaya). So Gaya was a proper name applied to a composer of [[Rgvedic|Ṛgvedic]] hymns. In the [[Rg.|Ṛg.]] the word Gaya has several other senses, which are not material here. In the [[Atharvaveda (I. 14.4)|Atharvaveda (I. 14. 4)]] Gaya appears to be a wonder-worker or a sorcerer along with Asita and Kaśyapa. Even in the Vedic [[Samhitās|Saṁhitās]] Asuras, Dāsas and Rākṣasas are credited with tricks and magic. Vide [[Rg.|Ṛg.]] VII 99. 4, [[VII, 104.24-25|VII. 104. 24-25]] and Atharva IV. 23. 5. It is not difficult to imagine how a sorcerer Gaya could have been transformed into [[Gayasura|Gayāsura]] later on. Proceeding further, the Nirukta2 while explaining ‘idam [[Visnur-vi|Viṣṇur-vi]] cakrame tredhā nidadhe padam’ [[(Rg.|(Ṛg.]] I. 22. 17) gives two very interesting interpretations, one explaining the verse as a natural phenomenon and the other explaining it from geographical or legendary points of view “He [[(Visṇu)|(Viṣṇu)]] puts down his foot in three ways. According to [[Sākapūṇi, Vispu|Śākapūṇi, Viṣṇu]] plants his foot on the earth, in mid air (sky) and heaven; according to [[Aurṇavābha, on Samārohana, on Viṣnu pada and on Gayasiras.’|Aurṇavābha, on Samārohaṇa, on Viṣṇupada and on Gayaśiras.’]] Whatever the Vedic verse may mean, it is clear that two interpretations had arisen several centuries before Christ and if the usual dates about Buddha’s nirvāṇa are accepted [[Aurpavābha|Aurṇavābha]] and Yāska both flourished before Buddha. Vide S. B. E. vol. XIII pp. XXII-XXIII for Buddha’s nirvāṇa being placed at 483 B. C. according to Ceylonese chronicle, while some western scholars would place it between 429-400 B. C.3 [[Gayasiras|Gayaśiras]] is named in the Vanaparva (87. [[P646]]11, 95. 9), in the [[Visnudharma-sūtra 85,4 (in the form Gayā śirṣa),|Viṣṇudharma-sūtra 85. 4 (in the form Gayāśīrṣa),]] [[Vāmanapurāṇa (22.20|Vāmanapurāṇa (22. 20]] as the eastern vedi of Brahmā), Skanda I. 2. [[13.21|13. 21]] and even in the Buddhist books such as the Mahāvagga (I. [[21.1,|21. 1,]] S. B. E. vol. XIII p. 134) in which last it is said that after having dwelt at Uruvelā, Buddha [[accom - panied|accompanied]] by a thousand bhikkhus went to [[Gayāśirsa|Gayāsīsa]] (which is the Pali equivalent of [[Gayāśirsa).|Gayāśīrṣa).]] Traditions of Gaya as a king of the territories round about Gayā are mentioned in Jaina and Buddhist works. The Uttarādhyayanasūtra (S. B. E. vol. 45 p. 86 and note 3 there) states that he was son of king Samudravijaya of [[Rājagrha|Rājagṛha]] and became the 11th Cakravartin. The Buddhacarita of [[Aśvaghosa|Aśvaghoṣa]] states that Buddha visited the hermitage called a city of the royal sage Gaya, that the saint (future Buddha) fixed his dwelling on the pure bank of the [[Nairanjanā|Nairañjanā]] river (S. B. E. vol. 49 part 1 p. 132, Canto [[XIT.|XII.]] 87-88) and p. 193 (chap. XVII. 8 of the same work) and that Buddha went to the hermitage of Kāśyapa at Gayā called Uruvilvā. The same work (S. B. E. vol. 49 part 1 p. 192) speaks of [[Dharmātavi|Dharmāṭavī]] where dwelt 700 ascetics whom Buddha helped to enter nirvāṇa. Viṣṇupada occurs in the [[Viṣnudharmasūtra 85.40|Viṣṇudharmasūtra 85. 40]] as a holy place very fit for śrāddha. In my opinion at least [[Aurnavābha|Aurṇavābha]] held that in a particular region there were three places on which according to the legends current in his day the foot-prints of [[Visnu’s|Viṣṇu’s]] foot could be [[seen 1971.|seen]]4. Two of [[P647]]these, [[Visnupada|Viṣṇupada]] and [[Gayasiras|Gayaśiras]] are well-known; hence it would not be unreasonable to hold that ‘Samārohaṇa’ is a place and has to be located somewhere near the other two. [[Samārohana|Samārohaṇa]] means ‘mounting up’ or ‘ascending’. This word probably refers to the ascent of the hill that rises up from the river Phalgu. It is also possible that it is the same as the Udyanta hill echoing with the warbling (of birds).5 I therefore hold that at least 600 years before Christ (and hence even before Buddha) there was a tradition about [[Visnu’s|Viṣṇu’s]] footprints in at least two well-known places viz. [[Vispupada|Viṣṇupada]] and Gaya-śiras (both in Gayā). Even if some other work does not mention any one of these it does not follow from mere non-mention that that spot was not so named or did not exist.

Let us now turn to the Vanaparva. Dr. Barua mainly relies on Vanaparva 84. 82-103 and 95. [[9–29.|9-29.]] But some preliminary remarks must be made before the two passages are examined.

The [[Nāradiyapurāṇa|Nāradīyapurāṇa]] (uttara, 46. 16) states that [[Gayasiras|Gayaśiras]] extends from Krauñcapada to [[Phalgutirtha.|Phalgutīrtha.]]

Vanaparva chapter 82 sets out Pulastya’s replies to Bhīṣma’s question about [[tirthas.|tīrthas.]] First comes [[Puskara|Puṣkara]] (verses 20-40), then Jambumārga, [[Tandulikāśrama,|Taṇḍulikāśrama,]] Agastyasaraḥ, Mahākāla, [[Kotitirtha,|Koṭitīrtha,]] [[Bhadravata (Sthānutirtha),|Bhadravaṭa (Sthānutīrtha),]] Narmadā, Prabhāsa and several other [[tirthas|tīrthas]] (not in any particular order but selected at random). In chap. 83 [[Kuruksetra|Kurukṣetra]] is described at some length.

Before discussing the most important passage of the [[Vana parva|Vanaparva]] (viz. [[84, 82-103)|84. 82-103)]] we must see what precedes the [[descrip tion|description]] of Gayā. Dr. Barua and many of those that have written on this passage have not carefully considered the implications of verses 1-81 of chapter 84 and also of the following chapters. In Vanaparva 84. 1-81 the author makes Dhaumya name [[P648]]about 57 holy places (including such famous ones as [[Naimisa,|Naimiṣa,]] [[Sakambhari,|Sākambharī,]] [[Gangadvara,|Gaṅgādvāra,]] Kanakhala, the confluence of [[Ganga|Gaṅgā]] and Yamunā, Kubjāmraka) and then comes to speak of the sacred spots in [[Gayā.1473|Gayā.6]] It is therefore clear that the intention of the author is not to speak exhaustively about any [[tirtha,|tīrtha.]] For this reason some [[tirthas|tīrthas]] are described twice in the [[Vana parva.|Vanaparva.]] For example [[Naimisa|Naimiṣa]] is very highly praised in chap. 84. 59-64 and again in chap. 87. 6-7. The same happens with [[re gard|regard]] to Gayā in [[85.82-103 and 87.8-12.We|85. 82-103 and 87. 8-12. We]] cannot at all presume [[P649]]from the manner in which [[tirthas|tīrthas]] in Gayā are named and [[des cribed|described]] that the writer of Vanaparva (chap. 84. 82-103) meant to be exhaustive as regards the holy spots in Gayā or the legends about Gayā. This conclusion is very much strengthened by the fact that in the Anuśāsanaparva three sacred spots in Gayā are mentioned which do not occur in Vanaparva 84. 82-103 viz. ‘In Gayā a man becomes purified in [[Aśmaprstha|Aśmapṛṣṭha]] (the same as Pretaśilā), on the hill Niravinda and of even a third [[brāhmana|brāhmaṇa]] murder in [[Krauñcapadi’,1474|Krauñcapadī’.7]] These three, [[Aśmaprstha,|Aśmapṛṣṭha,]] [[Nira vinda|Nira-vinda]] and [[Krauñcapadi,|Krauñcapadī,]] are in addition to those sacred spots that are mentioned in Vanaparva 84. In Vāyu [[109.15|109. 15]] a hill Aravindaka is said to be a peak of the [[Silāparvata|Śilāparvata]] and [[Nāradiya|Nāradīya]] (uttara [[47.83),|47. 83),]] Vāyu [[108.75|108. 75]] and [[Nāradiya (‘uttara 46.16)|Nāradīya (‘uttara 46. 16)]] [[men tion|mention]] the hill Krauñcapada ([[Munda-prstha|Muṇḍa-pṛṣṭha]] was so called). [[There fore|Therefore]] it is clear that three important sites in the Gayāmāhātmya are mentioned by the Anuśāsanaparva.

It was unfortunate that Dr. Barua confined his attention on the question of the antiquity of Gayā to merely three works viz. the Vanaparva chapters 84 and 95, the Agnipurāṇa chap. 114-116 and the Vāyupurāṇa chapters 105-112. He did not care to investigate all the other [[Purānas|Purāṇas]] nor did it strike him that the explanation of three padas of [[Visnu|Viṣṇu]] furnished by [[Aurnavābha|Aurṇavābha]] probably referred to spots around Gayā alone. Other [[Purānas|Purāṇas]] such as [[Padma (Adikhanda 38.2-21), Garuda I chapters 82-86, and the Nāradiya (uttara chap. 44-47)|Padma (Ādikhaṇḍa 38. 2-21), Garuḍa I chapters 82-86, and the Nāradīya (uttara chap. 44-47)]] contain much that concerns Gayā and all of them have many verses in common. The critical edition of the Mahābhārata (published by the B. O. R. I., Poona) reads ‘sāvitram padam’ [[(chap. 82.81)|(chap. 82. 81)]] for [[‘sávitryāstu padam’|‘sāvitryāstu padam’]] of the Bombay edition. The reading of the critical edition is supported by numerous and ancient Mss. Further, the printed [[Padmapurāṇa in Adikhanda 38. 13)|Padmapurāṇa (in Ādikhaṇḍa 38. 13)]] reads ‘Sāvitram padam’ and thus lends support to the reading adopted in the critical edition. The words ‘sāvitram padam’ may be taken to mean the foot-print of [[Visṇu (Savitr).|Viṣṇu (Savitṛ).]] Dr. Barua goes very much further than the facts warrant when he says that there is no reference in the Vanaparva to any [[struc tural|structural]] erection or iconic suggestion. It is clear that there must have been an image of Dharma, since the Vanaparva speaks of [[P650]]the pilgrim to Gayā having to touch Dharma ([[tatrā - bhi-samsprśya|Dharmam tatrā-bhi-saṁspṛśya]]). Besides, there is a reference to the footprints of a cow with a calf and to ‘Sāvitra pada’. All these would surely be things that have an ‘iconic suggestion’. Moreover, Dr. Barua forgets for the moment that, according to the Chinese traveller Fa Hien who visited India between 399-413 A. D., the Gayā of the Hindu faith was ‘all emptiness and desolation’ (vide Legge’s translation p. 87). It is possible that Gayā might have suffered from earthquakes before [[400 A, D.|400 A. D.]] (as it is a region that is liable to severe earthquakes even in modern times) and therefore no structural erection of the ancient city of Gayā (to which ancient [[Pali|Pāli]] works and the Lalitavistara p. 311 refer) might have been left in Fa Hien’s day. Gayā appears to have passed through several [[vicissitudes, Some|vicissitudes. Some]] centuries before the Christian era it was in existence and was a flourishing town. By [[400 A, D.|400 A. D.]] it had been reduced to desolation. But in the 7th century the Chinese traveller Hiouen Thsang notes that the Hindu Gayā was a town with 1000 [[brāhmana|brāhmaṇa]] families (vide Beal’s B. R. W. W. vol. II p. 113). Later on when Buddhism waned and vanished, Gayā came to comprehend even Buddhist remnants, since the Vāyupurāṇa speaks of Gayā as extending from Pretaśilā to Mahābodhi tree (a distance of about 13 miles, according to Mitra p. 19).

Dr. Barua has also misunderstood verse 12 of the [[inscrip tion|inscription]] at Gayā edited by Dr. Kielhorn in [[I. A, vol.|I. A. vol.]] 16 p. 63 ff. The [[verse1475|verse8]] set out below may be translated as follows: ‘That wise (prince Yakṣapāla) caused to be constructed a temple for the images of Maunāditya and the other gods (mentioned in it), he dug up the famous Uttaramānasa lake and [[esta blished|established]] a sattra (charity house for food) near the Akṣaya [[(vata)’.|(vaṭa)’.]] This inscription of the reign of Nayapāla was [[P651]]engraved about [[1040 A,D.|1040 A. D.]] Dr. Barua thought that [[Uttaramānas a|Uttaramānasa]] was dug up at that time and therefore Uttaramānasa tank is taken to be not older than 1040 A. D. and that it and many of the sacred spots must have been later and the [[Gaya māhātmya|Gayā-māhātmya]] which speaks of Uttarmānasa must be much later than the 11th century A. D. But Dr. Barua is entirely mistaken in this. If a tank was dug up for the first time at the time of the inscription, it is impossible that it should have been called ‘khyāta’ (famous). The tank can become famous some centuries after it was dug. Uttarmānasa tank is mentioned as a sacred spot in [[Vāyupurana 77. 108|Vāyupurāṇa 77. 108]] (which verse is quoted about 1110 A. D. in the Kalpataru on [[tirtha at p. 168),|tīrtha at p. 168),]] Vāyu [[82.21,|82. 21,]] Agni 115. 10. Therefore Uttaramānasa tank was well known at least in the 8th or 9th century [[A.D.|A. D.]] What the inscription means is either that the famous tank had silted up and so it was dug up or that it was made longer, broader and deeper in about 1040. That is all.

In my opinion the Gayāmāhātmya (Vāyu, chap. 105-112), though possibly later than the main text of the Vāyupurāṇa, cannot be placed so late as the 13th or 14th century A. D. It is really a patch-work from many sources of earlier times such as the Vanaparva, Anuśāsanaparva, Padma I. 38, the Nāradīya (uttara) chap. 44-47. Several verses and half verses of it are repetitions (such as the half verse ‘Gayāyām akṣayam śrāddham japahomatapāṁsi ca’ Vāyu 108. 35 and Vāyu [[82.43|82. 43]] quoted as from Vāyu in Kalpataru on [[tirtha p. 167,|tīrtha p. 167,]] and Vāyu [[110. 29-30 are the same as Vāyu 105.37-38.|110. 29-30 are the same as Vāyu 105. 37-38.]] So also Vāyu [[82, 31-33|82. 31-33]] are the same as Vāyu 110. 51-53. Dr. Barua does not notice that in chap. 82 of the Vāyu (20-24) several [[sub-tirthas|sub-tīrthas]] of Gayā are [[ex pressly|expressly]] mentioned viz. [[Brahmakunda,|Brahmakuṇḍa,]] Prabhāsa, Pretaparvata, Uttaramānasa, [[Udici,|Udīcī,]] Kanakhala, Dakṣiṇamānasa, [[Dharm āraṇya,|Dharmāraṇya,]] Gadādhara, [[Matanga;|Mataṅga;]] so also chap. 77. 97–108 mention [[Gțdhrakūța,|Gṛdhrakūṭa,]] hermitage of Bharata, [[Matangapada,|Mataṅgapada,]] [[Munda prstha|Muṇḍapṛṣṭha]] and Uttaramānasa. Many of the verses occurring in the Gayāmāhātmya are found quoted in the [[Smộticandrikā 1476|Smṛticandrikā]]9 [[P652]](about 1150-1225 A. D.) on [[Srāddha and Asauca|Śrāddha and Āśauca]] (without name) as briefly exemplified below. I think that the [[Gayā. māhātmya|Gayā-māhātmya]] is to be assigned to some date between the 7th and 10th centuries A. D.

Having disposed of the question of the date of the Gayā-māhātmya and the references to the sacred spots in Gayā in Vanaparva chap. 84, it is necessary to see how Gayā is spoken of in the other parts of the [[Mahabhārata|Mahābhārata]] and the [[smrtis.|smṛtis.]] In the Vanaparva itself references to Gayā occur in chap. 87 and chap. 95. Vanaparva, chap. 87, starts by [[say ing|saying]] that going towards the east (from Kāmyaka-vana where the [[Pāndavas|Pāṇḍavas]] dwelt for some time) the pilgrim would come to the [[Naimiṇa|Naimiṣa]] forest and the [[Gomati|Gomatī]] river. Thus it is said that in the east there is a holy hill called after Gaya and a holy pool called Brahmasaras; then comes a famous [[verse 1477|verse10]] ‘one should desire to have many sons if even [[P653]][[(of whom )|(of whom)]] goes to Gayā, or performs the Aśvamedha [[sacri fice|sacrifice]] or lets loose a [[nila|nīla]] bull’ [[(the pitrs may be gratified ).|(the pitṛs may be gratified).]] Then Vanaparva chap. 87 goes on to refer to the holy river (mahānadī) Phalgu, the hill Gayaśiras, the [[Akṣayya Vata,|Akṣayya Vaṭa,]] where food offered to pitṛs becomes inexhaustible. In Vanaparva 95 [[men tion|mention]] is made of Brahmasaras (where Agastya went to [[Dharma rāja|Dharmarāja]] i. e. Yama, verse 12), of Akṣayavaṭa (v. 14) which is an imperishable place of worship and where the fruit [[(of offerings made )|(of offerings made)]] is inexhaustible; it further states that king Gaya, son of [[Amūrtarayas, 1477a|Amūrtarayas,11]] performed a sacrifice in which food and fees in plenty were distributed (verses 18-19). Vas. (XI. 42) states: when a man goes to Gayā and offers food his ancestors become delighted like agriculturists that rejoice when there is good rain and pitṛs become (really) putrin (having a son) on account of such a son. The [[Viṣnudharmasūtra, chapter 85, 1478|Viṣṇudharmasūtra, chapter 85,12]] mentions by name about 55 holy places that are most proper for [[sraddha|śrāddha]] among which those connected with Gayā are [[Gayāsirṣa,|Gayāśīrṣa,]] [[Akṣaya vata,|Akṣayavaṭa,]] Phalgu, Uttaramānasa, [[Matangavāpi,|Mataṅgavāpī,]] [[Visnupada,|Viṣṇupada,]] and winds up by quoting three verses as gāthās sung by pitṛs. Yāj. I. 261 states that whatever a man gives while in Gayā leads to inexhaustible rewards. Atri-smṛti (55-58) refers to a son’s going to Gayā for the benefit of the pitṛs, to a bath in Phalgu and tarpaṇa of pitṛs in the river, to seeing Gadādhara ([[Vispu)|Viṣṇu)]] at Gayā and to [[Gayāsīrṣā.|Gayāśīrṣa.]] [[Sankha|Śaṅkha]] (14. 27-28) [[refers 1479|refers13]] to several tīrthas (one being Gayā) the offerings made at which [[P654]]become inexhaustible. The Likhita-Smṛti [[speaks 1480|speaks14]] of the importance of Gayā as follows: In whosesoever name a piṇḍa is offered at Gayāśiras, whether for [[oneslf|oneself]] or for another, that person, if in hell, goes to heaven and if in heaven that man secures release (from saṁsāra). The story of Gayāsura has puzzled Mitra and subsequent writers. Mitra holds that nothing appears more absurd or stupid than the story of Gayāsura who, he emphasizes, is not a demon or a vicious monster, but a devout [[Vaisnava (‘Bodh-Gaya’|Vaiṣṇava (‘Bodh-Gayā’]] pp. 15-16). The story of Gayāsura is not unique. The [[Purānas|Purāṇas]] abound in stories that appear to modern minds absurd and puerile. Besides, there were several asuras like Prahlāda, [[Bāna|Bāṇa]] (devotee of [[Siva)|Śiva)]] and Bali (who was an exemplary and pious king and a worshipper of [[Visnu )|Viṣṇu)]] that were not monsters but good and devout persons, and yet fought with gods. For [[ex ample,|example,]] Kūrmapurāṇa (I. 16. 59-60 and 91-92) narrates that Prahlāda fought with [[Nrsimha;|Nṛsiṁha;]] the Padma (Bhūmikhaṇḍa [[1.8)|1. 8)]] calls him a Mahābhāgavata but states that he first fought with [[Visnu and entered Vaisnavītanu;|Viṣṇu and entered Vaiṣṇavītanu;]] while [[Vāmanapurana|Vāmanapurāṇa]] (chap. 7-8) speaks of his fight with [[Naranārāyana.|Naranārāyaṇa.]] In Pali works (e. g. [[Anguttara-nikāya,|Aṅguttara-nikāya,]] part IV pp. 197-204) he is called Pahārāda and asurinda (asurendra). Vide [[Anguttara-nikāya|Aṅguttara-nikāya]] (text) vol. IV p. 197. For Bali who was grandson of Prahlāda, a good king and a devotee of [[Visnu,|Viṣṇu,]] vide Brahmapurāṇa, chap. 73, Kūrma I. 17, Vāmana (chapters 77 and [[92),|92).]] For [[Bāna,|Bāṇa,]] son of Bali and the fight of Bāṇa helped by [[Siva with Krsna,|Śiva with Kṛṣṇa,]] vide Brahma chap. 205-206 and [[Visnupurāṇa|Viṣṇupurāṇa]] V. 33. 37–38.

According to Dr. Rajendralal Mitra (in [[‘Bodh-Gaya’ pp. 14. 18)|‘Bodh-Gayā’ pp. 14-18)]] the story of Gayāsura is an allegory of the vanquishing of Buddhism by Brahmanism. O’Malley thought (J. A. S. B. vol. LXXII part 3 p. 7 for 1904) that the Gayāsura legend represents an amalgamation or compromise of Brahmanism with the popular demonolatry which preceded [[Brahmanism, Dr.|Brahmanism. Dr.]] Barua critisizes both theories and appears to hold (vol. I. pp. 40-41) that the idea underlying the legend is to impress on people’s minds the high sanctity of the Gayā range of hills along the western bank of the river Phalgu, that Gayā figures nowhere in Buddhism, that [[Gaya 1492|Gaya]]23 or Namuci or [[Vrtra|Vṛtra]] is represented as a demon of darkness and as an enemy of Indra and that the Vedic word [[Trivikrarna|Trivikrama]] and the interpretation of [[Aurnavābha|Aurṇavābha]] are the nucleus of the legend of Gayāsura. From considerations of space it is not possible to discuss these [[P660]]theories here. To my mind it appears that Gayā had become a famous [[pitrtartha|pitṛtīrtha]] centuries before Christ and that the legend of Gayāsura is a post facto attempt to account for the sanctity of the place and the numerous shrines and holy places that had sprung up round Gayā.

Chap. 109 deals with the questions how Ādi-Gadādhara abided in manifest and unmanifest forms, how his gadā (mace) was produced and how the tīrtha called Gadālola became a destroyer of all sins. There was a powerful Asura called Gada, who, when [[Brahmā. requested,|Brahmā requested,]] gave up his bones to Brahmā. Viśvakarmā, at the desire of Brahmā, fashioned a wonderful mace out of the bones. In the times of Svāyambhuva Manu an Asura named Heti, son of Brahmā, performed severe austerities for thousands of divine years. He secured from Brahmā and other gods the boon that he could not be killed by gods, daityas, men or with such weapons as the discus of [[Krsna|Kṛṣṇa]] and others. Heti conquered the gods and became Indra. The gods went to Hari and requested him to kill Heti. He asked for a powerful weapon and the gods gave him the mace manufactured from the bones of Gada and [[Visnu|Viṣṇu]] killed Heti with the [[Gada.|Gadā.]] This story about demon Heti is mentioned in Agni 114. 26-27 and Nāradīya (uttara) 47. 9-11. Hari is called Ādi-Gadādhara [[because 1492a|because]]24 he was the first to wield that mace and stood on the śilā on the head of Gayāsura, supported by the Gadā, in order to make (the head of Gayāsura) stable. He shows himself in the form of hills viz. the [[Mundaprstha|Muṇḍapṛṣṭha]] hill, the Prabhāsa and other hills. These and [[Aksayavata,|Akṣayavaṭa,]] Phalgu and other rivers are the non-manifest forms of Ādigadādhara. The [[Vispupada, Rudrapada, Brahma pada|Viṣṇupada, Rudrapada, Brahmapada]] and other padas are the non-manifest and manifest forms of [[Gadādhara. 1493|Gadādhara.]]25 The image of Gadādhara is purely a mani-[[P661]]fest form. Asura Heti was killed by [[Visnu|Viṣṇu]] and went to [[Visnu loka.|Viṣṇuloka.]] When the body of Gayāsura was made stable Brahmā sang a hymn of praise to [[Visnu|Viṣṇu]] who asked Brahmā to choose a boon. The [[Tristhalisetu|Tristhalīsetu]] (p. 368) remarks that the above are the fixed rites for seven days in Gayā, that other [[tirthas|tīrthas]] may be visited according to one’s convenience if one stays in Gayā for half a month or a month; that the śrāddha at the Pretaśilā is to be the first and that at the Akṣayavaṭa is to be performed at the end,40 and that although there is variation between the order in which tīrthas are to be visited on the several days according to the Vāyupurāṇa, the Agnipurāṇa and other purāṇas, yet as the Vāyupurāṇa is most detailed as regards Gayā rites, the order therein should be followed and that one not knowing the order may visit the [[tirthas|tīrthas]] in any order41 [[1508 except|except]] [[Pretasila|Pretaśilā]] and [[Aksayavata.|Akṣayavaṭa.]] Gayāyātrā, chapter 112 of the Vāyupurāṇa, refers to king Gaya who performed a yajña and who got two boons, one about the restoration of the Gayā brāhmaṇas to favour and the other that Gayāpurī be named after him and who then went to [[Visnuloka.|Viṣṇuloka.]] Then it narrates the story of king [[Visāla|Viśāla]] (verses [[7-15|7–15]]) who being sonless performed [[pindadāna|piṇḍadāna]] on [[Gayāśīrsa|Gayāśīrṣa]] and thereby saved his three ancestors, got a son and also himself went to heaven. Then another story is told (verses [[16-20|16–20]]) of a deceased person, who was in the condition of a preta, offering one-sixth of his property to a trader and asking him to perform Gayāśrāddha with the rest of his wealth and who on account of śrāddha at Gayā became free from the condition of preta.42 Then verses [[20-60|20–60]] mention several other [[tirthas|tīrthas]] of Gayā such as [[Gāyatritirtha,|Gāyatrītīrtha,]] [[Prāci-sarasvatitirtha,|Prācī-sarasvatītīrtha,]] Viśālā, Lelihāna, the [[P667]]hermitage of Bharata, [[Mundaprstha, Akāśagangā, Vaitarani|Muṇḍapṛṣṭha, Ākāśagaṅgā, Vaitaraṇī]] and other rivers and sacred places. It winds up by saying that six Gayās confer mukti (by worship and [[pindadāna|piṇḍadāna]] at them) viz. Gayāgaja, Gayāditya, [[Gāyatri (tirtha),|Gāyatrī (tīrtha),]] Gadādhara, Gayā and Gayāśiras.43

The Agnipurāṇa (chap. 116. [[1-34|1–34]]) has a long list of [[tirthas|tīrthas]] at Gayā that is quoted by the [[Tristhaltsetu|Tristhalīsetu]] pp. [[376-378,|376–378,]] which are passed over here.

Though the number of [[tirthas|tīrthas]] at [[Gaya|Gayā]] is very large, the majority of pilgrims do not visit them all. Visits to three places are absolutely necessary for every pilgrim to Gayā, viz. Phalgu river, [[Visnupada|Viṣṇupada]] and [[Akṣayavata,|Akṣayavaṭa,]] at all of which worship has to be done with milk, water, flowers, sandalwood paste, tāmbūla, lighted lamp and [[pindas|piṇḍas]] to ancestors have to be offered. The [[Visnupada|Viṣṇupada]] temple is built over the footprint of [[Vispu|Viṣṇu]] on the rock to the west of the river Phalgu. The old town of Gayā was built round [[Visnupada|Viṣṇupada]] and it is the largest and most important temple in Gayā. The footprint (about 16 inches in length) deemed to be God [[Visnu’s|Viṣṇu’s]] is inside an [[octa gonal|octagonal]] basin plated with silver. Pilgrims of all castes (except untouchables) surround the basin and cast their offerings [[in side|inside]] the basin, though for a substantial payment the priests clear out all people, shut the door for a minute or two and allow a very orthodox or a rich person to make his offerings without being disturbed by anyone else. There are 45 vedis (or sacred places) at which the leisured pilgrim may offer [[pindas|piṇḍas]] in the Gayākṣetra which extends from about five miles to the north-west of Old Gayā town to about 7 miles south of it. Though the ancient Buddhist works and Fa Hien and Hiouen Thsang make a distinction between [[Gaya|Gayā]] and Uruvilvā or Uruvelā where Buddha attained Enlightenment after six years of severe tapas, the Bodhi tree (Mahābodhitaru) was recognized by the Gayāmāhātmya as one of the sacred places which Hindu pilgrims should visit and is so regarded even now. When the Hindus appropriated Buddhist sacred places to themselves it is difficult to say. The Bodhi tree is the oldest historical tree in the world. A branch of it is said to have been transplanted to Ceylon in the times of the great Emperor [[Asoka|Aśoka]] (about 250 B.C.) [[P668]]and at Kandy in Ceylon a pippala tree is even now shown as that transplanted tree or the successor of that branch. The [[Gayāśīrsa 1510|Gayāśīrṣa44]] is a set of rocky hills such as [[Gayāśīrsa, Munda prstha,|Gayāśīrṣa, Muṇḍapṛṣṭha,]] Prabhāsa, [[Grdhrakūta, Nāgakūța,|Gṛdhrakūṭa, Nāgakūṭa,]] ranging over one krośa (about two miles).

It has been already stated above (p. 666) that the rites at the [[Akṣayavata|Akṣayavaṭa]] are the last in the pilgrimage to Gayā. The [[Gayā wal|Gayāwāl]] priest binds the thumbs or hands of the pilgrim with a garland of flowers and receives his fees. He gives sweetmeats to the pilgrim as prasāda, applies tilaka to the pilgrim’s [[forehead, .|forehead,]] touches the pilgrim on his back, pronounces the word ‘suphala’ (the pilgrimage has been fruitful or successful), declares that the pilgrim’s ancestors have gone to heaven and blesses the pilgrim. It must be noted that there is a special class of priests called Dhāmin, who alone (and not the ordinary [[Gayāwals)|Gayāwāls)]] have the right of officiating at five vedis viz. at [[Pretasila,|Pretaśilā,]] Rāmaśilā, [[Rāmakunda,|Rāmakuṇḍa,]] [[Brahmakunda|Brahmakuṇḍa]] and Kākabali, that are situated on or about the two hills [[Rāmaśila|Rāmaśilā]] and Pretaśilā (vide Bengal District Gazetteers, vol. 12 for Gayā p. 66). They are regarded as inferior in status to the regular [[Gayāwals.|Gayāwāls.]]

One important question on which there is great divergence of views among the medieval digests is about the ancestors to whom śrāddha is to be offered at Gayā. In the Vāyu and other [[Purāpas|Purāṇas]] it is frequently said that he who performs [[srāddha|śrāddha]] at [[Gaya|Gayā]] becomes free from the debt due to the [[pitrs 1511|pitṛs45]] or that what is offered to the [[pitrs|pitṛs]] at [[Gaya, Dharmaprstha, Brahma saras,|Gayā, Dharmapṛṣṭha, Brahmasaras,]] or on the [[Gayasirsa|Gayāśīrṣa]] and under the imperishable [[Vata|Vaṭa]] tree becomes inexhaustible. In most of these passages the plural of the word [[‘pitr’|‘pitṛ’]] is used. Therefore it follows that śrāddha is to be offered in Gayā to three paternal ancestors. And on account of a verse attributed to Gautama,46 [[Gautama,1512 the|the]] three male maternal [[an cestors|ancestors]] also are to be offered śrāddha. The difference arises with [[P669]]reference to the wives of these six male paternal and maternal ancestors. The Agnipurāṇa proposes an option (115. 10) that the devatās in [[Gayasraddha|Gayāśrāddha]] are nine or twelve. When they are to be nine, the śrāddha is for three paternal ancestors, three male ancestors of the mother and the wives of the latter three male ancestors, while there is a separate śrāddha for the mother, paternal grand-mother and paternal great-grandmother. If there are to be twelve devatās in Gayāśrāddha, then all the wives of the paternal ancestors and of maternal ancestors would be included in one [[sraddha.|śrāddha.]] Aparārka allowed an option (as in Agni) in Gayā-śrāddha.47 [[Gayā-śrāddha. 1513. According|According]] to the [[Smrtyarthasāra 1514|Smṛtyarthasāra48]] and Hemādri the [[Anvastakā-śrāddha|Anvaṣṭakā-śrāddha]] and Gayāśrāddha for the paternal ancestors and that for the wives of these (i. e, mother, paternal grandmother &c.) must always be separate, but the śrāddha for the mother’s paternal ancestors and their wives should be one (and therefore the devatās will be nine). Yama (verse 80) appears to hold that the mother, paternal grandmother and paternal great-grandmother are always to be associated with their respective husbands in [[śrāddha, Some|śrāddha. Some]] held that the devatās of Gayāśrāddha were only six viz. the three male paternal ancestors and three male maternal ancestors.49 [[ancestors.1515 The|The]] Tri. S. ultimately remarks that the usage of the country should be the deciding principle. The [[Prajāpati-smrti|Prajāpati-smṛti]] [[(183-184)|(183–184)]] has some rather contradictory views as to when there would be 12 or 9 or 6 devatās in a [[sraddha.|śrāddha.]] When there are twelve devatās, the [[sankalpa|saṅkalpa]] at the [[Pretaśila|Pretaśilā]] śrāddha which is the model of all the śrāddhas at the other sacred spots in Gayā (with suitable modifications) will be as noted below.50 [[below. 1516|]]

It should be noted that there are certain special [[charac teristics|characteristics]] of Gayāśrāddha. There is no tonsure (Vāyu 83. 18 [[P670]]and note 1485 above). One must honour the Gayāwals alone, and not other brāhmaṇas, however learned they may be ; one should not consider the family or character or learning of Gayā [[brahmanas|brāhmaṇas]] (vide p. 579 above and note 1313). But it has to be remembered that [[Narayanabhatta 1517|Nārāyaṇabhaṭṭa51]] did not accept this for all [[śrāddhas t|śrāddhas at]] Gayā, but only as to the śrāddha under the [[Akṣaya vata,|Akṣayavaṭa,]] since the Vāyu [[(111.79-80)|(111. 79–80)]] again refers under [[Aksaya vaṭa|Akṣayavaṭa]] to the worship of Gayā brāhmaṇas and since following the maxim of [[‘caturdhakarana|‘caturdhākaraṇa’]] laid down in Jai. III. 1. [[26-27|26–27]] the later specific provision narrows down the wide words used in the earlier passage. At Gayā one could offer [[pinda|piṇḍa]] to oneself but without sesame.52 [[sesame.1518 The|The]] Tri. S. (p. 350) observes (relying on Vāyu) that when a man offers a [[pinda|piṇḍa]] to himself, it should be in the hands of the image of Janārdana on [[Bhasmakūta 1519|Bhasmakūṭa53]] and that the pilgrim to Gayā can offer a [[pinda|piṇḍa]] to himself only when it is certain that he has no son or other adhikārin for offering śrāddha. Further, any one related or unrelated, could offer a [[pinda 1520_to any body,|piṇḍa54 to anybody,]] as laid down in Vāyu ‘Whether it is one’s own son or the son of another (who offers), in whosesoever name a [[pinda|piṇḍa]] is let fall at Gayā, that man attains everlasting Brahma. Then Raghunandana briefly touches upon the śrāddhas to be performed at the several padas and remarks that the last śrāddha at the padas is that at [[Kaśyapapada.|Kāśyapapada.]] He prescribes [[Vāyu 111.76|Vāyu 111. 76]] as the mantra for bath in [[Gadalola-tirtha.|Gadālola-tīrtha.]] Then he states that the śrāddha at the [[‘Akṣayavata|Akṣayavaṭa]] is to be performed on the north side of the [[Vata|Vaṭa]] near its bottom. Further, he speaks of śrāddhas to be performed at several sub-tīrthas, such as [[Gāyatri,|Gāyatrī,]] [[Sarasvati,|Sarasvatī,]] Viśālā, the hermitage of Bharata, [[Mundappstha.|Muṇḍapṛṣṭha.]] Then he prescribes that the pilgrim should cross the [[Vaitarani|Vaitaraṇī]] (situated to the east of the [[Devanadi|Devanadī]] which is near [[Bhasmakūta)|Bhasmakūṭa)]] with the mantra [[(Vāyu 105.44,| (Vāyu 105. 44,]] [[yāsau Vaitarani|yāsau Vaitaraṇī]] nāma &c.).
