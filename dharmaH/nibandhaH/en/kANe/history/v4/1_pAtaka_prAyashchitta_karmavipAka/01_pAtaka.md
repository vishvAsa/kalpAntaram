[[CHAPTER I|SECTION I]]

PĀTAKA, [[PRĀYAŚCITTA AND KARMAVIPAKA.|PRĀYAŚCITTA AND KARMAVIPĀKA.]]

(Sins, expiations and the residual consequences of sins).

CHAPTER I

[[P1]][[20 The|The]] Article on sin in Hasting's Encyclopædia of Religion and Ethics (vol. 11) will clearly show how the idea of sin varies from religion to religion, from age to age and from [[coun try|country]] to country. We are concerned here with the growth and [[developinent|development]] of the doctrine of sin in India from Vedic times to the medieval digests and commentaries on [[Dharmaśāstra, do|Dharmaśāstra.]]

[[1o Sin|Sin]] (pātaka) is a word that belongs to the terminology of religion rather than to that of [[Ethics. 1|Ethics.[^1]]] It is very difficult to define sin. In a general way it may be said that it is an act which is regarded as a wilful rebellion against or disobedience of some law supposed to be laid down by God or Revelation; it is opposition to the will of God manifested in an authoritative work or at least failure to abide by the regulations contained therein.

[^1]:
    In these days many people, both in the East and the West, question the very existence of sin. As Barbour puts it in his 'Sin and the new Psychology' (p. 19) "The feeling is gaining wide acceptance that there is no such thing as sin in the Christian sense. Evil may encompass a man's life and bring disaster to his personality, but it is not sin. It is psychic evil that can be explained by psychic causes and perhaps cured by psychological therapy. ... Many say 'nothing is right and nothing is wrong. Every thing is the result of complexes'. It has resulted in an easy tolerance of sin". Sir Oliver Lodge in his article on 'Christian Doctrine' in the Hibbert Journal for 1903-4 at p. 466 says 'The higher man of to-day is not worrying about his sins at all, still less about their punishments. His mission, if he is good for anything, is to be up and doing and in so far as he acts wrongly or unwisely he expects to suffer'. The followers of Cārvāka who was the prince of Atheists in ancient India used to say 'man should live among pleasures as long as life lasts; he should feed himself sumptuously (lit. should drink ghee) even by borrowing money from others. There is no coming again into the world after the body is reduced to ashes by cremation'.

[[P2]]The [[Rgveda|Ṛgveda]] is full of fervent and heart-felt expressions of the sense of sin and betrays intense desire on the part of the sages of old to be free from it. The idea of sin in the [[Rgveda|Ṛgveda]] is bound up with the conception of [[ta.|ṛta.]] The conception of [[rta|ṛta]] cannot be [[disoussed|discussed]] here in detail. But a brief statement is absolutely necessary in order to be able to convey what the Vedic doctrine of [[sin was.2|sin was.[^2]]]

There is a great deal of discussion about surā in many of the digests and the propositions [[P21]]established by most of them are[^48] : (1) that all the three higher varṇas are forbidden to drink the surā prepared from flour and drinking it is a grave sin (mahāpātaka) in the case of anyone belonging to the three first [[varnas;|varṇas;]] (2) All intoxicants (madya) are forbidden to brāhmaṇas at all stages of life (Gaut. Steya (theft). In order to constitute theft as a grave sin according to the commentaries the theft must be of a brāhmaṇa's gold of a certain quantity. Steya is defined by [[Ap. Dh, S.|Āp. Dh. S.]] I. 10. 28. 1 [[as|as[^53]]] 'a man becomes a thief by coveting another's property (and taking it) in whatever situation he may be (without the owner's consent)'. Kātyāyana (810) [[defines54|defines[^54]]] it as 'depriving a man of his property, whether clandestinely or openly and, whether by night or by day, is known to be theft'. Vyāsa defines it similarly. [[Vacaspati|Vācaspati]] in his [[bhāsya|bhāṣya]] on Yogasūtra II. 30 defines steya as taking property from another in a manner that is not allowed by the śāstras ([[steyam aśāstra-pūrvakam dravyāṇām paratah svikaranam).|steyam-aśāstra-pūrvakaṁ dravyāṇāṁ parataḥ svīkaraṇam).]] Though Manu (XI. 54) and Yāj. III. 257 [[(brāhmanasvarnahāri)|(brāhmaṇasvarṇahārī)]] add the qualification that he should be a thief guilty of stealing gold (from a [[brāhmana,|brāhmaṇa,]] acc. to Yāj.). [[Vas,|Vas.]] 20. 41 and Cyavana (q. by Pr. V. p. 117) mention [['brāhmana suvarna-haraṇa'|'brāhmaṇa-suvarṇa-haraṇa']] as a [[mahāpåtaka|mahāpātaka]] and the Sāmavidhāna Br. (I. [[Mahāpātaki samsarga-Association|Mahāpātakisaṁsarga—Association]] with those who are guilty of the four mahāpātakas has already been described in H. of Dh. vol. III. pp. 614, 944. Gaut. [[21.3,|21. 3,]] Vas. I. 21-22, Manu XI. 180 (=[[śāntiparva|Śāntiparva]] 165. 37), Yāj. III. 261, Viṣṇu Dh. S. [[35.3,|35. 3,]] Agnipurāṇa 170. 1-2 prescribe briefly that one who closely associates or dwells with any one of the four grave sinners for one year himself incurs [[mahāpataka 60|mahāpātaka[^60]]] and they add that this applies when a man occupies the same conveyance or same seat (as the sinner) or dines in the same row (with the sinner), but that if a person enters into spiritual relationship (such as that of teaching the Veda to the sinner or learning it from him or of officiating as a priest for him or allowing him to officiate as a priest for himself) or into a sexual or matrimonial alliance with him [[(i. e,| (i. e.]] a man who cohabits with a woman that is a grave sinner or if a woman cohabits with a male that is a great sinner or if a man marries the daughter of a patita or if a woman marries a bridegroom that is [[palita)|patita)]] that person becomes guilty of mahāpātaka at once. Bṛhaspati [[speaks 61|speaks[^61]]] [[P26]]of nine kinds of saṁsarga (contact or association) out of which the first five were treated as light sins, but the other four were serious viz. occupying the same bed or seat, taking food in the same row with the sinner, cooking food in the sinner's cooking pots or partaking of food prepared by him, being his sacrificial priest or employing him as one's sacrificial priest, being the sinner's teacher of the Veda or employing him as one's teacher of the Veda, sexual intercourse, taking food with him in the same pot.

The Pr. Prakāśa (folio 74a) holds that saṁsarga is of three kinds, the highest, middling and lowest. The first includes four viz. yonisambandha (marriage), [[srauva|śrauva]] (i. e. that due to sacrificing for a sinner or making him a priest), [[maukcha|maukha]] (about learning or teaching Veda) and eating of food from the same vessel ([[ekāmatrabhojana);|(ekapātrabhojana);]] the middling is of five kinds [[viz,|viz.]] using the same vehicle, seat, bed or coverlet; eating in the same row and learning the Veda together [[( sahādhyayana).|(sahādhyayana).]] The lowest is of various other kinds such as intimate talks, touching, [[cook ing|cooking]] food in the same vessel, eating food at his house, receiving a gift from him &c. Adhyāpana in order to be a great sin must relate to the Veda, and so also yājana must relate to such Vedic sacrifices as [[Darsapūrnamāsa,|Darśapūrṇamāsa,]] Cāturmāsya, [[Agnistoma.|Agniṣṭoma.]] Helping a mahāpātakin to perform the five daily yajñas or teaching him the aṅgas (metrics, grammar etc.) and the śāstras is only a minor sin. [[Parāśara62|Parāśara[^62]]] (XII. 79) states that sins are transferred (or spread) from man to man like a drop of oil on water by sitting or sleeping together or by using the same conveyance or by speaking with or dining in the same row. Devala and Chāgaleya q. by the Mit. on Yāj. [[III, 261,|III. 261,]] the [[Pr. Prakarana|Pr. Prakaraṇa]] (p. 101), the [[Pr, V.|Pr. V.]] (p. 145), the Par. M. (II. part 1. p. 28) and others contain similar verses. Acc. to [[Pr. Prakasa|Pr. Prakāśa]] (folio 75a), in order to become patita one must simultaneously practise these four together; if practised separately, there is no pātitya but only doṣa. Parāśara (I. 25-26) states that in the [[Křta|Kṛta]] age a man became patita by speaking with a patita, in Tretā by touching him, in Dvāpara by partaking of food prepared in his house and in Kali by actually committing a sinful act and that in the [[Kịta|Kṛta]] age a district was abandoned (if any one therein became patita), in Tretā the village, in Dvāpara the family (of the patita was abandoned) and in Kali only the actual perpetrator himself (is [[P27]]abandoned as [[palita).|patita).]] It is clear from this that such contacts with a patita as speaking with [[him,63|him,[^63]]] touching him or eating food (in the house of a patita) were not regarded as involving a man in the same sin as that of the actual perpetrator. [[Speak ing,|Speaking,]] touching and eating are also actions ([[karman )|(karman)]] and Parāśara did not include them in the word 'karmaṇā' which refers only to the actual perpetration of a murder, surāpāna etc. Therefore when some writers include such contacts as eating or touching under [['karmaṇāl|'karmaṇā']] they put two meanings on the word karman in Parāśara viz. perpetration of a murder or the like and also doing some of the acts (touching, eating) which are forbidden to be done in reference to a perpetrator by some [[smrtis.|smṛtis.]] This is a procedure which is against the maxim of the [[Mimāṁsā|Mīmāṁsā]] that the same word conveys [[only 64|only[^64]]] one sense in the same context. The reasonable inference from the words of Parāśara is that merely touching a mahāpātakin or eating food in his house did not involve the toucher and eater in pātitya.

Though many crimes do not in so many words come within the words of the definitions of the mahāpātakas the [[smrtis|smṛtis]] extend by analogy the [[saine|same]] condemnation as the mahāpātakas in three ways. For example, Yāj. III. 251 expressly states that one who kills a [[ksatriya|kṣatriya]] or [[vaigya|vaiśya]] engaged in (soma) sacrifice or a foetus or an [[ātreyi|ātreyī]] woman has to undergo the same expiation as for [[brāhmana|brāhmaṇa]] murder (therefore this is vācanikātideśa). Yāj. [[IIL|III.]] 232-233 extends the sin of gurutalpa-gamana to intercourse with several near female relatives (such as mother's or father's sister). This is extension by tādrūpya. The [[smrtis|smṛtis]] declare many actions as equal to [[( sama)|(sama)]] mahāpātakas in general or as equal to one of the mahāpātakas. This is atideśa by sāmya. A few words must be said on this topic. The general rule is that the [[expia tion|expiation]] for those lapses that are declared to be equal to a mahāpātaka is less than (i. e. half of) what would be prescribed [[P30]]for the [[mahāpātaka itself?!.|mahāpātaka itself[^71].]] And the penance for those that come under atideśa of the vācanika or tādrūpya kind is [[three fourths|three-fourths]] of that for the mahāpātaka itself. But it has to be noted that there is divergence of views about these in the sūtras and the [[smrtis.|smṛtis.]] Acc. to Gaut. [[21.10|21. 10]] [[giving72|giving[^72]]] false evidence, backbiting about another's guilt that will reach the king, falsely accusing one's guru with a grave sin or crime are equal to mahāpātaka. In Manu [[XI.55|XI. 55]] (= Agnipurāṇa 168. 25) the last two of these three and false statement about one's caste or learning or family (i. e. saying that one is a [[brāhmana|brāhmaṇa]] when one is not) for securing [[pro sperity|prosperity]] or eminence are stated to be equal to brāhmaṇa's murder. Acc. to Yāj. III. 228 falsely charging one's guru is equal to brahmahatyā and false statement about one's caste or learning is equal to drinking surā (Yāj. III. 229), while acc. to [[Visnu|Viṣṇu]] Dh. S. [[37.1-3|37. 1-3]] the three sins mentioned in Manu XI. 55 are to be [[in cluded|included]] among minor sins (upapātakas), while giving false [[evid ence|evidence]] is declared by [[Visnu|Viṣṇu]] to be equal to drinking surā [[( 36.2).|(36. 2).]] No useful purpose would be served by dilating at great length on the divergences of [[smrtis|smṛtis]] on what lapses are equal to one of the [[Mahāpātakas.|mahāpātakas.]] The digests explain that where the same lapse is described as equal to surāpāna by one [[smrti|smṛti]] and as equal to [[brāhmana-murder|brāhmaṇa-murder]] by another the idea is that there is an option as to the penance prescribed for that lapse. [[There fore|Therefore]] only the remarks of Manu and [[Yaj.|Yāj.]] will be set out here. Manu XI. 56 [[( = Agnipurāṇa 168, 26 )|(= Agnipurāṇa 168. 26)]] prescribes that forgetting the Veda [[(already studied ),|(already studied),]] reviling the Vedas, giving false evidence, killing a friend, eating forbidden food or food that should not be eaten, these six are equal to surāpāna. Vide Yāj. III. 228 already referred to above. Manu XI. 57 holds that stealing a deposit or a man or horse or silver or land or diamonds and other gems is equal to the theft of [[brāhmana's|brāhmaṇa's]] gold. Yāj. III. 230, [[Visnu|Viṣṇu]] Dh. S. 5. 383, [[Agni purāṇa 168.27|Agnipurāṇa 168. 27]] are to the same effect. Acc. to Manu XI. 58 (= Agnipurāṇa 168. 28) carnal intercourse with a sister by the same mother, with maidens, with females of the lowest castes [[(like Candālas),|(like Caṇḍālas),]] with the wife of a friend or son is declared to [[P31]]be equal to the violation of a guru's bed. Yāj. [[III,|III.]] 231 is practically the same (but he adds intercourse with a sagotra woman to the list). Gaut. 23. 12 and Manu XI. 170 are very similar. Yāj. [[III,|III.]] 232-233 declare that a man who has sexual intercourse with a sister of his father or mother, with his maternal uncle's wife, with his daughter-in-law, with a co-wife of his mother, with his sister, with the daughter or wife of his Vedic teacher or his own daughter, is guilty of being a violator of the guru's bed and should have his penis cut off and be killed (by the king) and the woman concerned, if she was a willing party, should also be killed. Nārada (strīpuṁsayoga, [[verses 73-75)73|verses 73-75)[^73]]] states: "If a man has sexual intercourse with any of these women viz. mother, mother's sister, mother-in-law, maternal uncle's wife, father's sister, a wife of the paternal uncle or of a friend or of a pupil, a sister, sister's friend, daughter-in-law, daughter, the wife of one's Vedic teacher, a woman of the same gotra, one who has come for protection, a queen, an ascetic woman, one's wet-nurse, a woman performing a vrata and a brāhmaṇa woman, he becomes guilty of the sin of the violator of the guru's bed (i. e. incest). For that crime no other punishment is laid down except that of the cutting of the penis." These two, Yāj. and Nārada, show that excision of the organ and death are both the expiation and the punishment for this crime. The Mit. (on Yāj. III. 233) adds that this [[punish ment|punishment]] applies only to the offenders other than a [[brāhmana,|brāhmaṇa,]] since Manu [[(VIII, 380)|(VIII. 380)]] prescribes that a [[brāhmana|brāhmaṇa]] offender should not be punished with death whatever sin or crime he may have committed and that he is to be banished from the country unhurt and is to retain all his wealth. The [[Viṣnudharma-sūtra|Viṣṇudharma-sūtra]] [[(36 4-7)|(36. 4-7)]] adds a few more women to the list of Yāj. and Nārada (such as a woman in her monthly illness, the wife of a learned brāhmaṇa or of one's sacrificial priest or of one's Upādhyāya).

Having dealt at length with several classes or grades of sins, we have now to address ourselves to the question of the consequences of sins and the means recommended or adopted for removing sin and its consequences.

It has already been shown above how the sages of the [[Rgveda|Ṛgveda]] prayed to the gods, particularly to Aditi, Mitra, [[Varuna,|Varuṇa,]] the [[Adityas|Ādityas]] and Agni to free them from [[ūgas|āgas]] or enas etc. They often say that they violated the dharmas or the vratas (ordinances) of the gods and implore forgiveness. They were afraid of the consequences of their guilt, viz. the god's anger. They thought that when God was angered by their [[transgres sions|transgressions]] he smote them with adversity, destruction, disease and death. For example, they [[prey:|pray :]] 'whatever ordinance (vrata) of yours we may violate every day like subjects (in the case of a king's ordinances) do not subject us to your death-dealing blow' ([[Rg. I. 25.2);|(Ṛg. I. 25. 2);]] 'O [[Varuna!|Varuṇa!]] whatever transgression we men have committed towards the divine host and whatever rules of yours we may have confounded through heedlessness, do not, on [[P37]]account of that sin (enas), strike us down' ([[Rg. VII, 89.5)88.|(Ṛg. VII. 89. 5)[^88].]] In [[Rg. X. 89.8-9|Ṛg. X. 89. 8-9]] it is said that Indra hurls his sharp and deadly weapon at those who break the settled order (dharman) of Mitra, [[Varuna|Varuṇa]] and Aryaman. The gods are implored to save the worshipper from the deadly attacks of the wolf and from sinking into a pit (in adversity) in [[Rg. II. 29.6.|Ṛg. II. 29. 6.]] In [[Rg. IX. 73,8|Ṛg. IX. 73. 8]] it is said that the wise God has his eye on all worlds and he pierces in pits those who do not observe his ordinances and who are therefore not liked by [[him89.|him[^89].]] On the other hand, when God is pleased with the worshipper, he is implored to lead the latter by the path of virtue ([[Rg. I. 189.1),|(Ṛg. I. 189. 1),]] to confer happiness on the [[wor shipper's|worshipper's]] offspring ([[Rg. I. 189,2,IV.12,5)|(Ṛg. I. 189. 2, IV. 12. 5)]] and to bestow wealth on him ([[Rg. VIII, 45.40).|(Ṛg. VIII. 45. 40).]]

In the [[Rgveda|Ṛgveda]] the first means of removing the consequences of sin appear to be prayers for mercy and for the remission of sin as in [[Rg.|Ṛg.]] VII. 86. 4-5, VII. [[88, 6-7,|88. 6-7,]] VII. [[89.1-4|89. 1-4]] (where [[‘mrlā suksatra mrlaya'|'mṛḻā sukṣatra mṛḻaya']] is the refrain of all four verses). Even in the [[Rgveda|Ṛgveda]] water was supposed to remove sin, e. g. I. 23. 22 says 'O water! carry away whatever sin [[(durita)may|(durita) may]] exist in me'. This shows that sin was supposed to have physical effects like a disease, which were removed by [[water,|water.]] Sacrifices were offered to the gods in order to win their favour and in order that the sacrificer may be [[freedoo|freed[^90]]] from the consequences of even grave sins. The Tai. S. V. 3. [[12 1-2|12. 1-2]] and the [[Sat. Br. XIII, 3.1.1|Śat. Br. XIII. 3. 1. 1]] (S. B. E. vol. 44 p. 328) state that the gods redeemed themselves from all sin by means of this [[( Aśvamedha|(Aśvamedha]] sacrifice), they even redeemed themselves from the sin of brāhmaṇa murder. That a confession of sin was deemed to remove sin is illustrated in a striking way by what happened in the Varuṇapraghāsa (one of the [[cãturmāsya|cāturmāsya]] sacrifices). In [[that'l|that[^91]]] sacrifice the wife of the sacrificer had to confess whether she had any lovers and if she felt ashamed to [[P38]]reply in so many words she had only to raise up in her hand small pieces of grass blades to indicate the number of lovers, if any. If she confessed she had to recite the verse, Vāj. S. III. 44 and was allowed to take further part in the sacrifice. If she [[con cealed|concealed]] the truth, it was stated in the Tai. Br. that evil would [[be fall|befall]] her near and dear relatives (her son or husband). It is said by many European scholars who have written on the religion of the Veda that sacrifice became a sort of magic rite to wrest from gods or independently of them what the worshipper desired [[(e.g. see Keith's Religion and Philosophy of the Veda and Upanishads pp. 260-268).92|(e. g. see Keith's Religion and Philosophy of the Veda and Upanishads pp. 260-268)[^92].]] But this is an over-statement. When initiated for a sacrifice the sacrificer and his wife had to observe a fast or live on a low diet, both were called upon to observe many strict rules such as telling the truth, they had to make large preparations and spend wealth on the materials and the fees to the priests. These things were deemed to cleanse the mind and soul of the taint of sin. There is no reason to suppose that it was only the ritual that was thought to bring about the desired result (and not all the other matters referred to above).

We shall now discuss the prescriptions of the sūtras and [[smrtis|smṛtis]] about the consequences of sins.

In this connection the doctrines of Karma and Punarjanma must be well remembered. The details of the origin and [[develop ment|development]] of those doctrines will be discussed in a later [[section, Here|section. Here]] it is enough to state the few main propositions of the doctrine of Karma. The fundamental proposition is that cause and effect [[areas inseparably|are as inseparably]] linked in the moral sphere as assumed in the physical sphere by science. A good action has its reward and a bad act leads to retribution. If the bad actions do not yield their consequences at once or in this life, the soul begins another existence and in the new environment undergoes suffering for its [[past . bad|past bad]] deeds. The theory of karma and [[P39]]the theory of transmigration of souls (of pre-existence and post-existence) are inextricably mixed up in Indian thought from at least the ancient times of the [[Upanisads.|Upaniṣads.]] The general rule is that Karma, whether good or evil, cannot be got rid of, except by enjoying or undergoing its consequences. This is said by Gaut. 19. 5, [[Mārkandeya-purāṇa|Mārkaṇḍeya-purāṇa]] and many other works. 'Because the deed does not perish' [[Gaut. 93,|Gaut.[^93];]] 'whatever human action it may be, whether good or evil, it cannot be got rid of except by enjoying its consequences; know from me that a man gets rid of good and evil deeds by enjoying (their [[con sequences)'.|consequences)'.]] This doctrine is based on the [[Sat. Br. 7.2.2.27,|Śat. Br. V. 2. 2. 27,]] [[Br. Up. IV. 4|Bṛ. Up. IV. 4]] and VI. 2, Chān. Up. III. 14, and V. 3-10, [[Katha 5, 6-7|Kaṭha 5. 6-7]] and other Upaniṣad texts.
