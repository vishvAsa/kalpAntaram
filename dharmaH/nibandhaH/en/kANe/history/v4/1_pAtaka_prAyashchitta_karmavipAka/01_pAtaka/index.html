(Sins, expiations and the residual consequences of sins).

CHAPTER I

[[P1]][[20 The|The]] Article on sin in Hasting’s Encyclopædia of Religion and Ethics (vol. 11) will clearly show how the idea of sin varies from religion to religion, from age to age and from [[coun try|country]] to country. We are concerned here with the growth and [[developinent|development]] of the doctrine of sin in India from Vedic times to the medieval digests and commentaries on [[Dharmaśāstra, do|Dharmaśāstra.]]

[[1o Sin|Sin]] (pātaka) is a word that belongs to the terminology of religion rather than to that of [[Ethics. 1|Ethics.1]] It is very difficult to define sin. In a general way it may be said that it is an act which is regarded as a wilful rebellion against or disobedience of some law supposed to be laid down by God or Revelation; it is opposition to the will of God manifested in an authoritative work or at least failure to abide by the regulations contained therein.

[[P2]]The [[Rgveda|Ṛgveda]] is full of fervent and heart-felt expressions of the sense of sin and betrays intense desire on the part of the sages of old to be free from it. The idea of sin in the [[Rgveda|Ṛgveda]] is bound up with the conception of [[ta.|ṛta.]] The conception of [[rta|ṛta]] cannot be [[disoussed|discussed]] here in detail. But a brief statement is absolutely necessary in order to be able to convey what the Vedic doctrine of [[sin was.2|sin was.2]]

[[Mahāpātaki samsarga-Association|Mahāpātakisaṁsarga—Association]] with those who are guilty of the four mahāpātakas has already been described in H. of Dh. vol. III. pp. 614, 944. Gaut. [[21.3,|21. 3,]] Vas. I. 21-22, Manu XI. 180 (=[[śāntiparva|Śāntiparva]] 165. 37), Yāj. III. 261, Viṣṇu Dh. S. Bṛhaspati [[speaks 61|speaks61]] [[P26]]of nine kinds of saṁsarga (contact or association) out of which the first five were treated as light sins, but the other four were serious viz. occupying the same bed or seat, taking food in the same row with the sinner, cooking food in the sinner’s cooking pots or partaking of food prepared by him, being his sacrificial priest or employing him as one’s sacrificial priest, being the sinner’s teacher of the Veda or employing him as one’s teacher of the Veda, sexual intercourse, taking food with him in the same pot.

The Pr. Prakāśa (folio 74a) holds that saṁsarga is of three kinds, the highest, middling and lowest. The first includes four viz. yonisambandha (marriage), [[srauva|śrauva]] (i. e. that due to sacrificing for a sinner or making him a priest), [[maukcha|maukha]] (about learning or teaching Veda) and eating of food from the same vessel ([[ekāmatrabhojana);|(ekapātrabhojana);]] the middling is of five kinds [[viz,|viz.]] using the same vehicle, seat, bed or coverlet; eating in the same row and learning the Veda together [[( sahādhyayana).|(sahādhyayana).]] The lowest is of various other kinds such as intimate talks, touching, [[cook ing|cooking]] food in the same vessel, eating food at his house, receiving a gift from him &c. Adhyāpana in order to be a great sin must relate to the Veda, and so also yājana must relate to such Vedic sacrifices as [[Darsapūrnamāsa,|Darśapūrṇamāsa,]] Cāturmāsya, [[Agnistoma.|Agniṣṭoma.]] Helping a mahāpātakin to perform the five daily yajñas or teaching him the aṅgas (metrics, grammar etc.) and the śāstras is only a minor sin. [[Parāśara62|Parāśara62]] (XII. 79) states that sins are transferred (or spread) from man to man like a drop of oil on water by sitting or sleeping together or by using the same conveyance or by speaking with or dining in the same row. Devala and Chāgaleya q. by the Mit. on Yāj. [[III, 261,|III. 261,]] the [[Pr. Prakarana|Pr. Prakaraṇa]] (p. 101), the [[Pr, V.|Pr. V.]] (p. 145), the Par. M. (II. part 1. p. 28) and others contain similar verses. Acc. to [[Pr. Prakasa|Pr. Prakāśa]] (folio 75a), in order to become patita one must simultaneously practise these four together; if practised separately, there is no pātitya but only doṣa. Parāśara (I. 25-26) states that in the [[Křta|Kṛta]] age a man became patita by speaking with a patita, in Tretā by touching him, in Dvāpara by partaking of food prepared in his house and in Kali by actually committing a sinful act and that in the [[Kịta|Kṛta]] age a district was abandoned (if any one therein became patita), in Tretā the village, in Dvāpara the family (of the patita was abandoned) and in Kali only the actual perpetrator himself (is [[P27]]abandoned as [[palita).|patita).]] It is clear from this that such contacts with a patita as speaking with [[him,63|him,63]] touching him or eating food (in the house of a patita) were not regarded as involving a man in the same sin as that of the actual perpetrator. [[Speak ing,|Speaking,]] touching and eating are also actions ([[karman )|(karman)]] and Parāśara did not include them in the word ‘karmaṇā’ which refers only to the actual perpetration of a murder, surāpāna etc. Therefore when some writers include such contacts as eating or touching under [[‘karmaṇāl|‘karmaṇā’]] they put two meanings on the word karman in Parāśara viz. perpetration of a murder or the like and also doing some of the acts (touching, eating) which are forbidden to be done in reference to a perpetrator by some [[smrtis.|smṛtis.]] This is a procedure which is against the maxim of the [[Mimāṁsā|Mīmāṁsā]] that the same word conveys [[only 64|only64]] one sense in the same context. The reasonable inference from the words of Parāśara is that merely touching a mahāpātakin or eating food in his house did not involve the toucher and eater in pātitya.

In spite of this medieval writers gradually extended the scope of saṁsarga in a spirit of exclusiveness and of over-emphasis on ideas of ceremonial purity. For example, the [[Smartyarthasāra65|Smṛtyarthasāra65]] (p. 112) remarks that he who associates with the person that associates with a mahāpātakin has to undergo half the expiation that the first associator has to undergo. But that work does not go beyond this. The Mit. (on Yāj. III. 261) appears to hold that the associator even though he does not [[bo come|become]] patita is liable to undergo expiation and that even the 4th and 5th associators in a series are liable to undergo expiation [[P28]]though it is [[Jesser|lesser]] and [[lesser66.|lesser66.]] The Pr. Prakaraṇa (p. 109), Pr. V. (pp. 169-170) and the Pr. T. (p. 547) quote certain verses of Āpastamba and Vyāsa which very much widen the circle of [[samsarga.|saṁsarga.]] [[Apastamba-smrtion|Āpastamba-smṛti67]] (III. 1-3) states : ‘if a [[cāndāla|cāṇḍāla]] stays in the house of any one of the four castes without being known, the latter on [[conting|coming]] to know of the fact should undergo expiation, which is [[Candrāyana|Cāndrāyaṇa]] or Parāka for a member of the first three [[varnas|varṇas]] and Prājāpatya for a [[sūdra.|śūdra.]] Those who partook of cooked food in that man’s house should undergo [[Kịcchra;|Kṛcchra;]] one should prescribe one half of [[Kịcchra|Kṛcchra]] for those who took cooked food in the house of the 2nd associator and for those who partook of cooked food in the house of these last [[one fourth|one-fourth]] of [[Krochra|Kṛcchra]] is prescribed’. A few writers took a more reasonable view. The Par. M. Having dealt at length with several classes or grades of sins, we have now to address ourselves to the question of the consequences of sins and the means recommended or adopted for removing sin and its consequences.

It has already been shown above how the sages of the [[Rgveda|Ṛgveda]] prayed to the gods, particularly to Aditi, Mitra, [[Varuna,|Varuṇa,]] the [[Adityas|Ādityas]] and Agni to free them from [[ūgas|āgas]] or enas etc. They often say that they violated the dharmas or the vratas (ordinances) of the gods and implore forgiveness. They were afraid of the consequences of their guilt, viz. the god’s anger. They thought that when God was angered by their [[transgres sions|transgressions]] he smote them with adversity, destruction, disease and death. For example, they [[prey:|pray :]] ‘whatever ordinance (vrata) of yours we may violate every day like subjects (in the case of a king’s ordinances) do not subject us to your death-dealing blow’ ([[Rg. I. 25.2);|(Ṛg. I. 25. 2);]] ‘O [[Varuna!|Varuṇa!]] whatever transgression we men have committed towards the divine host and whatever rules of yours we may have confounded through heedlessness, do not, on [[P37]]account of that sin (enas), strike us down’ ([[Rg. VII, 89.5)88.|(Ṛg. VII. 89. 5)88.]] In [[Rg. X. 89.8-9|Ṛg. X. 89. 8-9]] it is said that Indra hurls his sharp and deadly weapon at those who break the settled order (dharman) of Mitra, [[Varuna|Varuṇa]] and Aryaman. The gods are implored to save the worshipper from the deadly attacks of the wolf and from sinking into a pit (in adversity) in [[Rg. II. 29.6.|Ṛg. II. 29. 6.]] In [[Rg. IX. 73,8|Ṛg. IX. 73. 8]] it is said that the wise God has his eye on all worlds and he pierces in pits those who do not observe his ordinances and who are therefore not liked by [[him89.|him89.]] On the other hand, when God is pleased with the worshipper, he is implored to lead the latter by the path of virtue ([[Rg. I. 189.1),|(Ṛg. I. 189. 1),]] to confer happiness on the [[wor shipper’s|worshipper’s]] offspring ([[Rg. I. 189,2,IV.12,5)|(Ṛg. I. 189. 2, IV. 12. 5)]] and to bestow wealth on him ([[Rg. VIII, 45.40).|(Ṛg. VIII. 45. 40).]]

In the [[Rgveda|Ṛgveda]] the first means of removing the consequences of sin appear to be prayers for mercy and for the remission of sin as in [[Rg.|Ṛg.]] VII. 86. 4-5, VII. [[88, 6-7,|88. 6-7,]] VII. [[89.1-4|89. 1-4]] (where [[‘mrlā suksatra mrlaya’|‘mṛḻā sukṣatra mṛḻaya’]] is the refrain of all four verses). Even in the [[Rgveda|Ṛgveda]] water was supposed to remove sin, e. g. I. 23. 22 says ‘O water! carry away whatever sin [[(durita)may|(durita) may]] exist in me’. This shows that sin was supposed to have physical effects like a disease, which were removed by [[water,|water.]] Sacrifices were offered to the gods in order to win their favour and in order that the sacrificer may be [[freedoo|freed90]] from the consequences of even grave sins. The Tai. S. V. 3. [[12 1-2|12. 1-2]] and the [[Sat. Br. XIII, 3.1.1|Śat. Br. XIII. 3. 1. 1]] (S. B. E. vol. 44 p. 328) state that the gods redeemed themselves from all sin by means of this [[( Aśvamedha|(Aśvamedha]] sacrifice), they even redeemed themselves from the sin of brāhmaṇa murder. That a confession of sin was deemed to remove sin is illustrated in a striking way by what happened in the Varuṇapraghāsa (one of the [[cãturmāsya|cāturmāsya]] sacrifices). In [[that’l|that91]] sacrifice the wife of the sacrificer had to confess whether she had any lovers and if she felt ashamed to [[P38]]reply in so many words she had only to raise up in her hand small pieces of grass blades to indicate the number of lovers, if any. If she confessed she had to recite the verse, Vāj. S. III. 44 and was allowed to take further part in the sacrifice. If she [[con cealed|concealed]] the truth, it was stated in the Tai. Br. that evil would [[be fall|befall]] her near and dear relatives (her son or husband). It is said by many European scholars who have written on the religion of the Veda that sacrifice became a sort of magic rite to wrest from gods or independently of them what the worshipper desired [[(e.g. see Keith’s Religion and Philosophy of the Veda and Upanishads pp. 260-268).92|(e. g. see Keith’s Religion and Philosophy of the Veda and Upanishads pp. 260-268)92.]] But this is an over-statement. When initiated for a sacrifice the sacrificer and his wife had to observe a fast or live on a low diet, both were called upon to observe many strict rules such as telling the truth, they had to make large preparations and spend wealth on the materials and the fees to the priests. These things were deemed to cleanse the mind and soul of the taint of sin. There is no reason to suppose that it was only the ritual that was thought to bring about the desired result (and not all the other matters referred to above).

We shall now discuss the prescriptions of the sūtras and [[smrtis|smṛtis]] about the consequences of sins.

In this connection the doctrines of Karma and Punarjanma must be well remembered. The details of the origin and [[develop ment|development]] of those doctrines will be discussed in a later [[section, Here|section. Here]] it is enough to state the few main propositions of the doctrine of Karma. The fundamental proposition is that cause and effect [[areas inseparably|are as inseparably]] linked in the moral sphere as assumed in the physical sphere by science. A good action has its reward and a bad act leads to retribution. If the bad actions do not yield their consequences at once or in this life, the soul begins another existence and in the new environment undergoes suffering for its [[past . bad|past bad]] deeds. The theory of karma and [[P39]]the theory of transmigration of souls (of pre-existence and post-existence) are inextricably mixed up in Indian thought from at least the ancient times of the [[Upanisads.|Upaniṣads.]] The general rule is that Karma, whether good or evil, cannot be got rid of, except by enjoying or undergoing its consequences. This is said by Gaut. 19. 5, [[Mārkandeya-purāṇa|Mārkaṇḍeya-purāṇa]] and many other works. ‘Because the deed does not perish’ [[Gaut. 93,|Gaut.93;]] ‘whatever human action it may be, whether good or evil, it cannot be got rid of except by enjoying its consequences; know from me that a man gets rid of good and evil deeds by enjoying (their [[con sequences)’.|consequences)’.]] This doctrine is based on the [[Sat. Br. 7.2.2.27,|Śat. Br. V. 2. 2. 27,]] [[Br. Up. IV. 4|Bṛ. Up. IV. 4]] and VI. 2, Chān. Up. III. 14, and V. 3-10, [[Katha 5, 6-7|Kaṭha 5. 6-7]] and other Upaniṣad texts.

Therefore they say, ‘a man is born again in that world for which he has worked.’ ‘According as a man acts and [[accord ing|according]] as he believes so will he be; a man of meritorious acts will be meritorious, a man of evil deeds sinful. He becomes pure by pure deeds and evil by evil deeds. And here they say that a person consists of desires. And as is his desire, so is his will ; and as is his will, so is his deed; and whatever deeds he does that he will reap’ ([[Br. Up. IV. 4. 5);|(Bṛ. Up. IV. 4. 5);]] ‘a man is made up of will; whatever he wills in this world the same he becomes after departing from this world’ (Chān. Up. III. 14. 1); ‘some human beings enter the womb in order to have an embodied [[existence:|existence;]] others go into inorganic matter (the stump of a tree and the like) according to their deeds and according to their knowledge’ ([[Katha Up. 5. 7). 94|(Kaṭha Up. 5. 7)94.]]

‘Actions done by a man do not perish unless they [[(i. e,| (i. e.]] their consequences) are enjoyed even after crores of years; (the fruits of) actions whether good or evil must of necessity be [[enjoyed95,|enjoyed95.’]]

[[P40]]Vide also Āp. Dh. S. II. 1. 2. [[2–7|2-7]] and [[Vispu|Viṣṇu]] Dh. S. XX. 47 ‘just as a calf finds out its own mother from among thousands of cows, so actions done in a former life unerringly reach the perpetrator thereof.’

But this general principle was modified in various ways by the [[smrtis|smṛtis]] and later writings. Gautama [[19.1196|19. 1196]] (=[[Vasistha 22.8)|Vasiṣṭha 22. 8)]] states: ‘Reciting the Veda inaudibly (japa), austerities, a sacrifice, fasting and giving gifts are the means of expiating that [[(i.e.|(i. e.]] a blamable act).’ Vas. 20. 47 and [[25.3|25. 3]] lay down [[‘A.|‘A]] sinner is freed from sin by tormenting his body, by austerities, by reciting the Veda and also by making gifts’ and ’those who are [[constant ly|constantly]] engaged in [[prānāyāmas|prāṇāyāmas]] (suppression and regulation of breath), reciting purificatory texts, giving gifts, offering sacrifices, muttering sacred texts will undoubtedly be freed from sins’. Manu [[III.|XI.]] 227 observes: ‘by confession, by repentance, by austerity, by reciting Vedic texts (sacred [[Gāyatri|Gāyatrī]] or other texts), a sinner is freed from guilt and in case of difficulty (i. e. if he is unable to perform austerities or to recite Vedic texts) by liberality’. Similar provisions occur in [[Parāsara|Parāśara]] X. 40, Śātātapa [[T.|I.]] 4, Saṁvarta 203, [[Harita|Hārīta]] [[(q,| (q.]] in Pr. T. p. 467), [[Yama, (q,|Yama (q.]] in Pr. V. pp. 30, [[31 ),|31),]] [[Bhavisyapurāṇa|Bhaviṣyapurāṇa]] (q. by Pr. V. p. 31).

Before proceeding with the subject of prāyaścittas we shall speak briefly about the other means of reducing the [[con sequences|consequences]] of sin. And first comes confession. With reference to the confession of paramours by the wife in the [[Varunapraghāsa,|Varuṇapraghāsa,]] the Tai. Br. [[(I. 6.5. 2 )97|(I. 6. 5. 2)97]] says ‘He makes the wife confess, [[there by|thereby]] he renders her pure and then he leads her to penance’. The [[Sat. Br. (II. 5. 2. 20)|Śat. Br. (II. 5. 2. 20)]] puts it in this form [[for|‘for]] when confessed the sin becomes less, since it becomes truth’. This was a confession before God (Fire) and men [[(the priests )|(the priests)]] to restore one to divine forgiveness and favour. As regards several lapses, confession was part of the procedure prescribed for atoning for the sin.
