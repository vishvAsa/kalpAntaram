RĀJADHARMA 

( GOVERNMENT AND STATECRAFT) 

CHAPTER I 

[[P1]][[Rajadharma|Rājadharma]] has been a subject of discussion in works on [[dharmasastra|dharmaśāstra]] from very ancient times. The Āp. Dh. S. II. 9.25.1 says [[“The|'The]] general and special [[dharnus|dharmas]] of all varṇas have been explained: but now we shall declare the dharmas of a king in [[particular!|particular'.]] Āp. then states that the king should cause to be built a capital and a palace, the gates of which both should face the south; that the palace should be in the heart of the capital; that in front of the capital there was to be a hall called [['aman trana'|'āmantraṇa']] (hall of invitation); that to the south of the town there was to be an assembly house with doors on the north and south. Āp. requires that in the king's realm no brāhmaṇa should suffer hunger, sickness, cold or heat; [[Ap.|Āp.]] gives regulations about the assembly house and about persons who were to be allowed to engage in gambling; he prescribes that [[the exercise in)|(the exercise in)]] arms, dancing, singing and music should not go on anywhere except in the houses of the king's servants, that the king was to see that there was no danger from thieves in villages and forests, that the king should donate fields and money to brāhmaṇas according to their worth without detriment to his servants, that the king who meets death in recovering the property of [[brāh mapas|brāhmaṇas]] and other warlike persons who meet death in fighting for a similar worthy cause reap the merit of a sacrifice [[(i, e,| (i. e.]] reach heaven as reward); that the king should appoint in towns and [[Villages|villages]] officers and their subordinates, who are pure and [[truth ful,|truthful,]] for the protection of the subjects against thieves and who are to be made to make good what is stolen; that they are to collect lawful taxes for the king from the people except from learned brāhmaṇas, women of all castes, minors, students [[stay. ing|staying]] with teachers and ascetics fulfilling the rules of their order, [[sūdras|śūdras]] who do menial work for the higher castes (such as washing their feet), persons that are blind, deaf or dumb. [[Ap.|Āp.]] then lays down punishments for adultery and rape, for slander and defamation, for manslaughter, for theft and appropriation of another's land, for breach of contract to cultivate the field of another, for a [[herdeman|herdsman]] leaving his herd or negligently allowing [[P2]]it to perish. He states that if the king does not punish the guilty he incurs sin, that in disputes judges should be men of learning, clever and fulfilling their duties, that witnesses were to tell the truth and were to be punished if they be untruthful. It will be noticed how [[Ap.|Āp.]] briefly covers the whole field of [[rājam dharma.|rājadharma.]] In the [[Sāntiparva|Śāntiparva]] of the Mahābhārata rājadharma is dealt with at great length in chapters 56-130 and to some extent in [[131-17%.|131-172.]] The [[Manusmrti|Manusmṛti]] also states at the beginning of chap. VII (1) that it will expound rājadharmas. That great literary activity on the science and art of government went on for many centuries before the Christian era follows from several considerations. The Anuśāsanaparva (chap. 39.8) speaks of the śāstras composed by [[Bphaspati|Bṛhaspati]] and [[Usanas.|Uśanas.]] The [[Santi parva|Śāntiparva]] (58.1-3) names as expounders of [[rājaśūstra|rājaśāstra]] [[Bphaspati|Bṛhaspati]], Bharadvāja, Gauraśiras, Kāvya, Mahendra, Manu [[Pracetasa|Prācetasa]] and [[Visalākṣa.|Viśālākṣa.]] [[Sāntiparva|Śāntiparva]] (102.31-32) opposes the view of Śambara to that of [[acāryas|ācāryas]][^1]. The Arthaśāstra of [[Kautilya|Kauṭilya]] mentions five schools (viz. those of the Mānavas, the Bārhaspatyas, the [[Ausa nasas,|Auśanasas,]] the [[Pārāsaras|Pārāśaras]] and the [[Ambhiyas|Āmbhiyas]]), seven individual [[tea chers|teachers]] (V. 5, and I. 8) only once (viz. [[Bāhudantiputra|Bāhudantīputra]], [[Dirgha|Dīrgha]] Cārāyaṇa, [[Ghotamukha|Ghoṭamukha]], [[Kaṇinka|Kaṇiṅka]] Bhāradvāja, Kātyāyana, [[Kin jalka|Kiñjalka]], [[Pigunaputra,)|Piśunaputra,)]] and several times mentions the views of Bhāradvāja, Kauṇapadanta, Parāśara, [[Pisuna|Piśuna]], Vātavyādhi, Viśālākṣa. [[Kautilya|Kauṭilya]] also cites at least 53 times the views of [[acāryas|ācāryas]], almost in every case for dissent. [[Sānti|Śānti]] 103.44 refers to a [[Bhāsya|Bhāṣya]] on [[rājasāstra.|rājaśāstra.]] Is it possible to see in this a reference to one out of the [[bhāsyakāras|bhāṣyakāras]] indicated in the verse appended at the end of the [[Kauṭiliya|Kauṭilīya]] or to the Kauṭilīya itself? Another fact indicative of the [[systematiza tion|systematization]] of the science of government is that in the Mahābhārata, the Rāmāyaṇa, Manu and [[Kautilya|Kauṭilya]] ideas expressed by numbers had already been stereotyped long before those works were written viz. such as [[saptāóga|saptāṅga]] rājya (State with seven constituent [[elements ),|elements),]] [[ṣadguṇya|ṣāḍguṇya]] (the six ways of policy viz. alliance, declaration of war &c.), three śaktis, the four upāyas (sāma, [[dana|dāna]], bheda, [[danda|daṇḍa]]), [[astavarga,|aṣṭavarga,]] and pañcavarga (Manu VII. 155), the 18 and 15 [[tirthas|tīrthas]] (Sabhāparva 5.38)[^2]. For detailed information [[P3]]about the Rājaśāstras of [[Bșhaspati|Bṛhaspati]], Uśanas, Bhāradvāja culled from the [[Mahabharata|Mahābhārata]] and other ancient works vide my paper on them in the Journal of the University of Bombay, vol. XI part 2 pp. 73-83, 1942.

The fulfilment of their duties and responsibilities by rulers was of paramount importance to the stability and orderly [[develop ment|development]] of society and to the happiness of individuals in the State and therefore one often finds that rājadharma is said to be the root of or the quintessence of all dharmas. [[Santiparva|Śāntiparva]] (63.25) states [[* know|'know]] that all dharmas are merged in rājadharma; that rājadharmas are at the head of all dharmas' and (141.9-10) [[* the welfare|'the welfare]], good rains, sickness, calamities and death among people owe their origin to the king[^3].' It was the king's duty to see that the people in his kingdom acted according to the rules laid down in the [[smrtis|smṛtis]] for the several varṇas and [[āsramas,|āśramas,]] to administer justice and to interfere when his help was sought for by a [[( assembly|(assembly]] of learned men) in enforcing the [[prāyaścitla|prāyaścitta]] (penance) prescribed for various lapses. Vide Gautama [[x.|X.]] 7-8, XI. 9-11, [[Ap.|Āp.]] Dh. S. II. 5. 10. 13-16[^4], [[Vasistha|Vasiṣṭha]] 19. 1-2, [[Vispu|Viṣṇu]] III. 2-3, [[Narada|Nārada]] ([[prakirnaka,|prakīrṇaka,]] 5-7 and 33-34), [[Santiparva|Śāntiparva]] 77.33 and 57.15, [[Matsyapurana|Matsyapurāṇa]] 215.63, [[Markandeya purāṇa|Mārkaṇḍeyapurāṇa]] 27.28 and 28.36 and H. Dh. vol. II. p. 965. Therefore it may be said with truth (as done by the Mahābhārata) that rājadharma was the highest goal of all the world, that it [[com prehended|comprehended]] within itself all rules of ācāra, vyavahāra ([[admini stration|administration]] of justice) and [[prayascitta|prāyaścitta]] (penance). It is on account of this all-pervading influence of government or royal power that the Mahābhārata frequently emphasizes that the king is the maker of his age, that it is he who can usher a golden age or an age of strife and misery for the country[^5]. The [[Sukranitisara|Śukranītisāra]] (I. [[P4]][[59-60 )|59-60)]] remarks that one should learn from [[sastra|śāstra]] what are good and evil actions, give up evil deeds and perform good ones and that the king is hence declared to be the cause of (good or evil) times according as he does good or evil acts. [[Sukra IV.1, 60|Śukra IV. 1. 60]] states that the king is the prompter of his age.

Though rājadharma was thus an integral part of [[dharma śāstra|dharmaśāstra]] and was one of the most important subjects therein, yet apart from the works on dharmaśāstra separate treatises dealing with rājadharma alone came into existence in very early times. [[Santiparva|Śāntiparva]] (chap. 59) states that originally in the [[Kr̥ta|Kṛta]] age there was no king nor punishment, that then moha (delusion), greed and lust entered men, that in order to provide against the complete destruction of dharma, [[Brahma|Brahmā]] composed a work in one hundred thousand chapters on dharma, artha, [[kānia|kāma]] and mokṣa (verses 30 and 79); that part of this work on [[Niti|Nīti]] (science of government) was abridged by [[Śhaṅkara Vitālākṣa|Śaṅkara Viśālākṣa]] (verse 80, and so it was called [[Vaigalaksa|Vaiśālākṣa]]) into 10000 chapters, that Indra studied it and reduced it to 5000 chapters (and the work was called [[Bahudantaka|Bāhudantaka]], verse 83), that it was compressed into 3000 chapters by [[Bșhaspati|Bṛhaspati]] (and so was called Bārhaspatya) and that Kāvya (Uśanas) reduced the work to 1000 chapters. The Kāmasūtra (I. 5-8) contains a somewhat similar story that Prajāpati composed a work in one hundred thousand chapters, that Manu abridged it as regards dharma, Bṛhaspati as regards artha and Nandi abridged in 1000 chapters the science of erotics. The [[Sāntiparva|Śāntiparva]] (69) gives (verses 33-74) [[& summary|a summary]] of the contents of the work of [[Brahma|Brahmā]] on rājadharma which [[remarkab ly|remarkably]] agrees with the principal topics of the [[Kauṭiliya.|Kauṭilīya.]]

The [[Nitiprakāśikā|Nītiprakāśikā]] (I. 21-22) states that [[Brahma,|Brahmā,]] Maheśvara, Skanda, Indra, Prācetasa Manu, [[Brhaspati,|Bṛhaspati,]] [[Sukra,|Śukra,]] Bhāradvāja, Veda-Vyāsa, [[Gaurasirag|Gauraśiras]] were the expounders of Rājaśāstra, that [[Brahma|Brahmā]] composed a work on rājaśāstra in 100000 chapters, which was gradually reduced in size by each of the above mentioned founders until [[Gaurasiras|Gauraśiras]] reduced it to 500 chapters and Vyāsa to 300. The [[Sukranitisāra|Śukranītisāra]] (I. 2-4) states that [[Brahma|Brahmā]] composed [[nitiśāstra|nītiśāstra]] in 100000 verses, which was subsequently abridged by [[Vasiṣtha|Vasiṣṭha]] and others (including [[Sukra|Śukra]]).

It is interesting to note the names given to the science of government. The most appropriate word is [[rājaśāstra'|'rājaśāstra']] and it is employed by the [[Mahabharata|Mahābhārata]], which speaks of [[Bphaspati|Bṛhaspati]], Bharadvāja and others as [['rājaśāstra-pranetāraḥ'|'rājaśāstra-praṇetāraḥ']] (vide p. [[%|2]] above). The [[Nitiprakaśika|Nītiprakāśikā]] (I. 21-22) also dubs the divine and human [[P5]]writers on government [['rājaśāstrāṇām. pranetāraḥ.'|'rājaśāstrāṇāṃ praṇetāraḥ.']] The same word is employed by such ancient classical writers as Aśvaghoṣa in his Buddhacarita (I. 46)[^6]. The first verse in Prof. Edgerton's reconstructed Pañcatantra performs obeisance to Manu, [[Bṛhas pati|Bṛhaspati]], [[Sukra|Śukra]], Parāśara and his son and [[Canakya|Cāṇakya]] as the authors of nṛpaśāstra (science of kingship). Another name is [[Dandaniti.|Daṇḍanīti.]] The [[Sāntiparva|Śāntiparva]] (59. 78) explains why [[Dandaniti|Daṇḍanīti]] is so called viz. [[this|'this]] world is led (on to the right path) by [[danda|daṇḍa]] (the power of punishment, sanction) or this science carries (or sets forth) the rod of punishment; hence it is called [[dandaniti|daṇḍanīti]] and it (faces) pervades the three worlds.' In [[śāntiparva 69.76|Śāntiparva 69. 76]] it is stated that [['Dandaniti|'Daṇḍanīti]] controls the four varṇas so as to lead them on to the performance of their duties and when it is employed by the ruler properly, it makes them desist from adharma.'[^7] [[Santi|Śānti]] 63. 28 identifies [[Dandaniti|Daṇḍanīti]] with [[rajadharmas.|rājadharmas.]] The [[Kautiliya|Kauṭilīya]] (I. 4) explains [['Dagda|'Daṇḍa]] is the means of the stability and welfare of [[Anviksiki,|Ānvīkṣikī,]] [[Trayı|Trayī]] (the three Vedas) and Vārtā; the rules that treat of [[danda|daṇḍa]] are called [[dapdaniti,|daṇḍanīti,]] which is a means of acquiring what has not been acquired, which safeguards what is acquired, which increases what is guarded and distributes (increased wealth) among the [[deserv ing|deserving]][^8]. The Mahābhārata says ([[Santi|Śānti]] 69. 102) that a wise [[ksatriya|kṣatriya]], putting [[dandaniti|daṇḍanīti]] in front, should always desire to acquire what is till then unacquired and should guard what is acquired[^9]. The [[Nitisāra|Nītisāra]] (II. 15) says that dama (control or chastisement) is called [[danda,|daṇḍa,]] the king is called [['danda'|'daṇḍa']] because control is centered in him; that the [[niti|nīti]] (rules) of [[danda|daṇḍa]] is called [[danda niti|daṇḍanīti]] and [[niti|nīti]] is so called because it leads (people).'[^10] [[Sānti(69.|Śānti (69.]] 104) says that [[dandaniti|daṇḍanīti]] is the special concern of the [[ksatriya|kṣatriya]] (rājan). It is said in the Vanaparva 150. 32 that without [[Dapdaniti|Daṇḍanīti]] this [[P6]]whole world would break all bounds. Vide also [[Sānti|Śānti]] [[15,29,|15.29,]] 63.28, 69.74 ff (eulogy of [[Dandaniti|Daṇḍanīti]]). [[Dandaniti|Daṇḍanīti]] is said to be the support of the world ([[Sānti|Śānti]] 121.24) and to have been produced by [[Devi Sarasvati|Devī Sarasvatī]] ([[Santi|Śānti]] 122.25.)

Arthaśāstra has been a synonym for [[dapdaniti.|daṇḍanīti.]] When the Āp. Dh. S. II. 5.10.16 requires the king to appoint as purohita a brāhmaṇa well-versed in dharma and artha, it is clear that [[Apastamba|Āpastamba]] has in view dharmaśāstra and arthaśāstra. The Anuśāsanaparva says that [[Bphaspati|Bṛhaspati]] and others composed [[artha sāstras|arthaśāstras]] (39.10-11). [[Dronaparva 6,1|Droṇaparva 6.1]] speaks of a science of artha composed by Manu (Mānavī arthavidyā). Vide [[Jayagwal|Jayaswal]] in [[Manu|'Manu]] and Yājñavalkya' (pp. 5, 7, 16, 25, 26, 41, 42, 50, 84, for Manu and Artha). [[Sānti|Śānti]] (71.14) states that a king whose sole concern is with arthaśāstra does not secure dharma and kāma and that all the wealth of such a king vanishes in (the pursuit of) improper objects. Vide [[Sānti 30%. 109|Śānti 302. 109]] which speaks of [[artha sāstra|arthaśāstra]] as resorted to by the best among kings. The [[Rāmāyana|Rāmāyaṇa]] (II. [[100.14)|100. 14)]] states that Sudhanvā, Upādhyāya of Rāma, was an adept in arthaśāstra. The [[arthasāstra|arthaśāstra]] of [[Kautilya|Kauṭilya]] starts with the statement that it is the quintessence of all the [[arthagāstras|arthaśāstras]] composed by former teachers in the whole world and at the end [[Kautilya|Kauṭilya]] states that that [[sāstra|śāstra]] which is a means of acquiring and guarding the earth is arthaśāstra. This mentions two of the four objects of [[dandaniti|daṇḍanīti]] stated in the [[Kauṭiliya|Kauṭilīya]] itself (as shown in [[n.8|n. 8]] above). Only two objects are mentioned by [[Kautilya|Kauṭilya]] (as in [[Santi|Śānti]] 69. 102) since they are the first and principal ones to be gained by the practice of the science of government. It is not meant that there was, as regards the topics to be dealt with, any [[diṣtinction|distinction]] between [[Dandaniti|Daṇḍanīti]] and [[Arthagāstra.|Arthaśāstra.]] The four objects mentioned by [[Kautilya|Kauṭilya]] are always placed before the [[ksatriya|kṣatriya]] by Manu (VII. 99-100), [[śānti|Śānti]] 102. 57 and 140. 5, [[Yaj.|Yāj.]] I. 317, [[Nitisāra|Nītisāra]] I. 18 (while I. 8 mentions only two purposes of [[rājavidya|rājavidyā]]). At the end (XV. 1) [[Kautilya|Kauṭilya]] [[states' artha|states 'artha]] is the sustenance of human beings, that is, the earth peopled by [[men That gāstra|men. That śāstra]] which is a means of acquiring and guarding that earth is [[arthasāstra.'|arthaśāstra.']] Men derive their sustenance from the earth and all wealth also arises from the earth. Mr. Jayaswal [[( Hindu Polity|(Hindu Polity]] p. 5) is not right when he [[translates' Artha|translates 'Artha]] is human population.' Vide [[V. Ghoshal's|U. Ghoshal's]] 'Hindu Political Theories' p. 74 n and p. 76 for the various interpretations of this passage by Jayaswal and others. Authors a few centuries later than both the [[Mahabharata|Mahābhārata]] and the [[Kauṭiliya|Kauṭilīya]] treat the two as [[P7]]identical. [[Daṇdin|Daṇḍin]] in his [[Dasakumaracarita|Daśakumāracarita]] (VIII.) expressly says that Viṣṇugupta composed his work on [[dandaniti|daṇḍanīti]] for the Maurya king in 6000 ślokas, though the [[Kautillya|Kauṭilīya]] states at the very beginning that the work is an arthaśāstra. [[Dandin|Daṇḍin]] in the same context refers to 'Arthaśāstras as helping to polish the intellect' and speaks of some of the predecessors mentioned by [[Kautilya|Kauṭilya]] as writers on the śāstra (viz. the Arthaśāstra)[^11]. The Amarakośa treats the two as identical[^12]. Medhātithi on Manu VII. 43 holds that the word [['Dandaniti'|'Daṇḍanīti']] therein refers to the works of [[Canakya|Cāṇakya]] and others. The [[Mitāksarā or.|Mitākṣarā on]] Yāj. I. 313 paraphrases [[dandaniti|daṇḍanīti]] by [[arthasāstra|arthaśāstra]] and on [[Yaj.|Yāj.]] I. 311 explains [[dandanīti|daṇḍanīti]] as the lore useful in the acquisition and guarding of wealth (only the two objects mentioned by [[Kautilya|Kauṭilya]] in [[connec tion|connection]] with [[arthasāstra|arthaśāstra]]). According to the [[Sukranitisāra|Śukranītisāra]] (IV. 3.56) [[that|'that]] is said to be arthaśāstra in which instruction about the conduct of kings and the like is given without coming [[in.|in]] conflict with [[Sruti|Śruti]] and Smṛti and in which the acquisition of wealth with great skill is taught'.

The words arthaśāstra and [[dandaniti|daṇḍanīti]] are applied to the science of government from two different points of view. Artha is defined in the [[Kāmasutra|Kāmasūtra]] (I. 20) as 'education, lands, gold, cattle, corn, domestic utensils and friends and the augmenting of what is acquired' (vide H. Dh. Vol. II part I. p. 9 note 22). Therefore when wealth and prosperity of all kinds is the spring and motive of giving a name the science treating of these is called arthaśāstra and when the government of the people and the punishment of offenders are the main ideas the same is called [[dapdaniti.|daṇḍanīti.]] Though works like [[Kautilya's|Kauṭilya's]] Arthaśāstra place a high value on dharma they are principally concerned with the treatment of central and local government, taxation, the employment of [[sama|sāma]] and other upāyas, with alliances and wars, appointment of officers and punishment. Therefore [[Artha gāstra|Arthaśāstra]] is mainly what is called [[drṣtartha smrti,|dṛṣṭārtha smṛti,]] as stated by the Bhaviṣyapurāṇa (quoted by Aparārka p. 626, the Smṛticandrikā p. 24, vyavahāra and [[Viramitrodaya,|Vīramitrodaya,]] paribhāṣā p. 19). [[Medha tithi|Medhātithi]] on Manu VII. 1 explains that dharma in that verse means [[P8]][[* duties'|'duties']] (dharmaśabdaḥ kartavyatāvacanaḥ), that the duties of the king are either [[drstārtha|dṛṣṭārtha]] (i. e. the effects of which are worldly and visible) such as the employment of six guṇas (sandhi, vigraha &c.) or [[adrstārtha|adṛṣṭārtha]] (that have no visible effect but have a spiritual result) such as Agnihotra; here (in chap. VII-IX of Manu) the remarks relate principally to [[drstārtha|dṛṣṭārtha]] matters since it is well-known that the word rājadharma is [[appli cable|applicable]] to such matters (the six guṇas &c.) only[^13]. Medhātithi here declares that the rules of [[rājaniti|rājanīti]] are not based on the canonical books of dharmaśāstra but principally on the [[ex perience|experience]] of worldly affairs.

The relation of [[arthagāstra|arthaśāstra]] to dharmaśāstra has now to be understood. As stated above rājadharma is a very important subject of dharma-śāstra. [[Arthasāstra|Arthaśāstra]] which is principally concerned with the rights, privileges and [[res ponsibilities|responsibilities]] of the ruler is therefore properly speaking a part of dharmaśāstra. As shown above it is supposed to have [[P9]]like dharmaśāstra a divine source. But works on [[arthasāstra|arthaśāstra]] enter into great details about the government of a country in all its aspects, while dharmaśāstra works generally deal only with a few salient features of rājaśāstra. Just as the [[Kamasutra|Kāmasūtra]] (I. 2. 14) states that dharma is the highest goal and kāma is the lowest of the three puruṣārthas (vide H. Dh. vol. II part 1 p. 9), so Arthaśāstra also sets the highest value on dharma. The [[Kauṭiliya|Kauṭilīya]] states (III. 1. at end)[^16] 'In any matter where there is a conflict between dharmaśāstra and practices or between dharmaśāstra and any secular transaction, (the king) should decide that matter by relying on dharma. If śāstra comes in conflict with any rational and equitable rule then the latter shall be the deciding factor and the (strict) letter of the text will be nowhere'. But [[Kautilya|Kauṭilya]] and other writers on [[artha Bāstra|arthaśāstra]] lay the greatest emphasis on artha. In the eager and relentless pursuit of worldly prosperity means may be recommended or followed which may come in [[confict|conflict]] with the strictly ethical standpoint of the dictates of dharmaśāstra. In such cases of conflict dharmaśāstra works lay down (e. g. [[Ap.|Āp.]] Dh. [[1.9.|I. 9.]] 24. 23, [[Yaj.|Yāj.]] II. 21, Nārada, [[vyavahāramātrkā,|vyavahāramātṛkā]] chap. I 39) that dharmaśāstra rules are to be preferred to the dictates of [[arthasāstra.|arthaśāstra.]] Viśvarūpa (on Yāj. II. 21) states that according to some predecessors of his the conflict between the dictates of dharmaśāstra and [[arthasāstra|arthaśāstra]] is illustrated as follows. Manu [[VII.|VIII.]] 351 (which is the same as Viṣṇu-dharmasūtra V. 190 and Matsyapurāṇa 227. 116-117) when dealing with vyavahāra (a subject that pre-eminently belongs to [[arthasāstra )|arthaśāstra)]] provides that in killing an [[ātalāyin,|ātatāyin,]] no fault attaches to the [[killer:|killer;]] while Manu XI. 89 in the chapter on [[prayascittas|prāyaścittas]] (which is [[pre eminently|pre-eminently]] a topic of dharmaśāstra) states that no penance is prescribed (i. e. there is no prāyaścitta for removing the guilt) when a person of set purpose kills a brāhmaṇa. The result is that the latter rule prevails and sin is incurred by killing a brāhmaṇa even if the latter be an [[atatāyin|ātatāyin]] (though there may be no punishment by the king). Viśvarūpa does not accept [[P10]]this illustration. The [[Mitakṣarā|Mitākṣarā]] also refers to this example, disapproves of it and gives its own example. The [[Arthasāstra|Arthaśāstra]] declares that a king should endeavour to secure friends, since the acquisition of friends is superior to the acquisition of gold and land (as is laid down in Yāj. I. 352). The rule of [[dharma Gāstra|dharmaśāstra]] is that a king has to dispense justice, being free from anger and avarice and in accordance with dharmaśāstra. [[There fore|Therefore]] when a suit comes before a king he must decide it according to law, even though he may lose the friendship of a person if his decision goes against the latter. The [[Viramitrodaya|Vīramitrodaya]] follows the Mitākṣarā. Dharmaśāstra went by the name of [[smrti|smṛti]] (Manu II. 10), while [[arthasāstra|arthaśāstra]] was treated as an Upaveda. The Viṣṇupurāṇa III. 6. 28, Vāyu [[61.79|61. 79]] and [[Brahmānda|Brahmāṇḍa]] 35. 88-89 mention the four Upavedas[^17] viz. [[Ayurveda,|Āyurveda,]] Dhanurveda, [[Gandharvaveda|Gāndharvaveda]] and [[Arthasāstra|Arthaśāstra]] as [[afiliated|affiliated]] to the four Vedas respectively viz. [[Rgveda,|Ṛgveda,]] Yajurveda, Sāmaveda and Atharvaveda. [[Kautilya|Kauṭilya]] ([[15|I. 5]]) states that the king should listen to the recital of [[itihāsu|itihāsa]] in the latter part of the day and includes both [[dharmasastra|dharmaśāstra]] and arthaśāstra under [[itibāsa'|itihāsa]][^18] and requires the minister [[know ing|knowing]] arthaśāstra to advise the king (with examples and precepts derived) from itihāsa and [[purāṇa. .|purāṇa.]]

Though Arthaśāstra is in theory for keeping to the path of dharma, one cannot blink one's eyes to the fact that the [[Maha bhārata|Mahābhārata]] and the [[Kauṭiliya|Kauṭilīya]] both support in several places the adoption of means entirely divorced from all rules of fair [[deal ing|dealing]] and morality. In [[Adiparva|Ādiparva]] 140 we have a discourse by Kaṇika said to have been a mantrin of [[Dhrtarāstra|Dhṛtarāṣṭra]] and a [[pro found|profound]] student of Rājaśāstra, which is almost on the same lines as [[Santiparva|Śāntiparva]] chap. 140 where [[Bhisma|Bhīṣma]] sets out what lines of policy [[(niti )|(nīti)]] should be followed by the ruler in adversity and how he should give up all thoughts of kindliness in such straits. He is supposed to give the substance of the dialogue between the sage Bhāradvāja and [[Satruntapa,|Śatruntapa,]] king of [[Sauvira.|Sauvīra.]] The whole chapter is full of Machiavellian advice. A few [[speci mens|specimens]] may be set out here. One should be very courteous in speech, but like a razor at heart [[( verse 13);|(verse 13);]] one desirous of prosperity may fold one's hands, may take an oath, may use [[P11]]sweet words, place his head on another's feet and even shed tears, one may carry on one's shoulders one's enemy till one accomplishes one's object and when a fit opportunity arises should break him like an earthen pot on a stone (17-18); the king should raise hopes promising their fulfilment at a distant day, but should put obstacles in the fulfilment of the hopes raised in others (32); when one is reduced to a wretched state one may extricate oneself from it by any act whatever whether gentle or horrible and practise dharma after one becomes able to do so (38); one should inspire confidence in one's enemy by some means which appear to be true (to the enemy), but one should strike him down at the right moment when he takes a wrong step (44); whoever causes obstacles to the purpose of the king, whether son or brother or father or friend, should be killed by the king who desires to prosper (47); without cutting the vitals of others, without committing horrible deeds and without killing indiscriminately as the fisherman in the case of fish one does not secure high prosperity (50); when about to strike one should utter sweet words and even after striking one should use [[honoyed|honeyed]] words; even after severing another's head with a sword one should lament and shed tears (54); if some remainder is left in the case of a debt or fire or enemies, there is a tendency to grow and so no remainder should be left of these (58). It is to be noted that this chapter deals specially with what should be done in difficulties and [[can not|cannot]] be supposed to be the real teaching of the Mahābhārata which is generally in line with the higher code of [[dharma sastra.|dharmaśāstra.]] It embodied probably the words of earlier arthaśāstra writers like Bhāradvāja. After hearing this discourse on calculated cruelty and deceit [[Yudhisthira 'stood|Yudhiṣṭhira stood]] aghast and frankly told [[Bhisma|Bhīṣma]] that the course of conduct outlined by him did not differ from that of robbers and that he felt bewildered and would not exert himself in the way pointed out. In chap. 142 [[Bhisma|Bhīṣma]] replies to this by saying that he did not base his [[dis course|discourse]] merely on the sacred texts, but also on reason and the essence was distilled by wise men, that the considered rule of conduct for the king is twofold, viz. straightforward and crooked, that one should not ordinarily employ the crooked course of conduct, but should employ it as against an enemy who employs a crooked policy and effectively meet him by such policy. He further says that rājadharma cannot be based on a single source [[(v.7)|(v. 7)]] and that policies are to be pursued not merely by reliance on the sacred codes of law but on reason as [[well12|well]] [[P12]][[(v. 17 )|(v. 17)]] and that Indra himself has declared this rule which he derived from [[Bphaspati.|Bṛhaspati.]] In [[Sānti-parva|Śānti-parva]] a weak king is advised to harass a very powerful ruler's state by sword, fire and poison. In chap. 130 it is stated that the preservation of the state is the paramount consideration and that a king whose treasury is depleted and who is therefore in [[diffi culties|difficulties]] may replenish his treasury even by harassing his subjects (except tāpasas and learned brāhmaṇas). In chap. 138 it is said that self-interest makes friends or foes and that aviśvāsa (putting trust in no one) is the grand line of policy for kings. So it is clear that the Mahābhārata was prepared to give up in certain circumstances the strict rules of dharmaśāstra for kings and to allow them to pursue devious modes of action that were far from moral i. e. it tries to effect a synthesis of dharmaśāstra and arthaśāstra.

The [[Kautillya|Kauṭilīya]] also discloses how arthaśāstra writers did not shrink from giving advice which, to say the least, was most cruel, selfish and immoral. For example, in the section called [[' guarding the prince'( Kauṭ. I. 17 )|'guarding the prince' (Kauṭ. I. 17)]] the views of several teachers are set out. Bhāradvāja says that princes are of the nature of crabs eating up their parent; so it is better to finish them in secret when they have no love for their father. Viśālākṣa abhorred this advice as cruel, as unsanctioned murder and as destructive of the seed of [[ksatriyas|kṣatriyas]] and recommended that it was better to keep them confined in a single spot. Vātavyādhi recommended that princes should be made addicted to sensual excesses. [[Kaut.|Kauṭ.]] disapproves of this as living death (for the princes), recommends proper care before conception and after birth and instruction in dharma. In [[Kaut|Kauṭ.]] V. 6 Bhāradvāja is quoted as saying that when the king is on his death-bed, the minister may set up the kinsmen of the king and the principal princes against one another, that whoever attacks should be killed by inciting the people against him, or having secretly punished the kinsmen and chief princes and brought them under control, the minister should himself take possession of the kingdom. [[Kautilya|Kauṭilya]] does not approve of this. But even he advises (XIV Aupaniṣadika) the employment of poison, herbs and incantations against those who are irreligious or wicked. In I. 18 even [[Kaut,|Kauṭ.]] does not shrink from advising that secret emissaries (of the reigning king) may kill an abandoned prince with weapons and poison and in [[V,|V.]] 1 [[Kaut.|Kauṭ.]] remarks that, when [[ā|a]] king cannot openly put down the principal courtiers or chiefs [[P13]]who are dangerous to the kingdom, he may inflict punishment on them in secret or may induce the brother of the officer to be punished to attack the latter by promising to give him the [[posit ion|position]] and wealth of the officer and then destroy that attacker with weapons or poison saying that he was guilty of fratricide. In V. 2 for replenishing a depleted treasury [[Kautilya|Kauṭilya]] coolly asks the king to deprive the temples in the kingdom of their wealth through the superintendent of religious endowments.

Similarly it has to be borne in mind that the works referred to cover a period of several centuries and further that India is not one country but a continent of which northern India formed one more or less homogeneous unit, while the Deccan formed another and South India formed a third unit. The institutions of these, particularly of the last of the three, differ in many particulars from those of the other two. But the authentic data furnished by South India are later by centuries than those supplied by the north and besides are comparatively meagre. Therefore in this work one has to confine oneself principally to Sanskrit works composed in North India or the Deccan.

[[P14]]It would be impossible to go into all details about [[Govern ment|Government]] and Politics within the space at my disposal. What is [[P15]]proposed is to indicate and dilate upon only the fundamental ideas and principles of the several matters falling under rājadharma. Nor is it possible to enter here into formal and lengthy refutations of the several views propounded by Western and Indian writers about the forms and functions of Government and the state of society in ancient India. Most of the modern works referred to in note 19 above are more or less based on the same material in Sanskrit and Pāli, but the emotional or subjective element is different in each case. Some western writers have without very solid foundations [[iudulged|indulged]] in a facile and sweeping generalization that the great Empires of antiquity such as those of the Assyrians, the Babylonians, the Medians and Persians and the Indians were mainly tax-gathering institutions (vide Sir Henry Maine's 'Early History of Institutions' 1875, pp. 384 and 390; and Prof. Rangaswami Aiyangar's 'Ancient Indian Polity' 1935, pp. 5-6 and pp. 66-69 (where passages from T. H. Green and others are cited); while some Indian writers vehemently assert that the Government in [[An cient|Ancient]] India was always some form of limited monarchy. Both characterisations are in my opinion misleading. An endeavour will be made to present from the original texts as true a picture of the polity of ancient and medieval India as it is possible for me to do. It will do no good to label the ancient institutions of India with concepts and terms current in the West during the last one hundred and fifty years or so. Even in the West true [[democra cies|democracies]] or real democratic governments did not exist and the masses in no country had elective popular assemblies or councils before the last quarter of the 18th century. Such assemblies do not exist even now in several countries of Europe. Even in the ancient Greek city states the number of slaves who had no voice in the government was several times as large as the free citizens and those states were no democracies at all in the strict sense[^19a]. Indian polity has a recorded history of over two thousand years from at least the 4th century B. C. Its growth was gradual but its aims and ideals and its main elements have been the same throughout the centuries. India need not feel ashamed or fear [[P16]]a comparison of its ideals and theories of State with the ideals and theories of western countries in ancient and medieval times. If western countries could boast of so-called republics and city states in ancient Greece and Rome, India also had several [[re publics|republics]] in ancient times. The ancient Hindus made their own contributions to political thought, though unfortunately Western scholars of the 19th century like [[Max Mūller,|Max Müller,]] Weber and Roth were concerned most with the Vedic and allied literature and either did not know or ignored the vast literature on politics contained in Sanskrit and [[Pali|Pāli]] works. Vide 'Some Aspects of ancient Hindu Polity' by Prof. D. R. Bhandarkar (1929) pp. 2-3 for views of [[Max Mūller|Max Müller]] and others and criticism thereof, also Dr. Beni Prasad's [[“Theory|'Theory]] of Government &c.' [[P. I,|p. 1,]] U. Ghoshal [[History|'History]] of Hindu Political Theories' pp. 3-4.
