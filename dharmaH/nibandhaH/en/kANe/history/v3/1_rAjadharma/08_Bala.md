[[P200]]
CHAPTER VIII 

BALA (THE ARMY). 

Bala is also called [[danda|daṇḍa]] in [[Kaut|Kauṭ.]] and elsewhere. [[Accord ing|According]] to Sumantu [[dapda|daṇḍa]] means 'punishment, corporal or monetary' and the army with its four arms is included by Sumantu under kośa ( acc. to [[$. V.|S. V.]] p. 46).[^260] In the [[Rgveda|Ṛgveda]] we have frequent references to armies, weapons, battles &c. The word [[senāni|senānī]] occurs in [[Rg.|Ṛg.]] X. 84. 2 where Manyu ( martial Fury ) is invoked to be the Commander.[^261] [[Rg.|Ṛg.]] VI. 75 is full of references to bows, arrows, armour, bow-strings, quiver, charioteer, horses, chariots &c. Kām. ( XIII. 34–37 ) states that the king possessed of a sound financial position increases his depleted army, supports his own subjects and is depended upon even by his enemies. All the following result from the possession of a (powerful ) army viz. increase of the wealth of one's friends and enemies and of the ( king's) territories, prompt attainment of objects that seemed distant, protection of what is already [[secur ed|secured]], destruction of the armies of the enemy, the keeping together of one's army. Most authorities agree in saying that troops are of six kinds viz. maula (hereditary ), [[bhrta|bhṛta]] or bhṛtaka or [[bhitya|bhṛtya]] (hired troops ), [[śreni|śreṇi]] (guild troops ), mitra ( troops of an ally or friendly power or feudatories ), amitra ( troops that once belonged to the enemy ), [[atavi|aṭavi]] or ātavika (wild tribes as troops). Vide [[Kaut. 242|Kauṭ.]][^262] IX. [[%|2]] (first sentence ), Kām. XVIII. 4, Agnipurāṇa 242. 1–2, Mānasollāsa ( II. 6, [[verge|verse]] 556 p. 76). The first three works say that each earlier variety of troops is superior to each later one. Maula troops corresponded to the modern standing army, since [[Kaut.|Kauṭ.]] (IX. 2) prefers them on the ground that they depend on the king for maintenance and are being constantly drilled. They most probably consisted of persons who and whose ancestors got tax-free lands in lieu of military service. The [[Sabba parva 5, 63|Sabhāparva 5. 63]] appears to refer to four kinds ( omitting [[śreni|śreṇi]] and amitra ) and [[Yuddhakanda|Yuddhakāṇḍa]] 17. 24 refers to five ( omitting [[śrepi|śreṇi]]).

[[P201]]
The [[Asramavāsikaparva|Āśramavāsikaparva]][^263] ( 7. 7–8 ) names five kinds (except amitra ) and states that maula and mitra armies are superior to the rest and hired troops and śreṇi troops are each other's equals. This division of armies into various kinds is mentioned in the grant of Dhruvasena I of Valabhi in Gupta-Valabhi year 206 (E. I. vol. XI. p. 106, where the king is said to have acquired the kingdom with the help of maula, [[bhrta|bhṛta]], mitra and [[śreni|śreṇi]] armies ). Mānasollāsa defines the [[atavika|āṭavika]][^264] army as consisting of niṣādas, mlecchas and similar castes dwelling in the vicinity of mountains and amitra troops as soldiers who once belonged to an enemy king but being defeated were taken captive and made slaves. [[Accord ing|According]] to the [[Rājanitiratnākara|Rājanītiratnākara]] (p. 38) 'aribala' means 'troops that come to a king after leaving the king's enemy'. Kām. XVIII. 7 says that the ātavika troops are, by nature, irreligious, greedy, [[smāryas|anāryas]] and non-observers of truth. They correspond to the pendharis and freebooters of later times. The reasons why [[hered itary|hereditary]] and other troops are superior to amitra and āṭavika [[varie ties|varieties]] are explained at great length by [[Kaut.|Kauṭ.]] IX. 2 and Kām. XVIII. 5–9. [[Kaut.|Kauṭ.]] (IX. 2) states that an [[amitru|amitra]] army led by an ārya is superior to wild tribe troops. Both of them are out for plunder and in case no plunder can be had or when there is a disaster they may prove as dangerous as snakes. By [[greni-bala|śreṇi-bala]] he has in mind the organized bands of soldiers to whom he refers elsewhere as '[[vārtāśastropajivinah|vārtāśastropajīvinaḥ]]' (vide p. 89 above ). As it is not unlikely that members of trade-guilds either [[them. selves|themselves]] learnt the profession of arms or engaged soldiers for the protection of their merchandise and property, these could be pressed into his service by a king in case of need and were distinguished from the hereditary army and hired troops as [[' sreni-bala'|'śreṇi-bala']]. Differing from the ācāryas that troops composed of brāhmaṇas, [[ksatriyas|kṣatriyas]], vaiśyas and [[sūdras|śūdras]] are superior for [[en listment|enlistment]] in the order of the castes, [[Kaut.|Kauṭ.]] holds that an army of [[ksatriyas|kṣatriyas]] well-trained in the wielding of arms or an army of [[P202]] [[vaiśgas|vaiśyas]] and [[sūdras|śūdras]] having greater numerical strength is better than an army composed of brāhmaṇa soldiers, since an enemy may win over the latter army by prostrating himself before them.[^265] [[Vide H. Dh. vol. II, pp. 122-123|Vide H. Dh. vol. II. pp. 122–123]] for discussion on the question whether brāhmaṇas could become soldiers. In [[Udyoga parva 96.7|Udyoga-parva 96. 7]] (cr. ed. chap. 94 ) it is said that king [[Dambhod bhava|Dambhodbhava]] every day asked in the morning whether there was any śūdra, [[vaisya|vaiśya]], [[ksatriya|kṣatriya]] or [[brahmana|brāhmaṇa]] equal or superior to him in armed conflict. That shows that soldiers of castes other than kṣatriyas were not unheard of in the Epic age. Kām. IV. ( 63, 65, 67 ) says that the hereditary army ([[pitr-paitāmaha|pitṛ-paitāmaha]]) should consist mostly of [[ksatriyas|kṣatriyas]]. In the Maliya copperplate of Mahārāja Dharasena II ( 252 Valabhi [[samvat|saṁvat]] i. e. 571–72 A. D.) Bhaṭārka, the founder of the Valabhi dynasty, is said to have secured the kingdom with the help of maula, [[bhsta|bhṛta]], mitra and [[śreṛi|śreṇi]] troops (Gupta Inscriptions p. 165 ). [[Sukra|Śukra]] (II. 137–139 ) says that the soldiers may be [[sūdras|śūdras]], kṣatriyas, [[vaisyas|vaiśyas]], mlecchas or of mixed castes, provided they are brave, restrained, well-built, devoted to their master and their dharma, and hate the enemy. [[Santi|Śānti]] (101. 3–5) describes in what respects soldiers from Gandhāra, Sindhu and other countries and Yavana and Deccan soldiers excel, remarks that brave and strong men are to be found everywhere (verse 6 ) and that men from the border (i. e. bhillas and kaivartas [[accord ing|according]] to [[Nilakaptha|Nīlakaṇṭha]] ) are desperate fighters, would never run away from battle and so should be preferred for enlistment in the army (verse 19). The [[Yasastilaka|Yaśastilaka]] III (pp. 461–467) describes the characteristics of Northern Indian (auttarāpatha), Deccan (dākṣiṇātya), Dramila (South Indian), Tirhut ([[Taira bhukta|Tairabhukta]]) and Gurjara soldiers. An army was said to be composed of four parts, viz. elephants, horses, chariots and foot-soldiers ([[caturanga|caturaṅga]] bala). Kām. XVIII. 24 says that bala is [[sixfeld|sixfcld]], viz. the four sections of infantry, cavalry, chariots and elephants together with mantra (line of policy) and kośa (treasury). In [[Sānti.|Śānti.]] 103. 38 the army is said to have six [[angas|aṅgas]] (the [[well known|well-known]] four plus kośa and roads for traffic). According to [[Kautilya|Kauṭilya]] (II. [[%|2]], VII. 11) and [[Kām. XIX, 62|Kām. XIX. 62]] the destruction of [[P203]] the enemy's forces and victory depended on elephants.[^266] [[Santi parva|Śānti-parva]] (100. 24) says that an army in which the infantry [[pre dominates|predominates]] remains firm and that cavalry and chariots are all right when there is no rain. [[Sānti|Śānti]] (59. 41–42) enumerates eight elements of the army viz. the well-known four (elephants etc.), [[visti|viṣṭi]] (labourers or porters who gave free labour and were only fed but were paid no wages ), boats, spies, and guides (deśika).[^267] Vide [[Santi|Śānti]] 121. 44 also. In the Mahābhārata elephants do not, except in rare cases, play any role in the fighting that went on, while chariots and the other arms of an army are described everywhere. In the [[Virātaparva|Virāṭaparva]] ( 65. 6) [[Vikarna|Vikarṇa]] was seated on an elephant when he attacked Arjuna; [[Bhiṣmaparva|Bhīṣmaparva]] 20. 7 refers to Duryodhana as riding an elephant and (95. 32–33) Bhagadatta is described as seated on an elephant when he attacked [[Bhima|Bhīma]]. In this respect the Epic carries on the vedic tradition. Megasthenes (Fragment 1 p. 30) notes that in ancient India elephants were trained for war and turned the scale of victory. Vast armies were kept by ancient kings and emperors. When [[Satrughna|Śatrughna]] started against the demon [[Lavana|Lavaṇa]] he had an army of 4000 horses, 2000 chariots and 100 elephants with him (Rāmāyaṇa VII. 64. 2–4). In the [[Dasakumāracarita|Daśakumāracarita]] VIII the cynical jester [[Viharabhadra|Vihārabhadra]] reminds his master that the latter had 10000 elephants, three lakhs of horse and numberless foot-soldiers (B. S. S. p. 133, of ed. of 1919).

Though the Budhabhūṣaṇa (p. 42 ) contains a glowing eulogy of elephants, the नीतिवाक्यामृत holds that elephants, if not properly trained, cause loss of wealth ( because they eat vast quantities of fodder and grains ) and of lives (they trample down their own soldiers through fright) 'अशिक्षिता हस्तिनः केवलमर्थप्राणहराः ' 22. 5 p. 208. The यशस्तिलक III. p. 491 notes 'न विनीता गजा येषां तेषां ते नृप केवलम् । क्लेशायापि विनाशाय रणे चात्मवधाय च ॥'. III. (1928) pp. 5 and 16 for the same.

Megasthenes (in Fragment XXVII p. 68) speaks of the camp of Sandrakottos (Candragupta Maurya) that had 400000 men, notes ( in Fragment LVI) that the king of Palibothra had in his pay a standing army of six lakhs of foot-soldiers, 30000 cavalry and 9000 elephants (p. 141, McCrindle ), that the king of Horatae ([[Surāṣtra|Surāṣṭra]] ) had 150000 foot-soldiers, 5000 cavalry and 1600 elephants (ibid p. 150 ) and that even the [[Pāudyan|Pāṇḍyan]] kingdom ruled by women had 150000 foot and 500 elephants (ibid. p. 147). Vide Beal's 'Buddhist Records &c.' vol. I. p. 213 for the armies collected by [[Harga|Harṣa]] in his march against the murderer of his elder brother (viz. 5000 elephants, 2000 horse and 50000 foot-soldiers ) and the vast armies that he had after six years of conquest viz. 60000 elephants and 100000 cavalry. In the [[Asvamedhikarparya|Āśvamedhikaparva]] ( 60. 14–20 ) it is narrated that when [[Drona|Droṇa]] [[assunied|assumed]] the post of commander-in-chief the Kaurava army had been reduced to 9 [[akṣuuhinis|akṣauhiṇīs]] from eleven, that only five were left when [[Karna|Karṇa]] became Senāpati and the [[Pandavas|Pāṇḍavas]] had then only three left and under [[Salya's|Śalya's]] command there were only three [[akṣauhiṇis|akṣauhiṇīs]] and the [[Pandavas|Pāṇḍavas]] could oppose to him only one. In reply to a question from [[Dhrtarāstra|Dhṛtarāṣṭra]] [[Yudhisthira|Yudhiṣṭhira]] states that in the great war the total number of warriors killed in battle came to the colossal figure of [[1660020006|1660020000]] ([[Striparva|Strīparva]] 26. 9).[^268] The Udyogaparva gives ( [[155, 24-26|155. 24–26]] ) the following table for an [[akṣauhiṇi|akṣauhiṇī]] : 500 elephants, 500 chariots, 1500 cavalry and 2500 foot-soldiers constituted an army called senā, 10 senās = [[prtanā|pṛtanā]], 10 [[prianās=vāhini|pṛtanās = vāhinī]], 10 [[vāhinis=dhvajini|vāhinīs = dhvajinī]], 10 dhvajinis = [[cumū|camū]] and 10 [[camūs=aksauhiṇi|camūs = akṣauhiṇī]]. The Kauravas had eleven [[akṣauhinis|akṣauhiṇīs]] and the [[Pandavas|Pāṇḍavas]] had 7. If we take the table in [[Adi|Ādi]] 2. 19–22 one [[akṣauhini|akṣauhiṇī]] contained 21870 elephants, the [[sanie|same]] number of chariots, 65610 horses and 109350 foot-soldiers.[^269] But if the other tables be followed then the numbers may be much larger still. Another table furnished by [[Udyogaparva.|Udyogaparva]] 155. 28–29 is : 55 men constituted patti, [[3 pattis senāmukha|3 pattis = senāmukha]] or gulma, 3 gulmas = [[gana|gaṇa]] and there were ayutas (10 thousands ) of [[gañas|gaṇas]] in the army of the Kauravas. The [[Adiparva|Ādiparva]] (2. 19–22 ): [[P205]] differs from both tables in some respects. The Udyogaparva [[155, 22|155. 22]] further notes that each horseman was surrounded by [[ten men attendants|ten men as attendants]] ([[hayaścāsan|hayascāsan]] pādarakṣāḥ samantataḥ). Though throughout the centuries the infantry was theoretically and in practice more numerous than cavalry, not much importance seems to have been attached to them as compared with chariots or horsemen. The lexicon called [[Vaijayanti|Vaijayantī]] says that patti consists of three horses, five [[foot soldiers|foot-soldiers]], one chariot and one elephant, that three pattis are equal to senāmukha and that senāmukha, gulma, [[gana|gaṇa]], [[vābini|vāhinī]], [[pitanā|pṛtanā]], [[camũ|camū]], [[anikini|anīkinī]] represent each three times as many as the preceding one and that 10 [[anikinis|anīkinīs]] are equal to an [[akṣauhiṇi,|akṣauhiṇī.]] The [[Nitiprakāśikā 870|Nītiprakāśikā]][^270] gives a table of groups from [[akṣauhini|akṣauhiṇī]] and remarks that each of these groups had in turn numerous supporting men e. g. each elephant was followed by a hundred horsemen and a thousand foot-soldiers and each [[horse man|horseman]] was supported by a thousand foot-soldiers ( VII. 3–10 ). Manu VII. 192 refers to battles on water also. From references in the Mahābhārata it appears that chariots had only two wheels. Vide [[Bhiṣma|Bhīṣma]] 98. 47, [[Dronaparva|Droṇaparva]] 154. 3, [[Salya|Śalya]] 16. 24 ([[Śaineyo|Śāineyo]] [[dakṣiṇam cakram|dakṣiṇaṁ cakraṁ]] [[Dhrstadyumnas-tathottaram|Dhṛṣṭadyumnas-tathottaram]]). Two noted warriors were told off to guard the two wheels of the chariot of the principal commanders and are called 'cakra-rakṣau' ( vide [[Bhisma|Bhīṣma]] 54. 76, 108. 5, [[Drona 91, 36|Droṇa 91. 36]], [[Karpa 11, 31|Karṇa 11. 31]], 34, 44). Chariots of great warriors were drawn by four horses e. g. [[Adi.|Ādi.]] 198. 15, Udyoga 48. 50, Droṇa 145. 81. Udyoga 83. 15–21 describe the chariot of [[Krspa|Kṛṣṇa]] and Udyoga 140. 21 states that chariots had small tinkling bells attached to them and also screens of tiger-skin. The [[Rg.|Ṛg.]] contains very graphic descriptions of chariots. Chariots were generally drawn by two horses in the vedic age ([[Rg.|Ṛg.]] V. 30. [[I|1]], V. 36. 5, VI. 23. 1) and had two wheels, but the chariot of [[the.|the]] Aśvins is described as having three wheels ([[Rg.|Ṛg.]] I. 118. 2, I. 157. 3, X. 41. 1). [[Ghatotkaca|Ghaṭotkaca]], however, had eight wheels to his chariot (Droṇa 156. 61, 175. 13). The [[Sukranitisāra|Śukranītisāra]] (II. 140–148) mentions another method of grouping the army. Five or six foot-soldiers made a patti over which there [[P206]] was an officer called [[puttipa|pattipa]], over 30 pattipālas there was the gaulmika, over 100 gaulmikas was the officer called [[satanika|śatānīka]], who had as his assistants an officer called anuśatika, a [[senānı|senānī]] and a lekhaka (scribe ); the officer over twenty elephants or horses was called nāyaka ( compare modern “naik”). Each of these officers should have a distinctive badge indicative of their position. Ayodhyā 100. 32 ( =Sabhā 5. 48) asks: 'I hope you give at the proper time pay and rations to your soldiers according to their deserts and do not delay payment'. Both Nār. (sambhūya-22) and [[Br.|Bṛ.]] state that among persons who work for hire the best is the soldier. The [[Mānasollaga|Mānasollāsa]] ( II. 6. 566–569 p. 80) describes that the chiefs of the hereditary army should always be honoured by the king with presents of jewels, ornaments, costly clothes and sweet words and sumptuous provision for their maintenance should be made by bestowing on them a village, or two or more villages and heaps of gold, while hired soldiers should be paid every day or every month or once in three, four or six months or once in a year according to the needs of the king. Megasthenes ([[Frag ment|Fragment]] XXXIV p. 88 ) describes the organization of the Indian army: 'A third governing body directs military affairs, of which there were six divisions with five members to each. One co-operates with the admiral of the fleet, another with the superintendent of bullock trains, the third division has charge of foot-soldiers, the fourth of horses, the fifth of war chariots and the sixth of [[elephants.|elephants.']] In medieval times chariots appear to have become obsolete. In the graphic descriptions of armies contained in the Harṣacarita ( particularly in the 7th ucchvāsa ) war chariots are conspicuous by their absence. The Mahābhārata often speaks of horses from the countries to the north-west of India as the best; vide Sabhā 53. 5 (for [[Kamboja|Kāmboja]] and Gāndhāra horses ), Udyoga 86. 6 (horses from [[Balhi|Bālhi]]), [[Drona|Droṇa]] 125. 25 and Sauptika 13. 2 ([[Kamboja|Kāmboja]] horses). The Harṣacarita II speaks of the best horses as coming from Vanāyu, [[Aratta|Āraṭṭa]], [[Kamboja|Kāmboja]], [[Sindhudesa|Sindhudeśa]] and [[Pārasika|Pārasīka]]. [[Sukra|Śukra]] lays down certain practical rules about the army ([[IV, 7. 379-390|IV. 7. 379–390]]). He says that the soldiers should be encamped outside the town or village but not far from it, that no money-lending should be allowed between the soldiers and the village people, that the king should open separate shops for goods required by soldiers, that no army should be encamped at one place for more than a year, that soldiers should not enter the village without the king's permission, that receipts should be taken from soldiers [[P207]] for payments made to them and they should be furnished with a writing about their pay. Some of these rules are very old. The [[Udyogaparva 471|Udyogaparva]][^271] 37. 30 mentions among persons with whom no contractual transactions should be entered into, the king, the king's servants and soldiers.

The [[Artbaśāstra|Arthaśāstra]] contains an elaborate discussion (in IX. 1–7 and [[X, 1-6|X. 1–6]]) about the organization of the king's army, the proper time and place for starting on an invasion, internal and external troubles and calamities and measures against them, means of dealing with traitors and enemies, religious remedies (worshipping gods and falling at the feet of brāhmaṇas, magical rites based upon the Atharvaveda) against misfortunes like fire and flood, epidemics, famine &c., [[encampinent|encampment]] ( skandhāvāra ) of armies, treacherous and strategic fighting, proper battle grounds, encouragement to one's own army, free labourers and their work, different arrangements or formations (vyūhas ) of armies. Considerations of space prevent any treatment of these matters. But a few notable points will be set out here. A king may invade his enemy's country either in [[Mārgasirṣa|Mārgaśīrṣa]] ( when the crops sown in the rains are ready ) or in Caitra or when the enemy is suffering from some calamity. [[Sānti|Śānti]] (100. 10–11 ) says the same thing. Internal trouble arises when any minister, purohita, senāpati or the crown prince is angry or dissatisfied with the king, who should get rid of such trouble by giving up his own fault or by pointing out the danger arising from an enemy. If the crown prince causes trouble he should be kept in confinement or killed, if there is another son of [[good character,|good character.]] The trouble caused by a provincial Governor or the officer in charge of boundaries (antapāla), the chief of wild tribes or a conquered king is termed external. The king should meet it by setting up one against the other. The encampment of an army is to be made on a site declared to be the best according to the science of buildings and measured by the nāyaka ( the chief of the army), carpenter and astrologer, the encampment being circular, square or rectangular and having four gates, six roads and nine divisions. Disputes, drinking, holding merry [[gather ings|gatherings]] ( samāja ) and gambling should be prohibited in the camp and the system of passes should be enforced (X. 1). Vanaparva ( 15. 14, 19 ) also refers to the system of passes and the [[faot|fact]] that [[P208]] dancers and musicians were driven away, when Dvārakā was besieged by [[sālva|Śālva]]. From Udyoga 151. 58 (cr. ed. 149. 53), 195. 12–19 (cr. ed. 196. 12–19) it appears that markets, [[prosti tutes|prostitutes]], conveyances, oxen, machines, arms and physicians accompanied an army and that the camp ([[senānivesa|senāniveśa]] or skandhāvāra) of Duryodhana looked like the capital itself and was five yojanas in extent. Physicians with surgical instruments, blunt instruments (like tweezers ), medicines, curative oils and [[handages|bandages]] in their hands and women ( nurses ) looking after the food and drink of the army should stand behind the soldiers uttering encouraging words[^272] ([[Kaut|Kauṭ.]] X. 3). The [[Bhiṣmaparva|Bhīṣmaparva]] 120. 55 also states that doctors well-versed in [[extraoting|extracting]] splinters or arrow-heads ([[froni|from]] the body ) approached with their [[Burgical|surgical]] instruments to extract the darts from [[Bhiṣma's|Bhīṣma's]] body. The duties of the labourers ([[viṣti|viṣṭi]]) were to examine the camp, roads, bridges, wells, and river ghats, to carry machines, weapons, armour, utensils, fodder, to remove from the battle-field wounded men along with their weapons and armour.[^273] Each commander had some distinguishing device as his banner e. g. [[Bhisma|Bhīṣma]] had a golden tāla tree as his standard ([[Bhiṣmaparva|Bhīṣmaparva]] VI. 17. 18, tālena mahatā [[Bhiệmah|Bhīṣmaḥ]] pañcatāreṇa ketunā). In X. 6 [[Kautilya|Kauṭilya]] speaks of several formations (vyūhas) called [[danda, bhoga; mandala, aśanihata|daṇḍa, bhoga, maṇḍala, asaṅhata]] and the subdivisions such as gomūtrikā, makara &c. [[In Kām,|In Kām.]] XVIII. 48–49, XIX. 40 ff., Manu VII. 187–191, [[Nitiprakāśikā|Nītiprakāśikā]], chap. 6 and in the Mahābhārata many vyūhas are described. Vanaparva (285. 6–7) refers to [[Rāvaṣa's|Rāvaṇa's]] arrangement of his army according to the rules of [[Usanas|Uśanas]] and of [[Rama's|Rāma's]] army according to Bārhaspatya rules. The [[Asrama vāsikaparva|Aśrama-vāsikaparva]] 7. 15 refers to the formations called [[Sakata|Śakaṭa]], Padma and Vajra described in the work of Uśanas. [[Kautilya|Kauṭilya]] X. 6 also refers to Auśanasa and Bārhaspatya arrangements of armies. [[Drona 75, 27, 87, 22-24|Droṇa 75. 27, 87. 22–24]], [[Karpaparva|Karṇaparva]] 11. 14 and 28 mention certain [[vyūbas|vyūhas]] like Makara, [[Sakata|Śakaṭa]] &c. Vide also [[Manasollasa|Mānasollāsa]] II. 20 verses 1170–1181 pp. 134–135, Agnipurāṇa 242. 7–8 and 42–73 for vyūhas. Though [[Kautilya|Kauṭilya]] recommends all sorts of tricks and treachery for securing a victory, the [[P209]] [[Mahabharata|Mahābhārata]] holds up a high ideal. The [[Bhismaparva|Bhīṣmaparva]][^274] 21. 10 remarks that conquerors do not secure victory so much by their armies and prowess as by truthfulness, freedom from cruelty, the observance of dharma and energetic actions. The [[Santi parva|Śānti-parva]] ( 95. 17–18) states that it is better to die while fighting according to the rules of dharma rather than obtain a victory by wicked actions.

[[____ In|In]] the [[Bhismaparva|Bhīṣmaparva]] (I. 27–32) certain rules of war agreed upon between the Kauravas and the [[Pandavas|Pāṇḍavas]] are set out, such as one should fight only with one similarly equipped (i. e. a [[foot soldier|foot-soldier]] with a foot-soldier and so on), one should not kill a soldier who is already in combat with another, or who has turned back from fight or is without armour. [[Ap.|Āp.]] Dh. S. II. 5. 10. 12, Gaut. [[x.|X.]] 17–18, [[Yaj.|Yāj.]] I. 326, Manu VII. 90–93, [[Santi|Śānti]] 95. 7–14, 96. 3, [[Santi|Śānti]] 98. 48–49, 297. 4, [[Dronaparva|Droṇaparva]] 143. 8, [[Karna90.|Karṇa 90.]]111–113, Sauptika 5. 11–12, 6. 21–23, [[Saṅkha|Śaṅkha]] (quoted by the Mit. on [[Yaj.|Yāj.]] I. 326), Baud. Dh. S. I. 10. 10–12, [[Vrddha-Harita.|Vṛddha-Hārīta]] VII. 226, [[Br̥hat-Parasara x.|Bṛhat-Parāśara X.]] p. 281, [[Sukra|Śukra]] IV. 7. 354–362, [[Yuddha-kanda.|Yuddha-kāṇḍa]] 18. 27–28 contain rules of war dictated by noble sentiments of humanity and chivalry. Some of these ( that will bear comparison with the conventions of the Geneva and Hague Conferences) are set out here. Gaut. (X. 17–18 )[^275] states: 'no sin is committed by injuring or slaying men in battle excepting him who has lost his horse, charioteer or weapons, him who joins his hands ( in supplication for life ), whose hair are dishevelled (in flying), who turns away from the field, who sits down, who climbs an eminence or a tree (in flight), except envoys or messengers, except him who declares himself to be a cow or a [[brahmana|brāhmaṇa]]'. [[Vrddha-Harita.|Vṛddha-Hārīta]] VII. 216 exempts spectators. Manu (VII. 90–93) declares “one should not fight with treacherous [[P210]] (or concealed ) weapons, or with barbed or poisoned [[weapong|weapons]] or with weapons the points of which are blazing with fire. Let a fighter not strike one who has climbed on an eminence, nor a eunuch, nor one who joins the palms of his hand ([[ip supplica tion|in supplication]]), nor one with flying hair ( in flight), nor one who sits down or says 'I am thine', nor one who is asleep, nor one who has lost armour, nor one who is naked or disarmed, nor one who is merely looking on without taking part in the fight, nor one who is fighting with another foe, nor one whose weapons are broken, nor one who is afflicted with sorrow, nor one seriously wounded, nor one who is in fear, nor one who has turned to flee.'' [[Saṅkha|Śaṅkha]] adds that a soldier should not kill another while the latter is drinking water or taking his meals or is taking off his shoes, nor should one kill a woman, a female elephant, nor a charioteer, nor a bard nor a brāhmaṇa nor should one who is not a king (or noble) kill one who is a king. Baud. Dh. S. I. 10. 10 forbids the use of poisoned or barbed arrows ([[karnin|karṇin]]). [[Santi|Śānti]] 95. 11 also does the same. [[Sānti|Śānti]] ( 95. 13–14 ) enjoins that even an enemy soldier, when wounded, should be treated with medicine and allowed to go when his wounds are healed.[^276] Śānti adds that a soldier should not kill boys or old men nor from behind nor one who holds a blade of grass in his mouth (as a mark of submission). These rules, though probably ideal and not strictly followed in every case, are far more humane as compared with the practice in modern warfare when [[non combatants|non-combatants]] are killed from the air even at night without [[warn ing|warning]]. In ancient times [[un molested|unmolested]] went generally unmolested, to which Megasthenes bears testimony when he says (Frag. I. p. 32 ) [[Tillers|'Tillers]] of the soil even when battle is raging in the neighbourhood are undisturbed by any sense of danger, for the combatants allow those engaged in husbandry to remain quite [[unmolested,'|unmolested.']] Manu VII. 32 allows a king to harass his enemy's country, but [[Medbātithi|Medhātithi]] on VII. 32 asks the invader to save his enemy's people if possible (particularly [[brābmapas|brāhmaṇas]]). The rule in [[gudāyuddha|gadāyuddha]] (fight with maces or clubs ) was that no blow was to be struck below the navel ( [[Salyaparva|Śalyaparva]] 60. 6). But this rule was violated by [[Bhima|Bhīma]] when he struck [[Duryodbana|Duryodhana]] on the thigh with his mace. Duryodhana recounts ( in [[Salya 61 .|Śalya 61 )]] all the bad deeds of [[Krspa|Kṛṣṇa]] and the [[Pāndavas|Pāṇḍavas]] and the only reply that [[Krsna|Kṛṣṇa]] makes is that he too was guilty of [[P211]] numerous [[breachey|breaches]] of morality and the chivalrous rules of war ( such as the slaying of Abhimanyu by many engaging him at one time ). The general rule was that fighting ceased when the sun went down ( [[Bhisma|Bhīṣma]] 49. 52–53 ). But in [[Dronaparva|Droṇaparva]] 154 and 163. 16 ff. we have a description of night battles and it is provided that chariots, elephants and horses should carry lamps.

It has already been shown how it was the duty of a [[ksatriya|kṣatriya]] and of every soldier to fight and die in battle rather than run away. A fighting spirit was inculcated by holding out several rewards. One was the acquisition of booty and territory (Gaut. X. 41, Manu VII. 206, [[Bhagavadgita|Bhagavadgītā]] II. 37); others were the satisfaction of having done one's duty as a kṣatriya ([[Gita|Gītā]] II. 31–33), honour and fame ([[Gita|Gītā]] II. 34–35), heaven and other-worldly rewards (Yāj. I. 324, Manu VII. 88–89), protection of brāhmaṇas ([[Ap.|Āp.]] Dh. S. II. 10. 26. 2–3). Viṣṇu Dh. S. III. 44–46 have already been quoted above (p. 58). The Śānti (98. 40–41) states that a soldier who runs away from the field falls into Hell. Yāj. (I. 324–325) declares that those who, while fighting with weapons that are not treacherous (poisoned &c.) for the sake of the land (of their master or of the enemy) die in battle without turning back from it go to heaven like yogins, that each step of those who do not flee even when their comrades have been killed is equal to a solemn sacrifice (like the [[Asyamedha|Aśvamedha]]); the king (the master) takes away all the merit of [[thoge|those]] who run away from the battlefield and are then killed. Manu VII. 95 contains the same idea. These remarks were applicable not only to [[ksatriya|kṣatriya]] soldiers, but to soldiers of all castes who maintained themselves by following the [[pro fession|profession]] of arms. Vide Rājanītiprakāśa p. 407. [[Parāsara|Parāśara]] (III. 31) and Bṛhat-Parāśara X. p. 281 remark that a valiant soldier who does not seek mercy though surrounded by many enemies and falls fighting attains imperishable worlds and that when he reaches heaven divine damsels run after him to choose him as their Lord ( III. 34–35). Parāśara III. 36 is a[^277] verse which is [[P212]] one of the two quoted by [[Kaut.|Kauṭ.]] (X. 3) when soldiers are to be urged on to fight. [[Kautilya|Kauṭilya]] (in X. 3) advises that the king [[him. self|himself]] and his mantrin and purohita should urge on his soldiers by quoting Vedic and classical Sanskrit passages about the rewards waiting for those who fall fighting for their master and the religious punishments for those who run away. Astrologers should infuse spirit into their side by asserting that the heavenly aspects favour their side. The day before the battle the king should observe a fast, offer oblations into fire to the [[accom paniment|accompaniment]] of Atharvaveda mantras and cause benedictory texts to be repeated that refer to victory. Bards should recite lays describing heaven as the reward for the brave and hell for the timid and extol the caste, guild, family, deeds and character of the soldiers. The assistants of the purohita should declare that they have practised witchcraft against the enemy. The [[commander in-chief|commander-in-chief]] and the officers under him should address the army as follows:-'a hundred thousand ([[panas|paṇas]]) will be the reward for him who kills the enemy king, fifty thousand for him who kills the commander-in-chief or the crown prince, .......... a hundred for slaying the officer of the patti (a battalion), twenty for bringing the head (of a common soldier) and twice the pay and the booty seized by each to all soldiers'. Kām. (XIX. 18–21) says that the king should give the rewards (promised as in [[Kaut.)|Kauṭ.)]]) to the soldiers after they succeed in the exploits [[men tioned|mentioned]]. Vide also Mānasollāsa II. 20 verses 1163–1167 (pp. [[133 |133–]]134) for similar promises. Gaut. (X. 20–23) prescribes that whatever wealth is acquired by a soldier by [[bis|his]] individual effort should be given to him by the king, but the horse or elephant caught by a soldier goes to the king, that if many soldiers by a joint effort obtain some valuable plunder the king should choose and retain the best for himself and the rest should be divided among the soldiers according to their services in the battle. Vide Manu VII. 96–97 (which allow even [[even & chariot|even a chariot]], horse or elephant to be retained as booty by the soldier and everything else including female slaves, except jewels, gold and silver), Kām. XIX.[^278] 21–22, [[Sukra|Śukra]] IV[[,|.]] 7. 372.

A treatise will be required to deal with the weapons of war from ancient times. Even in the [[Rgveda|Ṛgveda]] several weapons are mentioned e. g. [[rsti|ṛṣṭi]] ([[Rg.|Ṛg.]] V. 52. 6, V. 57. 2 and 6 on the shoulders [[P213]] of the Maruts), arrows (V. 57. 2, VI. 75. 17), quivers (V. 57. 2), the [[ankusa|aṅkuśa]] (of Indra in VIII. 17. 10, X. 44. 9), paraśu (X. 28. 8), [[kspāṇa|kṛpāṇa]] (probably a dagger, in X. 22. 10), vajra made of ayas (X. 48. 3, X. 113. 5). Atharvaveda [[Atharvaveda IV, 6.6.|Atharvaveda IV. 6. 6.]] refers to poisoned arrows. In the Atharvaveda[^279] I. 16. [[%|2]] and 4 reference is made to lead as destroying sorcerers and it is said [[“if|'if]] you kill our cow or horse or man we shall pierce you with lead so that you will cease to kill our strong men'. In Tai. S. I. 5. 7. 6 it is said[^280] that when a samidh is offered into fire with the mantra 'indhānās-tvā [[satam|śataṁ]] himāḥ' the sacrificer discharges against his enemy the [[sataghni|śataghnī]] ( weapon killing a hundred) which acts like vajra itself'. Dr. Oppert in his Introduction to the [[Nitiprakāsika|Nītiprakāśikā]] pp. 10–13 relies on these and other passages for holding that the ancient Indians knew fire-arms and that Atharvaveda I. 16. 4 refers to leaden balls discharged from cylinders. Vide Dr. Oppert's work on the 'weapons, army organisation and political maxims of the ancient [[Hindus|Hindus']]' (1880), where he describes several weapons and holds that gunpowder was known in India long before the 13th century A. D. Mr. G. T. Date's 'Art of war in Ancient India' (London 1929), Dr. P. C. Chakravarti's work (1941, Dacca) and Prof. Dikshitar's book on the same subject may be consulted for details. The numerous weapons mentioned in the [[Mahabharata|Mahābhārata]] (e. g. Udyoga 155. 3–9) also are passed over here. Vide [[Hop. king'|Hopkins']] paper in J. A. O. S. Vol. XIII pp. 269–303 for detailed descriptions. The Allahabad Stone Pillar Inscription of [[Samu dragupta|Samudragupta]] (middle of 4th century A. D.) contains a long list of [[we apons|weapons]] (C I I. III pp. 6–7).[^280a] One important question is whether gunpowder and fire-arms were known to our ancient and medieval works. [[Sukra|Śukra]] refers to gunpowder called [[agnicūrna|agnicūrṇa]] in [[II. 93,196|II. 93, 196]], IV. 7. 203, guns (IV. 7. 209–211 ) and gives the formula of gunpowder in IV. 7. 201 (viz. the mixing of five palas of [[salt petre|saltpetre]], one pala of sulphur and one pala of coal powder). The [[Sukranitisāra|Śukranītisāra]] is comparatively a late work and was probably [[P214]] written in the [[L3th|13th]] or 14th century when cannon came to be used in Europe for the first time. Both in the Rāmāyaṇa and the Mahābhārata the word [[sataghni|śataghnī]] ( killing a hundred people ) occurs frequently. In the [[Yuddhakānda|Yuddhakāṇḍa]][^281] 3. 13 it is stated that at the gates of [[Lankā|Laṅkā]] hundreds of iron [[sataghnis|śataghnīs]], looking burnished, sharp and terrible, [[bad been arranged by the demons,|had been arranged by the demons.]] In a poetic description of [[Lanka|Laṅkā]] in the [[Sundarakānda|Sundarakāṇḍa]] chap. 2, it is said that [[sataghnis|śataghnīs]] and [[sūlas|śūlas]] were like the hair on the head of [[Lankā|Laṅkā]] (verse 21). In the Vanaparva 15 there is a fine description of [[Dvāravatl|Dvāravatī]] ([[Dvārka|Dvārkā]]) besieged by Śālva, in which it is said that the capital had numerous towers and turrets, machines, tomaras, [[askusas|aṅkuśas]], [[sataghnis|śataghnīs]] &c. In [[Adi.|Ādi.]] 207. 34, Vanaparva 169. 16, 284. 5, [[290, 24|290. 24]], [[Drona 156, 70|Droṇa 156. 70]], [[Karna|Karṇa]] 11. 8, [[Salya|Śalya]] 45. 110 the [[sataghni|śataghnī]] is [[Inentioned|mentioned]], but it is impossible to find from these what exactly it was like. From Vanaparva 284. 31 it appears that the [[sataghnis|śataghnīs]] were discharged with force by the hands and had wheels and round balls or stones in it. In [[Drona|Droṇa]] 179. 46 it is said that the [[sataghni|śataghnī]] employed by [[Ghatotkaca|Ghaṭotkaca]] had wheels and killed four horses at once. In Droṇa 199. 19 [[sataghnis|śataghnīs]] are said to have two or four wheels. In Vanaparva[^282] 284. 4 it is said that the powder of sarjarasa ( resin from Sal tree ) had been collected. In the [[Harivamsa|Harivaṁśa]] ( Bhaviṣyaparva 44. 20 ), among the weapons hurled at [[Narasimha|Narasiṁha]] by Hiraṇyakaśipu were blazing [[sataghnis|śataghnīs]] ([[sataghnibhiśca|śataghnībhiśca]] [[diptabhir-daṇdairapi|dīptābhir-daṇḍairapi]] [[sudāruṇaih|sudāruṇaiḥ]]). In Rāmāyaṇa VII. 32. 44 we are told that at the tip of the weapon called musala ( club) blazed forth fire like a bunch of [[Asoka|Aśoka]] flowers. The [[Sundarakānda|Sundarakāṇḍa]] couples together [[sataghni|śataghnī]] and musala ( [[4, 18|4. 18]]). It looks probable that resinous powder was used in some cylindrical contrivance (or rockets) mounted on wheels and capable of throwing stones. But gunpowder appears not to have been used. There is no description of smoke due to [[sataghnis|śataghnīs]]. Hopkins (J. A. O. S. XIII pp. 299–303 ) holds that gunpowder and fire-arms were unknown to the great Epic and in the present state of our knowledge this view seems to be correct.

The [[Nitiprakāśikā|Nītiprakāśikā]] (chapters [[%-5|2–5]]) names and describes numerous weapons divided into four [[classos|classes]] viz. mukta ( thrown [[P215]] or discharged such as arrows), amukta (not thrown such as swords ), [[muktamukta|muktāmukta]] (thrown and not thrown, such as astras which after discharge can be taken back) and mantramukta (astras which cannot be taken back ). [[The Agnipurana ( 249 252 )|The Agnipurāṇa ( 249–252 )]] and the Viṣṇudharmottara ( II 178–182) give a summary of the Dhanurveda (both agreeing almost word for word, but the latter containing more verses than the former ) and speak of five kinds of weapons viz. yantramukta ( discharged from a machine, a sling, bow &c. ), pāṇimukta ( thrown with the hand such as a stone or tomara ), [[muktā mukta|muktāmukta]] (like a [[prāga|prāsa]] ), amukta (sword) and niyuddha or [[bahuyuddha|bāhuyuddha]] ( wrestling). The science of astras was of a supernatural kind. In the epics and purāṇas, the great heroes are said to have learnt astravidyā either from a teacher or from their father or by practising austerities and sometimes (as in the case of Kuśa and Lava) certain astras pass to the son by the mere fact of his birth and the wish of his father. Whether the Dhanurveda often referred to in the epics had [[consign ed|consigned]] this science of astras to writing and could have enabled a reader to possess the miraculous powers attributed to astras is more than a modern can say. The Agnipurāṇa (chap. 134–135) contains magical incantations for victory in war and conquest of the worlds. The Paraśurāmapratāpa ([[Rājavallabhakānda|Rājavallabhakāṇḍa]] folios 9–12 ) contains numerous mantras [[apd|and]] yantras and [[incanta tions|incantations]] derived from Tantra works like the [[Brahmayamala|Brahmayāmala]].

The [[Mahabharata|Mahābhārata]] is careful to point out that an army [[consti tutes|constitutes]] the most inferior kind of bala (power). [[Udyogaparva (37. 59-55 )|Udyogaparva (37. 52–55 )]] states that bala is of five kinds viz. brute force ([[bahubala|bāhubala]] ), that due to the acquisition of ministers ( [[amatya. lābha|amātya-lābha]] ), that derived from wealth ( dhanalābha ), that from noble descent ([[abhijātubala|abhijātabala]]) and the power of wisdom (prajñābala), which last is the best of all. These are quoted by the [[Budhabhūgana|Budhabhūṣaṇa]] p. 79. In [[Santi.|Śānti.]] 134. 8 it is said[^283] that there is nothing that the strong cannot accomplish and that whatever the strong do is pure. In another place it is said ' everything is wholesome to the powerful'( [[Aśramavāsi|Āśramavāsi]] 30. 24). In [[Adi|Ādi]] 175. 45 a warrior's power is scorned and [[the power of spiritual merit|the power of the spiritual merit]] of brāhmaṇas is extolled as the real power.
