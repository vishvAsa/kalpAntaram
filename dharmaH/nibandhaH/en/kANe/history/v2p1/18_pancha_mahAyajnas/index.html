CHAPTER XVIII

PAÑCA MAHĀYAJÑAS

Pañca Mahāyajñas:—(the five daily great observances or sacrifices),

From early Vedic times five daily observances called mahāyajñas were prescribed. The Sat. Br, (XI.5. 6. I.)1 says ‘there are only five mahad[[yajfias|yajñas]], they are like great sacrificial sessions, viz, the sacrifice to beings, the sacrifice to men, the sacrifice to the Fathers, the sacrifice to the gods, the sacrifice to brahman (Veda)’. These are then briefly defined. In the Tai, Ar, II. 10 we read ‘these five [[mahayajfias|mahāyajñas]] indeed are spread out continuously, viz, devayajila, pitr[[yajfia|yajña]], bhiitayajña, manusyayajña, brahma[[yajfia|yajña]], That is fulfilled as devayajña when one makes an offering in fire, even if it be a mere fuel-stick; when one offers svadh& (&raddha repast) to the fathers, even if it be mere water, that becomes pitryajña; when a man offers a bali (a ball of food) to the beings it becomes bhiitayajña, when he gives food to [[brahmana|brāhmaṇa]]s that becomes manusyayajia, When one studies svddhydya even if it be a single or yajus formula or a saman, it becomes brahmayajña’.2

The [[Asv.|Aśv.]] gr. (III 1, 1-4) speaks of the five mahāyajñas and defines them in practically the same words as the Tai. Ar. [[IL|II.]] 10 and enjoins that those [[yajfias|yajñas]] must be performed every day. Nārāyaṇa on Aśv. gr. [[IIL|III.]] 1, 2 expressly asserts that the [[P697]] basis of the five [[yajfias|yajñas]] is the Tai. Ar.3 The [[Ap. Dh.|Āp. Dh.]] 8S, (1. 4.12,13-15 and I 4. 13.1) names and explains them simi. larly and states that they are called (in the Tai. Ar, II, 10) * great [[yajfias|yajñas]]’ and ‘ great sacrificial sessions’ by way of laudation, The word ‘ yajña’ applied to these five daily duties is figurative and the adjective * great’ is applied only for belauding4 them. Gaut. V.8 and VIII 17, Baud. Dh, S. [[IL|II.]] 6. 1-8, [[Gobbila|Gobhila]][[smrti|smṛti]] IT, 26 and numerous [[smrti|smṛti]] texts speak of the same five yajñas. QGaut. VIII. 17 includes them among sariskdras as stated above (p. 193).

It will be noticed from the description of the five [[yajfias|yajñas]] given below that they are distinguished from the solemn $rauta sacrifices in two respects. In these five the chief agent is the householder himself, he does not need the help and ministration of a professional priest, while in the [[Srauta|Śrauta]] sacrifices the priests occupy the most prominent place and the householder is more or less a passive spectator or agent in the hands of the priests who direct everything. In the second place, in the five yajñas the central point is the discharge of duties to the Creator, to the ancient sages, to the Manes, and to the whole universe with myriads of creatures of various grades of intelligence. In the grauta sacrifices the main-spring of action is the desire to secure Heaven or some object such as prosperity, a son &c. Therefore the institution of the five sacrifices is morally and spiritually more progressive and more ennobling than that of the &rauta sacrifices.

Similarly everyone could show his reverence for and gratitude to the great sages that had bequeathed a glorious heritage of sacred literature by repeating at least one verse and one could propitiate his deceased ancestors by offering in loving memory and filial devotion a mere handful or vessel-ful of water (which costs nothing). The whole world human and non-human is one creation and there must be a spirit of live and let live or give and take. Therefore one must offer what one can afford to a guest and also have something for all beings (including even such shunned animals as dogs, crows and insects). These feelings of devotion, gratitude, reverence, loving memory, kindliness and tolerance seem to have been the springs that prompted the Āryans of old to emphasize the importance of the five daily [[yajfias|yajñas]] and to have led sūtra writers like Gautama and legislators like Manu ([[IL|II.]] 28) to look upon them as sariskaras, as ennobling the soul by freeing it from mere selfishness and elevating the body to become a fit vehicle for higher things.6 Later on it appears that other purposes came to be attributed to the institution of the five daily yajñas. According to Manu III 68-71, [[Visnu Dh,|Viṣṇu Dh.]] 8, 59, 19-20, [[Sankha|Śaṅkha]] V. 1-2, [[Harlta|Hārīta]], [[Matsyapurina|Matsyapurāṇa]] 52. 15-16 and others every householder causes injury or death to sentient beings every day in five places, viz, the hearth, the grinding mill, broomstick, winnowing basket and similar household gear, mortar and pestle, and water-jar. The five daily yajñas were devised by the great sages as atonements for the sins arising from these five sources of injury to life. These five are: brahma[[yajfia|yajña]] which consists in the study and teaching of the Veda, pitr[[yajfia|yajña]] which consists of tarpaṇa, daivayajña which consists in offerings made into fire, bhutayajña which is offering oblations to beings and manusya[[yajfia|yajña]] which consists in honouring guests. He who performs these daily according to his means is never tainted by the sin of the injuries arising from the five places mentioned above, Manu ([[IIL|III.]] 73-74) further7 [[gays|says]] that former sages had a different nomenclature for the five [[P699]] yajñas i. e. ahuta, huta, prahuta, brahmya-huta and praéita, which were the same respectively as japa (or brahmayajña), homa (devayaijiia), bhiitayajña, manugyayajña and pitrtarpaṇa (pitr[[yajfia|yajña]]). In the Atharvaveda VI. 71. 2 four out of these seem to be alluded to ‘ what came to me as huta or ahuta or given by pitrs and assented to by men’.8 Huta and prahuta in the sense of koma to gods and bali to bhitas ocour in Br. Up. I. 5.2. But in some grhyasūtras different meanings are attached to these very words e. g. San. gr. I. 5 and Par. gr. I. 4 say that there are four [[pakayajñas|pākayajñas]] viz. huta, ahuta, prahuta and prasita and San. gr. I. 10. 79 explains that they are respectively the same as agnihotra (or daivayajña), bali (bhiatayajña), pitryajña and brahmya-huta (or manusyayajia). The [[Harlta|Hārīta]]dharma-sūtra has a very interesting passage on this point. ‘ We shall now explain the sunds (places of injury) which are so called because they kill moving and immovable sentient beings. They are five. The first (siind) is caused by actions like sudden entrance in water, plunging into water, whirling water, splashing it in various directions, taking water without straining it through a piece of cloth and driving vehicles; the gecond by walking about in the dark or away from the beaten path or in quick jerks or by treading upon insects &e.; the third by striking (a tree with an axe &o.), by plucking flowers &ec. by tying with a rope &c., by crushing (in a mortar), by splitting (wood &ec.); the fourth by cutting crops, by rubbing or grinding; and the fifth by ignition (of fire-wood), heating (of water), by roasting, frying and cooking. These five injuries that lead to Hell are committed every day by people. Brahmacarins get rid of the first three by attending on fire and on their teacher and by the study of the Veda; householders and forest hermits purify themselves from these five by performing the five yajñas; ascetics get rid of the first two injuries by sacred knowledge and contemplation, but the injury caused by crushing uncooked seeds under the teeth cannot be removed by any of these.10 Although in the [[Ap. Dh.|Āp. Dh.]] S. snd others the five yajñas are enumerated in the order of bhiita-yajña, manugya-yaijiisa, [[P700]] devayaijfia, pitryajña and svadhyaya, still the proper order from the point of view of the times of performance is first brahmayajña (japa &eo.), then devayajña, then bhita[[yajfia|yajña]], then pitr[[yajfia|yajña]], and lastly [[manusyayaijii|manusyayajña]].11 Therefore they will be dealt with in the same order here. But some matters have first to be noted. Various views were entertained about the time and nature of brahma[[yajfia|yajña]]12 and pitr[[yajfia|yajña]]. According to Gobhila-[[smrti|smṛti]] IT. 28-29 quoted above (n. 1663), japa prescribed in saṃdhyā adoration may be looked upon as brahmayajia, that the latter may be performed before tarpaṇa and after the morning homa or after vaiśvadeva. [[Narayana|Nārāyaṇa]] on Adv. gr. III. 2. 1 says that brahma[[yajfia|yajña]] may be performed before or after vaiśvadeva. According to the [[Snana|Snāna]]sitra of Kātyāyana, brahmayajña precedes tarpaṇa and AS8v, gr. as stated above (p. 695) appears to regard tarpaṇa as part of it. Manu III 82 ([[Vispu|Viṣṇu]] Dh. S. 67. 23-25) enjoins upon a man the performance of daily śrāddha with food or water or with milk, roots and fruits and thus to propitiate and please the Manes of his deceased ancestors; while Manu (III. 70 and 283) says that tarpaṇa (done after bath) constitutes pitr[[yajfia|yajña]]. Therefore Gobhila says that śrāddha, tarpaṇa and the bali offered to pitrs constitute pitryajia and even when one of them is gone through, the performance of pitr[[yajfia|yajña]] is effected and it is not necessary to perform all three.13 In the bali-harana (described below) the remnants of bali are offered to pitrs (Adv. gr. I. 2. 11, Manu III. 91).

Brahma-[[yajfia|yajña]]. Probably the earliest description of this is to be found in the Sat. Br, XI. 5. 6. 3-8, That [[Brahmana|Brāhmaṇa]], after stating that brahma[[yajfia|yajña]] is one’s own daily study of the Veda, compares several elements required in the ordinary sacrifice to certain elements of brahma[[yajfia|yajña]], viz. the juhi spoon, upabhrt, dhruva, sruva, avabhrtha (the solemn bath at the end of a sacrifice) and heaven are said to be represented by the speech, the mind, the eye, mental power, truth and the conclusion (that [[P701]] are present in [[brahmayaiiia|brahmayajña]]).14 Then it says that whoever studies day. by day his Vedic lessons gains an imperishable world which is thrice or more of the world that one may secure by making a gift to priests of the whole earth replete with wealth, Then 4-7 compare rk, yajus, siman and Atharvingiras (Atharvaveda) texts respectively to offerings to gods of milk, ghee, soma and fat and it is stated that the gods being delighted and satisfied with these, bestow on the man who performs brahma[[yajfia|yajña]] affluence and security, life.-breath, seed, his whole self and all auspicious blessings and streams of ghee and honey flow for his departed pitrs. The Sat. Br, XI. 5, 6. 8 enumerates other works that may be recited in brahmayasjiia, the recital of which is like honey offerings to gods who being delighted and gratified bestow on the reciter the boons stated above, the works being Anu$asanas (Vedingas), vidyas (such as sarpa and devajana vidy& mentioned in Chandogya VII. 1. 1), vakovakya (theological discourses called brahmodya, as in Vij. S. 23. 9-12 and 45-62), Itihdsa[[purana|purāṇa]] (traditional history and legends), Gathas, Naridarmsis (i. eo. stanzas in praise of heroes).15 The Tai. Ar. ([[IL|II.]] 10-13) has a more lengthy passage on brahmayajña; Tai. Ar, II. 10 is almost16 the same as Sat. Br. except in two respects, viz. in the Tai. Ar. Atharvingirasah are said to be honey offerings and [[brahmana|brāhmaṇa]] texts, itihdsas, [[puranas|purāṇas]], kalpas (works on $rauta ritual) and Gatha Narasamsis are said to be fat offerings and the rewards, bestowed by the gods when delighted by the brahmayajña, are long life, brilliance, lustre, prosperity, glory, spiritual eminence and food. Tai. Ar, II. 11 describes how and where brahmayajña is to be performed ‘ one who desires to offer brahmayajña should repair to a place so far away to the east, north or north-east of his village that the thatch covering houses is not visible and when the sun rises he should wear his sacred thread (in the upavita form) under his right arm, should sit down (on a pure spot), should wash both his hands with water, should sip water thrice, should wipe his hand twice with water, should [[P702]] once sprinkle his lips with water, and touch his head, eyes, nostrils, ears and heart; he should spread out a large seat of darbhas, should be seated facing the east with his legs crossed over each other (the left foot being underneath and the right foot on the left thigh) and then should repeat his Veda; (it is said that) the darbhas are indeed the flavour (or sweetness) of waters and herbs; he (by sitting on darbhas) makes his Veda full of sweetness. Placing his left hand on the right knee with the palm turned up and covering it with the right hand the palm of which is turned down and placing pavitras (blades of darbha)between both hands, he should begin with the syllable ‘om’ which is a yajus, which is the representative of the three Vedas, which is all speech, and is the highest syllable; this has been declared by ark (Rg. I. 164. 39 is quoted). He recites the syllables bhih, bhuvah, svah; he thereby (by repeating the vyahrtis) employs the three Vedas. This is the truth (quintessence) of speech; he thereby has employed the truth of speech. Then he recites thrice the GayatrI verse, which is addressed to Savitr, by its feet separately, then by half of }it, then the whole verse without stopping. The sun is the creator of glory, he secures glory itself; then he begins (the next day) the repetition of the Vedic texts from that point which he had noted (the previous day).17 Tai. Ar. II. 12 states that if a man is unable to go out of the village he may perform brahmyajña by revolving in his mind in the village itself the Veda by day or even by night; or if he cannot seat himself, then he may perform the brahmayajña even standing or lying down, since the principal matter is the recitation of the Veda (time and place being quite subordinate). Tai. Ar. II. 13 says that he should conclude the brahmayaijfia by repeating thrice the verse ‘Adoration to Brahma (Veda or Prajipati), to Agni, to the Earth, to herbs, to speech, to the Lord of speech (Brhaspati), I offer adoration to [[P703]] the great [[Visnu|Viṣṇu]]’.18 Then he should sip water and. return home; thereafter whatever he gives becomes the fee of the sacrifice (i, e. of brahmayajña).

The procedure of svidby&iya (or brahma[[yajfia|yajña]]) in [[Asv.|Aśv.]] gr. III. 2. 2-111. 3. 4 is practically in the same words as in the Tai. Ar, quoted above. One or two points may be noted. The [[A&v, gr.|Aśv. gr.]]19 prescribes that one should recite gazing at the horizon or one may close one’s eyes or one may look in such a way that one feels that one can concentrate one’s mind. According to the ASv, gr. the shortest brahmayajña would be: om bhir bhuvah svah; the [[Gayatrl|Gāyatrī]] verse repeated thrice; then at least one rk verse and then the verse ‘ namo brahmane’ (quoted above) repeated thrice, The [[Ahnikaprakasa|Āhnikaprakāśa]] p. 329 says that one who knows only a portion of the Veda should recite as brahma[[yajfia|yajña]] the Purusasiikta (Rg. X. 90) and other hymns and one who knows only the [[Gayatrl|Gāyatrī]] should repeat ‘om’ as brahma[[yajfia|yajña]] every day.20 [[A&v, gr.|Aśv. gr.]] ([[IIL|III.]] 3.1) mentions the following works for stadhyaya, Rgveda, Yajurveda, Sameveda, Atharvingirasah, [[Brahmana|Brāhmaṇa]]s, Kalpas, Gath&s Narasamsls, Itihasas, and Puranas. But it adds that one may recite only as much as one feels that one can afford to do with a concentrated mind.

The S&n. gr. I. 4 prescribes several hymns and verses of the Rgveda for reciting in brahma[[yajfia|yajña]]. Others following different Vedas and Sakhas differ as to the content of the brahmayajña. Vide [[Ahnika-prakasa|Āhnika-prakāśa]] pp. 328-336 for brahmayajña according to Kiatyayana and according to the followers of Samaveda. Ya&j. I. 101 prescribes that as time and ability allow one may include in brahmayajña the Vedas together with the Atharvaveda, Itihasa and philosophical texts.

Brahmayajña is very rarely performed every day (except by the most orthodox vaidikas and édstris) in modern times and a fixed formula of brahmayajña has been decided upon, which is recited once a year in Srivana by most [[brahmana|brāhmaṇa]]s in the Deccan, The formula for students of the Rgveda is as follows: [[P704]] After reciting ‘om bhiir bhuvah svah’ and the sacred [[Gayatri|Gāyatrī]], he recites Rg. I.1.1-9, then the first sentence of the Ait. Br., the first sentences of the five sections of the Ait. Ar.; the first sentences of the Black and White Yajurveda, of the Simaveda, of the Atharvaveda; the first sentences of the Nighantu and the six Vedangas viz, Adv, érauts, Nirukta, Chandas, Nighantu, Jyotisa, Siksa, Panpini’s grammar in order; the first ‘pada’ of [[Yaj.|Yāj.]] I. 1 and of the [[Mahabharata|Mahābhārata]] (1.1.1), the first sttra of the Nyiya, PirvamIm[[arsa|ārṣa]] and UttaramIm[[arsa|ārṣa]]; then a benedictory formula (‘taccharmyor… catuspade’) and lastly the verse‘ namo brahmane’ is repeated thrice.21 After this brahmayajña, tarpansa of devas, sages and pitrs follows.

  17. The idea is that one is not to begin to recite Vedic texts at random. When a man has recited a portion of any Veda one day, he should note where he stopped and continue his recitation next day from that point. The Āp. Dh. S. I. 3. 11. 19 also prescribes that one should go every day to the water-side before taking his morning meal and recite in a pure place a portion of the Veda in succession (i. e. one day he should begin where he stopped the previous day and so on). Vide Aśv. gr. III. 4. 6 where the Tai. Ār. allowing recitation of the Veda even while standing or lying down is quoted. ↩︎
