Objections

Two objections to Dharmaśāstra circulate today, and they seem to pull in opposite directions. If we look closely — they rest on the same picture of what a smṛti is. Worth pulling that apart before either objection can be answered, or conceded. 🧵

Sterility

Complaint one: sterility. Commentaries on the Upaniṣads and the Itihāsas keep appearing — in Sanskrit, in regional languages, well into this century. But no one has produced a Dharmaśāstra for the present.

Have we lost the capacity to compose one? And if we can’t restate the substance of the old texts in a form Hindu society at large would accept, is anything left but a defensive posture?

Content

Complaint two: content. There are passages in the smṛtis, the objection runs, that no one today would defend. Their presence taints the whole corpus. Not a demand for more — a demand for less, or none at all.

Summary

One complaint wants a new book. The other wants the old book disowned. Opposite demands — but both assume the same thing: a smṛti is a book of rules, issued by an author, binding as written on everyone who inherits it, replaceable only by another such book.

On that picture, complaint one is just obvious — no new book, no living tradition. And complaint two is unanswerable — the offending verses are sitting right there in the book.

Applicability

But the Mīmāṃsakas who established smṛti’s authority did not hold that picture. Neither did the nibandha writers who spent eight centuries applying it. Their smṛti was never a fixed, author-issued code binding as written.

Before agreeing with either complaint — or dismissing them — it’s worth recovering what the tradition actually took a smṛti to be, and on what terms it agreed to be bound by one. That’s the real starting point.

prāmāṇya of smṛti

Let us understand how the prāmāṇya of smṛti is established. It is not taken for granted — Jaimini argues it out, in the 1st adhyāya, 3rd pāda of the Mīmāṃsā Sūtras. And the case against smṛti is put first, and put strongly.

The pūrvapakṣa (1.3.1):
“Dharmasya śabdamūlatvād aśabdam anapekṣaṃ syāt”
Dharma is rooted in the Vedic word. Whatever is not that word has no claim on us. Simple, because smṛtis are texts composed by named human authors.

This isn’t an outsider’s attack. The pūrvapakṣin is using Mīmāṃsā’s own premise — word-rootedness — and following it to its logical end. If dharma = what the Veda says, and smṛti ≠ the Veda, smṛti should be discarded. The objection is internally consistent.

The siddhānta doesn’t answer with a rival claim to independent authority. It answers with an inference (1.3.2):

“Api vā kartṛ-sāmānyāt pramāṇam anumānaṃ syāt”

Same agents. The very people who composed and followed smṛti also studied and performed the Veda.

The argument: men of that formation — Vedic students, Vedic performers — do not invent obligations out of nowhere. If they record a duty with no surviving Vedic text behind it, the reasonable inference isn’t “they made it up.” It’s that a source text existed.

That source may simply be lost. Or it may survive in a śākhā no longer recited in their region. Either way, smṛti’s authority is derivative — it stands in for a Vedic text we can no longer see, not for itself.

Śabara’s bhāṣya leans on this exact point: the weight falls on the character of the transmitters. Trustworthy men, trained in Vedic discipline, don’t legislate dharma independently. Their smṛti is evidence of a Veda, not a substitute for one.

Kumārila takes it further in the Tantravārttika, building a full theory: anumitā śruti — “inferred śruti.” A valid smṛti implies a lost Vedic original behind it. He also has to defend this against the obvious challenge: why can’t anyone just manufacture scripture this way?

That’s the whole foundation, stated plainly: smṛti is a pramāṇa only by inference from a lost original. It has no independent standing — and the tradition doesn’t pretend otherwise. Authority was earned through argument, not assumed.
