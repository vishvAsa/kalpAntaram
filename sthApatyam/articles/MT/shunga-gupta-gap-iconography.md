Between empires and terracottas of deities

## Production program
This period, marked by the yavana and Iranic interlude, was accompanied by some dramatic changes in the archaeology of Hindu iconography. Apart from the emergence of a major iconographic program of stone and metal images of the deities skanda and his retinue, rudra, viShNu (along with his sAttvata vyUha-s) and durgA with associated stone and brick shrines in cultic cities like mathurA, there also emerged a vigorous program of production of terracotta depictions of some of these deities (up to $\approx$ 20 cm in height) in several North Indian cities of the age. 

I am not aware of a comparably vigorous program in South Indian cities, though several coeval iconography continuities can be observed in those sites. 

In terms of basic technique, these terracottas show a certain continuity with the maurya and shu~Nga productions, even if they did match the virtuosity of the idiosyncractic shu~Nga material from chandraketugArh.

While these terracottas have been discussed for nearly 60 years, the publications of V. Kumar et al, illustrating a large sample of them, allow for some conclusions regarding the religious depictions in them. 

## Deities

While some from the earlier period still remain mysterious, like the goddess of the shu~Nga age with weapons in her headdress, the subsequent ones are rather unambiguous. In terms of prevalence, the following Hindu deities seem most common in descending order:

1. nejameSha (the ectype of kumAra with a caprine head)
2. His consort nejameShI
3. mahiShamardinI
4. rudra
5. viShNu
6. skanda as kumAra
7. His consort ShaShThI
8. vaiShravaNa

## Custom


To us, this suggests that around this period, the custom of keeping terracotta images (and in some cases small stone images) of deities in a household shrine became widespread. These were perhaps kept on for the duration of a festival, like the autumnal nine-nights festival of durgA, followed by the "release (visarjana)" of the deity. The preponderance of the nejameSha/nejameShI images combined with the textual accounts (e.g., medical saMhitA-s and the later kAdambarI) suggests that these might have been kept in the birthing room (sUtikA-gR^iha). These were probably accompanied by perinatal ritual in the shrines of skanda and his retinue at the cultic centers (e.g., mentioned in the vaikhAnasa-smArta-sUtra-s). Such terracottas were probably mass-produced and sold in the vicinity of the shrines.

In textual terms, the household worship of images emerges in the latest vaidika tradition, in the gR^ihya-parishiShTha-s of the various schools. While Occidental indologists and their fellow travelers might, hence, date the gR^ihya-parishiShTha-s to this period, we do not propose that. Instead, we merely see this phase as the period of the rise in prevalence of iconographic conventions that subsequently came to dominate the H sphere.
